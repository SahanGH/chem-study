## Rules for writing a lesson (Tasks 1–3, 5–11, 13–15)

Read `src/content/lessons/unit-2/2-1-types-of-chemical-bonds.mdx` first. It is the model for length, tone and markup.

**The file.** `src/content/lessons/unit-3/<file name in the task>`. The name must be `<unit>-<number>-<slug>.mdx` matching the topic, or the build fails. Front matter is exactly three fields:

```mdx
---
topic: "3.1"
minutes: 12
summary: One sentence, 20 to 200 characters, saying what the lesson teaches.
---
```

The title comes from `src/data/units.ts`. Do not write an `# H1`.

**The seven parts, in order.** The build fails if a lesson lacks a worked example, a trap box, key points, practice-next or three checks.

1. Opening: two or three sentences, no heading, starting from something concrete.
2. Explanation: `##` sections, one idea each. At least one `<KeyIdea>`.
3. `<Worked title="...">` with numbered steps.
4. `<Check>` questions spread through the lesson, each placed right after the idea it tests. Never all at the end.
5. `<Trap>`: the specific mistakes for this topic, as a short list with the mistake in bold.
6. `<KeyPoints>`: four to six bullets. This is the review sheet, so each bullet must stand on its own.
7. `<PracticeNext />` as the last line, with no attributes.

**Markup.**

- Math is KaTeX inside `$...$` or a `$$` block. Formulas and equations use `\ce{...}`: `$\ce{CO2}$`, `$\ce{Ca^2+}$`. Units in math are upright: `$\text{L·atm/(mol·K)}$`, `$\text{nm}$`. In prose, plain Unicode is fine for a simple formula (H₂O, SO₄²⁻) when it needs no math.
- A choice check: `<Check answer={2}>`, the question, a blank line, a `-` list of options, a blank line, then `<div slot="why">...</div>`. `answer` is the 1-based number of the correct option.
- A numeric check: `<Check numeric="0.250">` (add `unit="..."` when there is one). **The number is text in quotes**, so its trailing zeros (significant figures) survive. The student's answer is accepted within 2 percent.
- A figure: `<Figure src="/figures/<name>.svg" alt="..." caption="..." />`.
- Inside a block (`<Worked>`, `<Trap>`, `<KeyPoints>`, `<KeyIdea>`), leave a blank line after the opening tag and before the closing tag.
- Excluded material goes in a paragraph that begins "**Beyond the exam:**". There is no `<Beyond>` block in lessons.
- Refer to another lesson by topic number in plain text ("topic 3.1"). Link only to a lesson that is already published (Units 2 and 5, and Unit 3 lessons merged earlier); a broken link fails the build.
- **Phone width.** A long equation or calculation goes on its own line (`$$ ... $$`), split with `aligned` if needed; math inside a sentence cannot wrap. This holds inside a Check's `<div slot="why">` too, written in block form with blank lines. Tables have at most five columns. No lesson may scroll sideways at 320 px with every answer open.
- **Astro drops the space between text and an inline element on the next line.** Keep a link or inline tag on the same line as the word before it.

**Lewis diagrams** use the `<Lewis>` block (see the props at the top of `src/lib/lewis-parse.ts`, and lesson 2.5 for examples). Neighbors go left, right, above and below; the build checks the electron count; `alt` says what the diagram shows in words.

**Voice.** A teacher talking, not a textbook. Start from something concrete. Short sentences. No "in this section we will". Say "you". Explain why before how. One new term at a time, in bold the first time, defined in the same sentence.

**Chemistry.**

### Task 1: Lesson 3.1, Intermolecular and Interparticle Forces

**Files:**
- Create: `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-1-intermolecular-and-interparticle-forces.mdx`
- Create: `public/figures/3-1-water-hydrogen-bonds.svg`, `public/figures/3-1-ion-dipole.svg`

**Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "3.1"`, `minutes: 14`, summary: "The attractions between molecules and ions: London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds and ion–dipole forces, and how a molecule's structure sets their strength."
- **Framework (3.1.A):** London dispersion forces come from the Coulomb attraction between temporary, constantly shifting dipoles, and between large molecules they are often the largest attraction overall; "London dispersion forces" and "van der Waals forces" are not two names for one thing; a polar molecule's dipole adds further attractions to other particles; dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules, depend on how large the dipoles are and how they line up, and act on top of London forces, so polar molecules usually attract each other more than nonpolar molecules of similar size; ion–dipole forces act between an ion and a polar molecule and are usually stronger than dipole–dipole forces; the sign of the partial charges explains which end of a polar molecule turns toward an ion or a neighbor; a hydrogen bond is a strong attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to N, O or F and the negative end (an N, O or F atom) of another molecule or of another part of the same molecule; in large biomolecules, noncovalent attractions act between molecules and between different regions of one molecule. The relationships are explained both for molecules of one substance and for two different substances.
- **Electronegativities used** (lesson 2.1's values): H 2.2, C 2.6, N 3.0, O 3.4, F 4.0, Cl 3.2.
- **Opening:** three things in a garage are made of the same two elements, carbon and hydrogen, and all three are nonpolar. The propane in a camping-stove tank (C₃H₈) is a gas at room temperature. Octane (C₈H₁₈), one of the hydrocarbons in gasoline, is a liquid. A paraffin candle, made of hydrocarbons with about 20 to 40 carbons, is a solid. Same atoms, same kind of bonds; what changes is the size of the molecules, and with it how strongly they hold on to each other. This lesson is about those attractions.
- **Section "Forces between molecules, not within them":** an **intermolecular force** is an attraction between separate molecules. It is not a bond: the covalent bonds of Unit 2 hold atoms together inside a molecule. When water boils, its molecules move apart, but every O–H bond stays whole; the steam is still H₂O. Give the scale: boiling a mole of water at 100 °C takes about 41 kJ, while breaking the O–H bonds in a mole of water would take more than 900 kJ, over twenty times as much. "Interparticle" widens the word to every kind of particle: atoms, molecules and ions. All of these forces are Coulomb attractions between opposite charges, full or partial. `<KeyIdea>`: intermolecular forces hold molecules near each other; covalent bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule. Melting and boiling a molecular substance overcome the first and leave the second alone.
- **Section "London dispersion forces: in every substance":** the electrons in a molecule are always moving. At any instant they may be bunched a little more on one side, making a **temporary dipole**. That dipole pushes on the electrons of a neighbor and gives it a dipole too, lined up so the two attract. A moment later both have shifted, and the attraction appears somewhere else. The flickering attractions between these temporary dipoles are **London dispersion forces**. They act between all molecules and atoms, polar or not, so the question is never whether a substance has them, only how strong they are. Two things make them stronger. (1) **More electrons.** A large electron cloud, held loosely far from the nuclei, is easily distorted; that ease is called **polarizability**. The noble gases show it, in one or two sentences, not a table: helium, with 2 electrons, boils at −269 °C; argon, with 18, at −186 °C; xenon, with 54, at −108 °C. Each step up in electrons raises the boiling point. (2) **More contact.** Long, straight molecules touch each other along their whole length; compact, branched ones touch at fewer points. Octane and 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (the "isooctane" of octane ratings) are both C₈H₁₈, with 66 electrons each, yet straight-chain octane boils at about 126 °C and branched, compact isooctane at about 99 °C. Say once, plainly: some books use "van der Waals forces" as a collective name for several attractions between neutral molecules; London dispersion forces are only one member of that group, so do not use the two names as if they meant the same thing, and on the exam name the specific force. `<KeyIdea>`: every substance has London dispersion forces. They grow with the number of electrons and with the area over which molecules touch, and between large molecules they are often the strongest attraction of all.
- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={1}>`: "Hexane and 2,2-dimethylbutane are both C₆H₁₄. Hexane is a straight chain of six carbons; 2,2-dimethylbutane is compact and branched. Which has the higher boiling point, and why?" Options: hexane, because its long molecules touch over a larger area, so the London dispersion forces between them are stronger; 2,2-dimethylbutane, because compact molecules have more electrons; hexane, because its molecules are polar and the branched ones are not; neither, because molecules with the same formula always boil at the same temperature. Why: both have the same atoms, so the same 50 electrons. The difference is shape: straight chains lie alongside each other and attract over more of their surface. Hexane boils at about 69 °C, 2,2-dimethylbutane at about 50 °C. Neither is polar: both have only C–C and C–H bonds.
- **Section "Dipole–dipole forces":** a polar molecule (topic 2.7) has a permanent δ+ end and δ− end. Neighboring polar molecules turn so that the δ+ end of one sits near the δ− end of the next: a **dipole–dipole force**. Its strength depends on how large the dipoles are and on how the molecules line up: head to tail they attract; two δ− ends side by side would repel, so in a liquid the molecules spend more of their time in the attracting arrangements. Dipole–dipole forces act on top of London forces, never instead of them, so a polar molecule usually attracts its neighbors more strongly than a nonpolar molecule of about the same size. Close the promise topic 2.7 made: cis- and trans-1,2-dichloroethene have the same atoms and the same 48 electrons, so about the same London forces. Cis is polar and trans is not, and cis boils at about 60 °C, trans at about 48 °C. The extra 12 degrees are the dipole–dipole forces.
- **Check 2**, `<Check answer={4}>`: "Which substance's molecules attract one another through dipole–dipole forces?" Options: SiCl₄; CS₂; PF₅; NF₃. Why: NF₃ is trigonal pyramidal, with a lone pair on nitrogen. Its three polar N–F bonds point down and away from the lone pair, so their dipoles do not cancel and the molecule is polar. SiCl₄ (tetrahedral), CS₂ (linear) and PF₅ (trigonal bipyramidal) are symmetrical: in each, the bond dipoles cancel, and only London forces act between their molecules.
- **Section "Hydrogen bonds":** when hydrogen is covalently bonded to N, O or F, the bond is very polar (O–H differs by 1.2, F–H by 1.8, N–H by 0.8) and the small hydrogen atom is left with a strong δ+. The size of the difference is not the whole story. Say it outright: H–Cl differs by 1.0, more than N–H's 0.8, yet HCl does not hydrogen-bond. Chlorine is a larger atom, its lone pairs are spread out over more space, and a hydrogen cannot get as close to them. What makes a hydrogen bond is a strongly δ+ hydrogen, a tiny atom with no inner electrons shielding its nucleus, that can get very close to a lone pair on a neighboring N, O or F. It is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of a neighboring molecule, or of another part of the same molecule. That attraction is a **hydrogen bond**. Between neutral molecules of similar size it is the strongest attraction, but it is still an attraction between molecules, not a covalent bond inside one; it is drawn as a dotted line. Two conditions, both needed: the hydrogen must be bonded directly to N, O or F, and the molecule it is attracted to must offer a lone pair on N, O or F. In CH₃F the hydrogens sit on carbon, so CH₃F cannot hydrogen-bond with itself, although it contains both H and F. Water is the great example: each molecule can take part in up to four hydrogen bonds, two through its hydrogens and two through the lone pairs on its oxygen. Place the figure here. Then two different substances: acetone, (CH₃)₂CO, has all its hydrogens on carbon, so its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond with each other. Mixed with water, though, a hydrogen of water is attracted to the lone pairs on acetone's oxygen: a hydrogen bond between two different molecules. That is part of why acetone and water mix in any proportion.

- **Section "Four quantities, one equation":** a sample of gas is described by four quantities: **pressure** P, the push of the gas on each bit of its container's wall (in atm, or in mm Hg or torr: 1 atm = 760 mm Hg = 760 torr); **volume** V (in L); **temperature** T (in K); and **amount** n (in mol). Its units decide everyone else's: with this R, P goes in atm, V in L, n in mol and T in kelvin. - **Section "Why a filter fails on a solution":** a filter holds back particles too big for its pores. - **Section "Chromatography":** chromatography has two parts. The **stationary phase** stays put: a sheet of paper, a thin layer of silica on a plate, or a column packed with a powder. The **mobile phase** is a solvent that moves through it. Put a small spot of the mixture on a pencil line near the bottom of a plate and stand the plate in a little solvent, below the line. Run with a mostly nonpolar solvent, the more polar a component is, the more it clings to the surface and the shorter the distance it travels. A mostly nonpolar component travels with the solvent nearly to the top. The plate after the run, with its spots, is a **chromatogram**. Distances are compared with the **R_f value** (write it $R_f$): the distance a spot moved from the start line divided by the distance the solvent moved from the start line, displayed on its own line as $R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent}}$. $R_f$ is always between 0 and 1. - **Section "Dissolving is a trade of attractions":** for a solute to dissolve, three things happen. Solute particles are pulled away from one another (their attractions are broken). Solvent molecules are pushed apart to make room (their attractions are broken too). Solute and solvent particles then attract each other (new attractions form). Dissolving goes well when the new solute–solvent attractions are about as strong as the ones broken. It goes badly when they are much weaker. Oil and water: water molecules hold each other by hydrogen bonds; an oil molecule is made mostly of long hydrocarbon chains, which can offer water only London dispersion forces. The short rule is **"like dissolves like"**: substances with similar intermolecular forces mix. The rule is shorthand; the reason is the trade of attractions. - **Framework (3.11.A):** a substance absorbs or emits light in different regions of the spectrum because each region matches a different kind of change in its molecules: microwaves go with changes between rotational levels, infrared with changes between vibrational levels, and ultraviolet and visible light with changes between electronic levels. The lesson also needs the order of the regions by wavelength and by energy per photon (the equations that link them are topic 3.12: here the relation is stated in words only). Do not teach which molecules can absorb in each region (selection rules): the framework does not ask for it.
- **Opening:** an electric heater with glowing coils can warm your face for an hour and never burn it. The midday summer sun can burn the same skin in well under an hour. The heater's glow is mostly infrared; the burn comes from the sun's ultraviolet. Both are light, but they do different things to the molecules in your skin, and the difference is how much energy each packet of light carries.
- **Section "A spectrum of light":** visible light is one small band of **electromagnetic radiation**, which travels as a wave; its **wavelength** is the distance from one crest to the next. Introduce the **photon** in one sentence: light is delivered in packets of energy called photons (topic 3.12 says how much energy each carries). The key fact, in words: the shorter the wavelength, the more energy each photon carries. Give the four regions the exam uses, in a table (two columns: Region, Typical wavelengths), from longest wavelength to shortest:

  | Region | Typical wavelengths |
  |---|---|
  | Microwave | about 1 mm to 1 m |
  | Infrared | about 700 nm to 1 mm |
  | Visible | about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red) |
  | Ultraviolet | about 10 nm to 400 nm |

  Say the borders are conventions, not sharp lines, and that radio waves lie beyond the microwaves and X-rays beyond the ultraviolet. Remind: 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m, 1 µm = 10⁻⁶ m, 1 mm = 10⁻³ m. - **Section "Energy comes in steps":** a molecule can take in energy in three ways that matter here: it can **rotate** (spin as a whole), it can **vibrate** (its bonds stretch and bend), and its electrons can move to higher energy levels. Each kind of energy comes only in certain amounts, like the rungs of a ladder: a molecule can be on a rung, never between two. It absorbs a photon only when the photon's energy matches the gap between the rung it is on and a higher one; the photon disappears and its energy goes into the molecule. Going down a rung, it can give out a photon of the same energy: that is emission. The rungs are spaced very differently: rotational levels are closest together, vibrational levels farther apart, electronic levels farthest apart. A small step needs a low-energy, long-wavelength photon; a big step needs a high-energy, short-wavelength photon.

- **Section "Three regions, three motions":** go through the three matches, one short paragraph each.

### Task 14: Lesson 3.12, Properties of Photons

**Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "3.12"`, `minutes: 12`, summary: "How a photon's wavelength, frequency and energy are linked by c = λν and E = hν, and why absorbing or emitting a photon changes an atom's energy by exactly the photon's energy." (No colon in the summary.)
- **Framework (3.12.A):** when an atom or molecule absorbs a photon its energy goes up by exactly the photon's energy, and when it emits one its energy goes down by exactly that much; a wave's wavelength and frequency are linked through the speed of light, c = λν; a photon's energy is proportional to the frequency, E = hν (Planck's equation). Both equations and both constants are on the equation sheet; students must be able to chain them, with the wavelength in meters. Constants, exactly as the sheet gives them: h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s (never 3.00 × 10⁸).
- **Opening:** under a black light, a white T-shirt glows blue. The lamp gives out ultraviolet light, which you cannot see; the shirt gives back blue light, which you can. The fabric took in one kind of light and sent out another, and each photon it sent out carries less energy than each photon it took in. By the end of the lesson you can say how much less.
- **Section "Wavelength and frequency":** topic 3.11 introduced the **photon**, a packet of light energy; recall it in one sentence. Light also behaves as a wave. Its **wavelength**, λ (the Greek letter lambda), is the distance from crest to crest, in meters. Its **frequency**, ν (the Greek letter nu, not the letter v), is how many waves pass a point each second, in s⁻¹, also written Hz (hertz; 1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹, as the equation sheet says). Every kind of electromagnetic radiation travels through a vacuum at the same speed, c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s (in air it is almost exactly the same). The three are linked by

  $c = \lambda\nu$

  Because c is fixed, wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: halve the wavelength and the frequency doubles.
- **Check 1**, `<Check numeric="0.122" unit="m">`: "A microwave oven works at a frequency of 2.45 GHz. What is the wavelength of its microwaves, in meters?" Why: giga means 10⁹ (from the equation sheet), so ν = 2.45 × 10⁹ s⁻¹. Then, as a block equation, λ = c/ν = (2.998 × 10⁸ m/s)/(2.45 × 10⁹ s⁻¹) = 0.122 m, about 12 cm: inside the microwave region of topic 3.11 (about 1 mm to 1 m).
- **Section "The energy of one photon":** Planck's equation gives the energy of a single photon:

  $E = h\nu$

  with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, **Planck's constant**. One photon carries a tiny amount of energy, so photon energies come out a few times 10⁻¹⁹ J for visible light. Most problems give a wavelength, not a frequency, so chain the two equations: find ν = c/λ, then E = hν; or put them together in one step as E = hc/λ. Either way, the wavelength must be in meters (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m). The chain says it all: shorter wavelength, higher frequency, more energy. `<KeyIdea>`: a photon's energy is set by its frequency, E = hν. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and more energy. Put the wavelength in meters before you use c = λν.
- **Worked example "The black light and the T-shirt":** a black light gives out ultraviolet light of wavelength 365 nm. A dye in the fabric, called a brightener, absorbs it and gives out blue light of wavelength 440 nm. Find the energy of each photon, and how much of the absorbed energy each brightener molecule does not give back as light. Write each calculation as a block equation.
  1. **Convert the wavelength to meters.** 365 nm × (10⁻⁹ m / 1 nm) = 3.65 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. **Find the frequency.** ν = c/λ = (2.998 × 10⁸ m/s)/(3.65 × 10⁻⁷ m) = 8.21 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹.
  3. **Find the energy.** E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(8.21 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹) = 5.44 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. **Do the blue photon in one step.** 440 nm = 4.40 × 10⁻⁷ m, and E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(2.998 × 10⁸ m/s)/(4.40 × 10⁻⁷ m) = 4.51 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  5. **Compare.** 5.44 × 10⁻¹⁹ J − 4.51 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 0.93 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 9.3 × 10⁻²⁰ J. Each molecule takes in 5.44 × 10⁻¹⁹ J and gives back 4.51 × 10⁻¹⁹ J as blue light. The other 9.3 × 10⁻²⁰ J goes to the neighboring molecules as heat. As expected, the photon with the longer wavelength carries less energy.

By how much does the molecule's energy go up?" Why: the molecule's energy rises by exactly the photon's energy. As a block equation: E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(8.85 × 10¹³ s⁻¹) = 5.86 × 10⁻²⁰ J. (The answer line shows "5.86 × 10⁻²⁰ J", not "5.86e-20 J"; the Why writes it the same way.)
- **Check 3**, `<Check answer={1}>`: "Photon A has a wavelength of 290 nm (ultraviolet). Photon B has a wavelength of 1500 nm (infrared). Which carries more energy, and about how many times as much?" Options: A, about 5.2 times as much; B, about 5.2 times as much; A, about 27 times as much; they carry the same energy, because both travel at the speed of light. - **Section "Absorbing and emitting":** an atom or molecule has definite energy levels (topic 3.11). When it absorbs a photon, its energy goes up by exactly the photon's energy; when it emits a photon, its energy goes down by exactly the photon's energy. The photon cannot be partly absorbed. So the gap between two levels, ΔE, fixes the photon that goes with them:

  $\Delta E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$

  Place the figure here. Then tie it back to the T-shirt: the brightener's blue photon carries less energy than the ultraviolet one because part of the absorbed energy went to its neighbors as heat along the way, so the drop that made the blue photon was a smaller one.

**Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "3.13"`, `minutes: 14`, summary: "How absorbance depends on concentration, path length and molar absorptivity, how a calibration line gives an unknown concentration, and which lab errors push the result up or down." (No colon in the summary.)
- **Framework (3.13.A, skill 2.E):** the Beer–Lambert law, A = εbc, links the light a solution absorbs to three things: the **molar absorptivity** ε, which says how strongly a species absorbs light of one particular wavelength; the **path length** b; and the concentration c. Path length and concentration together set how many absorbing particles are in the light's path. In most experiments b and the wavelength are held fixed, so absorbance is proportional to concentration alone. The instrument is set to the wavelength of maximum absorbance for the species, because that gives the most sensitive measurement. The suggested skill is identifying sources of experimental error, so the lesson names the common ones and the direction each pushes the result. The equation sheet gives A = εbc with A = absorbance, b = path length, c = concentration.
- **Opening:** pour grape juice into a tall glass. Look through it from the side: deep purple, but some light gets through. Look down through it from the top, through the whole height of juice: almost black. Mix half a glass of juice with the same amount of water and look from the side again: paler. Two things decide how much light a colored solution takes out: how far the light travels through it, and how much of the colored substance it meets on the way.
- **Section "Measuring absorbance":** a **spectrophotometer** sends light of one chosen wavelength through a sample held in a **cuvette** (a small clear container, usually 1.00 cm across) and measures how much light comes out the other side. It reports the **absorbance**, A: the more light the sample takes out, the larger A. Absorbance has no unit. Before measuring, you **blank** the instrument: you put in a cuvette of the solvent alone and set it to read zero, so that later readings count only the light absorbed by the dissolved substance, not light lost to the solvent or the cuvette's walls. Place the figure `3-13-spectrophotometer.svg` here. Then one short paragraph starting "**Beyond the exam:**": absorbance is a logarithmic scale. At A = 1.00, a tenth of the light gets through; at A = 2.00, a hundredth. The exam works with A itself.
- **Figure** `3-13-spectrophotometer.svg`: viewBox 480 wide, about 220 tall, font-size 18. A row, left to right: a lamp (a circle with short rays) labeled "light source"; a small box labeled "picks one wavelength"; a beam (a thick line with an arrowhead) going into a square cuvette drawn with a light shaded fill, labeled "sample"; a dimension bracket under the cuvette labeled "b"; a thinner beam leaving the cuvette into a box labeled "detector". Put the labels alternately above and below the row so they fit at 18. The beam leaving the sample is visibly thinner than the beam entering it. Alt: "A spectrophotometer drawn as a row: a light source, a part that picks one wavelength, a cuvette holding the sample with its width marked b, the path length, and a detector. The beam leaving the sample is thinner than the beam entering it, because the sample absorbs part of the light." Caption: "The detector measures how much light gets through the sample, and the instrument reports it as absorbance."
- **Section "A = εbc":** state the law as a block equation, $A = \varepsilon b c$, and name each letter: ε, the molar absorptivity, in M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ (the same as L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹); b, the path length, in cm; c, the concentration, in M (here c is concentration, not the speed of light; topic 3.12 uses c for the speed of light). The units cancel, which is why A has none. ε belongs to the substance and to the wavelength: the same substance has a large ε at a wavelength it absorbs strongly and a small one where it hardly absorbs. b and c count particles: double the path and the beam passes twice as many absorbing particles; double the concentration and the same is true. The particles can be molecules or ions: Cu²⁺ in water, for example, absorbs red light, which is why its solutions look blue. `<KeyIdea>`: absorbance is proportional to the number of absorbing particles in the light's path, set by b and c, and to how strongly each one absorbs at that wavelength, ε. Double b or double c and A doubles.
- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={3}>`: "A dye solution has an absorbance of 0.270 in a 1.00 cm cuvette. The same solution is poured into a 2.00 cm cuvette and measured at the same wavelength. What does it read?" Options: 0.135; 0.270; 0.540; 1.08. Why: ε and c have not changed, and A is proportional to b. Twice the path length puts twice as many absorbing particles in the beam, so A doubles to 0.540.
- **Check 2**, `<Check numeric="835" unit="M⁻¹ cm⁻¹">`: "A 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ M solution of substance Z has an absorbance of 0.668 in a 2.00 cm cuvette, at Z's wavelength of maximum absorbance. What is the molar absorptivity of Z at that wavelength?" Why: as a block equation, ε = A/(bc) = 0.668/[(2.00 cm)(4.00 × 10⁻⁴ M)] = 835 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Forgetting the path length gives 1670, twice too large. (Substance Z is made up.)
- **Section "Choosing the wavelength":** an **absorption spectrum** is a graph of one solution's absorbance against wavelength. It rises to a peak at the **wavelength of maximum absorbance**, written λmax (say it in words too). A solution looks the color of the light it lets through, so it absorbs most strongly the colors you do not see: a blue solution absorbs mostly orange and red light. With the path length and the wavelength fixed, εb is one fixed number, so A = (εb)c is a straight line through the origin when A is plotted against c, and its slope is εb. At λmax, ε is largest, so that line is steepest: a small change in concentration gives the largest change in absorbance. That is the most sensitive measurement, and it is why the instrument is set to λmax. A second, smaller reason: at the top of the peak the spectrum is nearly flat, so if the wavelength setting is slightly off, the absorbance hardly changes. - **Section "Finding an unknown concentration":** a **calibration line** is made from **standards**, solutions of known concentration of the same substance. Measure each at λmax in the same cuvette, after blanking. Plot absorbance against concentration: the points lie on a straight line through the origin, with slope εb. Then measure the unknown and divide: c = A ÷ slope. Say that the unknown's absorbance should fall within the range of the standards, so the result comes from the measured part of the line. - **Worked example "Blue dye in a sports drink":** a student finds the concentration of the blue dye in a sports drink. She makes four standards of the dye and measures them at 630 nm, the dye's λmax, in a 1.00 cm cuvette, after blanking with water. The readings are made up for this example. Give them as a small two-column table (Concentration (µM) | Absorbance): 1.50 | 0.195; 3.00 | 0.390; 4.50 | 0.585; 6.00 | 0.780. The drink, measured the same way, reads 0.455. (1 µM, micromolar, is 10⁻⁶ M; "micro" is on the equation sheet.)
  1. **Plot the standards.** The four points lie on a straight line through the origin, as A = εbc predicts.
  2. **Find the slope.** Use the origin and the last standard, as a block equation: slope = 0.780 / 6.00 µM = 0.130 µM⁻¹. (Any standard gives the same: 0.195 / 1.50 µM = 0.130 µM⁻¹.)
  3. **Read the slope as εb.** ε = slope ÷ b = 0.130 µM⁻¹ ÷ 1.00 cm = 0.130 µM⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Since 1 µM⁻¹ = 10⁶ M⁻¹, that is 1.30 × 10⁵ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. The slope is not a concentration.
  4. **Find the unknown.** As a block equation: c = A ÷ slope = 0.455 / 0.130 µM⁻¹ = 3.50 µM, which is 3.50 × 10⁻⁶ M.
  5. **Check it.** 0.455 lies between the readings of the 3.00 µM and 4.50 µM standards (0.390 and 0.585), and 3.50 µM lies between those concentrations.
- **Check 4**, `<Check numeric="2.40" unit="µM">`: "Using the same calibration line, a second blue drink reads 0.312 at 630 nm in the same cuvette. What is its dye concentration, in µM?" Why: c = A ÷ slope = 0.312 / 0.130 µM⁻¹ = 2.40 µM. It lies between the 1.50 µM and 3.00 µM standards, as its absorbance lies between theirs (0.195 and 0.390).
- **Section "When the result is wrong":** the lab skill here is spotting what can go wrong and which way it pushes the answer. Since c = A ÷ slope, whatever makes the unknown's absorbance read too high makes the concentration come out too high, and the other way round. Above the table add: "In each case the mistake happens only when the unknown is measured; the standards were measured properly." Give a table (three columns: What went wrong | Absorbance reads | Concentration comes out):

  | What went wrong | Absorbance reads | Concentration comes out |
  |---|---|---|
  | Fingerprints, smudges or scratches on the cuvette for the unknown | Too high | Too high |
  | Air bubbles in the light path | Too high | Too high |
  | Cuvette still wet with water, not rinsed with the solution first | Too low | Too low |
  | Unknown read at a wavelength off the peak, standards at the peak | Too low | Too low |
  | Instrument re-zeroed with an empty holder, not a cuvette of solvent, before the unknown | Too high | Too high |

  Then the reasons, briefly. Fingerprints, scratches and bubbles scatter or block light, and the instrument cannot tell light that was scattered from light that was absorbed, so it counts it all as absorbed. Leftover water dilutes the sample, so fewer absorbing particles sit in the beam. Off the peak, ε is smaller (the peak is the highest point of the spectrum, so moving either way lowers it). Without a proper blank, the light lost to the cuvette's walls and the solvent is counted as absorbance by the solute. Add: if the standards were measured without a proper blank too, every reading carries the same extra absorbance and the calibration line crosses the absorbance axis above zero instead of passing through the origin: a sign to blank again. And if everything, standards and unknown, is measured at the same wrong wavelength, the result is not pushed either way; it is just less sensitive.
- **Check 5**, `<Check answer={1}>`: "A student leaves the unknown sitting uncovered in its cuvette, and some of the water evaporates before the reading is taken. Is the concentration the student calculates for the unknown too high, too low, or unaffected?" Options: too high; too low; unaffected, because the amount of dye in the cuvette has not changed; unaffected, because the standards were measured correctly. Why: the dye stays behind while water leaves, so the solution becomes more concentrated and more absorbing particles sit in each cubic centimeter of the beam. The absorbance rises. Since c = A ÷ slope, the concentration comes out too high. It is true that the amount of dye is unchanged, but the calibration line relates absorbance to the concentration in the cuvette at the time of the reading, and the standards being correct does not protect the unknown's reading. (This error is not in the table above, so the student must reason it through.)
- **Trap:** **Calling the slope a concentration** (the slope is εb; divide the unknown's absorbance by it to get c). **Forgetting b when finding ε** (ε = slope ÷ b, or ε = A/(bc); with a 1.00 cm cuvette the number is the same but the unit is not). **Mixing µM and M** (1 µM = 10⁻⁶ M; keep one unit all the way through). **Thinking every error gives a high result** (anything that scatters or blocks light makes A, and so c, too high; anything that dilutes the sample or lowers ε makes them too low). **Thinking any wavelength will do** (the standards and the unknown must be measured at the same wavelength, and λmax gives the most sensitive line).
- **Key points:** the Beer–Lambert law is A = εbc, where ε is how strongly the species absorbs at that wavelength, b is the path length and c is the concentration; b and c together set how many absorbing particles are in the light's path; with b and the wavelength fixed, absorbance is proportional to concentration, so a plot of A against c is a straight line through the origin with slope εb; the wavelength of maximum absorbance is chosen because ε is largest there, giving the steepest line and the most sensitive measurement; an unknown's concentration is its absorbance divided by the slope of the calibration line; fingerprints, scratches, bubbles and a blank taken without the solvent-filled cuvette before the unknown make the result too high, and a sample diluted by leftover water or read off the peak makes it too low.
