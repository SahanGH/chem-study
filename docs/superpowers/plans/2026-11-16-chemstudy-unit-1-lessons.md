# ChemStudy Unit 1 Lessons, Diagrams and Reference Pages Implementation Plan

> **For agentic workers:** REQUIRED SUB-SKILL: Use superpowers:subagent-driven-development (recommended) or superpowers:executing-plans to implement this plan task-by-task. Steps use checkbox (`- [ ]`) syntax for tracking.

**Goal:** Put Unit 1 (Atomic Structure and Properties) on chem-study.com:
- two reference pages: the periodic table and the exam's equations and constants;
- eight lessons, 1.1 to 1.8;
- two interactive diagrams: a photoelectron spectrum explorer (1.6) and a periodic trends explorer (1.7);
- the drawn figures the lessons need.

**Architecture:**
- **Lessons** are MDX files built from the existing lesson blocks.
- **The reference pages** are plain pages with no script: the periodic table is an Astro page built from one data file, `src/data/elements.ts`; the equations page is an MDX page, so its math goes through the lessons' KaTeX pipeline.
- **Interactive diagrams** are Svelte components drawn in SVG, wrapped in Astro blocks and loaded when they scroll into view, as in Units 2, 3, 5 and 7.
- **Every number a diagram shows** comes from a plain TypeScript module with unit tests: `src/lib/pes.ts` and `src/lib/periodic-trends.ts`.

**Tech Stack:** Astro 7, MDX, Svelte 5, KaTeX 0.16 + mhchem, Vitest, Playwright + axe.

**Spec:** `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-16-chemstudy-unit-1-lessons-design.md`.

**How this plan is written.**

1. **Lesson prose is not in this plan.** Each lesson task carries a brief that fixes all the chemistry:
   - what the framework requires;
   - the sections;
   - the worked examples with their numbers;
   - every Check with its answer and explanation;
   - the traps, the key points and the figures.

   The writer supplies only the sentences.
2. **Every number in a brief is recomputed by a script.** Each brief has a check script in the session scratch folder (`scratchpad/unit1/checks/1-N.py`), which the controller ran before this plan was committed. For a numeric Check, the script also computes what each common wrong method gives and asserts that it lies more than 2 percent from the answer, so the grader catches the mistake.
3. **The briefs were checked independently before this plan was committed.**
   - **Fact-checks, on Fable 5.2**, by fresh agents that solved every Check and worked example from the question alone before reading the answer, recomputed every number, confirmed every value of a real substance against a source (NIST for isotopes and ionization energies, Shannon's table for ionic radii, the data files for binding energies and radii) and tested every ranking against the data. Each group of briefs was checked, revised and checked again; lessons 1.5 to 1.8 were checked a third time after their last changes. **No round found a wrong answer, number, molar mass, isotope value, configuration, count, formula or ranking.** What they changed was wording that said more than is true:
     - **1.1:** "every conversion runs through moles" and "nothing ties mass straight to particles" are not literally true (the mass of one atom does); the lesson now says that with a molar mass and Avogadro's number no single step takes grams to particles. A Check whose correct option repeated the key idea's words now offers lettered setups.
     - **1.2:** "the average lies nearest the tallest peak" is false for zinc, the lesson's own Check; it lies between the lightest and heaviest peaks, pulled toward the tallest, and nearer the taller one only when there are two isotopes. Two Checks named the element, so the answer could be read off the periodic table; they now say "a certain metal". "Every atom has a nucleus of protons and neutrons" is untrue of hydrogen-1.
     - **1.3:** the rule for a ratio that is not whole now ends with a check, because neighboring fractions (.2 and .25, .33 and .4) are easy to confuse.
     - **1.4:** "count the kinds of particle" misleads for an ionic compound, which lesson 1.3 draws as two kinds of ion in one pure substance, and "identical particles" collides with isotopes; the test now sorts particles of the same kind, with the formula unit as the thing sorted for an ionic compound.
     - **1.5:** the f block was called fourteen boxes wide; the exam's table and the site's page print fifteen in each lower row, one of them the row's d-block box. The test for an excited state was scoped wrongly (a cation that keeps d electrons can have an empty s subshell in its ground state). "For the same subshell in two atoms, the lever is the nuclear charge" predicts a rise from nitrogen to oxygen that the data do not show; it is limited to inner subshells.
     - **1.6:** "every peak moves to higher energy as protons are added" is true of inner subshells only; the outermost subshell's peak slips back twice among the first twenty elements (nitrogen to oxygen, phosphorus to sulfur), and a new subshell's peak is a new peak.
     - **1.7:** the two breaks in first ionization energy are large in period 2, present in period 3, nearly level in period 4 and gone in period 5, by the data; the lesson says so, with the steps. "Down a group the radius grows" became "generally grows" (aluminum to gallium is 1 pm). The comparison of an atom's radius with its ion's now says that the two are measured differently.
     - **1.8:** "the largest jump marks the end of the valence electrons" is not true in general: a longer list has later, larger jumps, and a list that stops inside the valence electrons shows the step from p to s electrons as its largest. The lesson says "the first big jump". Group 13's statements were over-broad (thallium; a fourth ionization energy only about twice the third for gallium and indium).
   - **Originality checks, on Sonnet**, against both Tro books, the teacher's notes and practice, the site's lessons and practice, and the other Unit 1 briefs. No brief shared a number with a source. The matches were of setting, analogy or kind of problem, and every one was replaced by an item that had been searched for first:

     - **1.5:** the "impossible" configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁷ is the one the teacher's Worksheet 3 asks about (now 1s³ 2s² 2p⁶); tin's core-and-valence count paralleled a textbook example (now tellurium); zinc oxide in sunscreen was in two lessons (now gallium(III) oxide).

## Rules for writing a lesson (Tasks 5–8, 10, 11, 13, 14)

Read `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-4-ideal-gas-law.mdx` first: it is the model for a lesson with calculations (display math with `aligned`, numeric Checks, a "why" in block form). `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-8-representations-of-solutions.mdx` is the model for particle drawings, and `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-1-intermolecular-and-interparticle-forces.mdx` for a lesson that explains.

**The file.** `src/content/lessons/unit-1/<file name in the task>`. The name must be `<unit>-<number>-<slug>.mdx` matching the topic, or the build fails. Front matter is exactly three fields:

```mdx
---
topic: "1.1"
minutes: 12
summary: One sentence, 20 to 200 characters, saying what the lesson teaches.
---
```

Put the summary in double quotes if it contains a colon. The title comes from `src/data/units.ts`. Do not write an `# H1`.

**The seven parts, in order.** The build fails if a lesson lacks a worked example, a trap box, key points, practice-next or three checks.

1. Opening: two or three sentences, no heading, starting from something concrete.
2. Explanation: `##` sections, one idea each. At least one `<KeyIdea>`.
3. `<Worked title="...">` with numbered steps.
4. `<Check>` questions spread through the lesson, each placed right after the idea it tests. Never all at the end.
5. `<Trap>`: the specific mistakes for this topic, as a short list with the mistake in bold.
6. `<KeyPoints>`: four to six bullets. This is the review sheet, so each bullet must stand on its own.
7. `<PracticeNext />` as the last line, with no attributes.

**Markup.**

- Math is KaTeX inside `$...$` or a `$$` block. Formulas and equations use `\ce{...}`: `$\ce{Mg3N2}$`, `$\ce{Ca^2+}$`. Units in math are upright: `$\text{g/mol}$`, `$\text{mol}$`. In prose, plain Unicode is fine for a simple formula (Mg₃N₂, Ca²⁺) or symbol when it needs no math.
- **An electron configuration is plain text, never math**: `1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴`, with a space between subshells and Unicode superscripts (⁰ ¹ ² ³ ⁴ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ ⁸ ⁹), and `[Ne] 3s² 3p⁴` for the shorthand. Written so, a long configuration wraps on a phone; inside `$...$` it cannot.
- A choice check: `<Check answer={2}>`, the question, a blank line, a `-` list of options, a blank line, then `<div slot="why">...</div>`. `answer` is the 1-based number of the correct option. **An option is never math alone**: write a configuration or a formula as plain Unicode text, or letter the expressions A to D in the question.
- A numeric check: `<Check numeric="0.250">` (add `unit="..."` when there is one). **The number is text in quotes**, so its trailing zeros (significant figures) survive. A very small or large answer is written in e-notation, `numeric="3.01e23"`. The student's answer is accepted within 2 percent.
- A figure: `<Figure src="/figures/<name>.svg" alt="..." caption="..." />`.
- Inside a block (`<Worked>`, `<Trap>`, `<KeyPoints>`, `<KeyIdea>`), leave a blank line after the opening tag and before the closing tag.
- Excluded material goes in a paragraph that begins "**Beyond the exam:**". There is no `<Beyond>` block in lessons.
- Refer to another lesson by topic number in plain text ("topic 1.5", "topic 3.12"), with no links. The periodic table page is linked once per lesson that uses it: `[periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/)`.
- **Phone width.** Math in a sentence cannot wrap, so every equation and calculation goes on its own line (`$$ ... $$` with blank lines around it), split with `aligned` when long, including inside a Check's `<div slot="why">`. Keep each line to about 28 characters of rendered math; a long substitution carries numbers only, with the units stated in words beside it, or is split over two lines. Tables have at most five columns and short cells. No lesson may scroll sideways at 320 px with every answer open.
- **Astro drops the space between text and an inline element on the next line.** Keep an inline tag on the same line as the word before it.

**Voice.** A teacher talking, not a textbook. Start from something concrete. Short sentences. No "in this section we will". Say "you". Explain why before how. One new term at a time, in bold the first time, defined in the same sentence.

**Chemistry.**

- Teach what the brief's "Framework" line lists, and nothing the exam excludes unless the brief says to, in a "Beyond the exam" paragraph.
- Use the brief's numbers exactly. They were checked when this plan was written. Recompute each as you write it; if one disagrees, stop and say so; do not quietly change it.
- Do not add a worked example or a check that the brief does not list. Do not drop one.
- **A Check must not be answered word for word by the sentence, caption or key idea just above it.** If the brief places a Check right after a sentence that states its answer, keep the Check, word the correct option differently, and report it.
- Atomic masses are the exam's periodic table's, to the figures it prints. Avogadro's number is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ mol⁻¹.
- **Every explanation of a trend, an ionization energy or a peak position is a claim followed by a Coulomb's-law reason** that names nuclear charge, distance or shielding. An atom does not "want" or "like" anything, and "a full octet is stable" is not a reason.
- **"Only", "always", "every" and "never"** in a statement of fact must be literally true. State the condition or soften the claim.
- The lesson assumes nothing from Units 2 to 9.
- Never take an example, a data table or a sentence from a textbook.

**Static figures.** Hand-written SVG in `public/figures/`, in the style of Unit 3's (`3-8-concentration-boxes.svg` for particles, `3-4-pressure-volume.svg` for graphs): a `viewBox` 480 wide (taller where a figure needs it), a `#FDFCF9` background rectangle, `font-family="system-ui, sans-serif" font-size="18"`, ink `#26231F`, secondary `#6B655D`, accent `#4F706C`, light accent fill `#E3EAE9`.

- Two things in one figure that must be told apart differ in shape, shading or dash pattern as well as color. Label directly; do not rely on a legend alone.
- Particle drawings keep the counts the brief gives exactly.
- **A mass spectrum** is drawn as vertical bars: mass on the horizontal axis, relative abundance in percent on the vertical axis, each bar at its isotope's mass with the height of its abundance, and its percent written above it.
- **A photoelectron spectrum** is drawn with binding energy in MJ/mol on a logarithmic horizontal axis, larger values at the left, and "Relative number of electrons" on the vertical axis. Each peak sits at its binding energy; its height is proportional to its number of electrons. Peaks are narrow and of one width.
- Plot curves, bars and points from computed coordinates, not by eye.
- No line may pass behind or touch a particle or label it does not belong to.
- The alt text says what the figure shows in words and must be true of the figure as drawn, including counts and values.
- **Render every figure and look at it** before reporting: `node <scratchpad>/unit3/svg-shot.cjs <a new output folder> 390 <name.svg>`. Fix overlaps and any text that would be under 12 px when the figure is 320 px wide.

### Task 5: Lesson 1.1, Moles and Molar Mass

**Files:**

**Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "1.1"`, `minutes: 13`, summary: "How chemists count atoms by weighing them, what a mole and a molar mass are, and how to get from grams to a number of particles and back, with moles in the middle." (No colon in the summary.)
- **Framework (1.1.A, skill 5.B):** the objective is to calculate amounts of a substance, or its relative number of particles, with dimensional analysis and the mole. Three statements. (1) Particles cannot be counted one by one in the laboratory, so chemistry needs a link between the mass of a sample and the number of particles in it. (2) Avogadro's number, $6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$, is that link between the moles of a pure substance and its number of particles (or formula units). (3) The average mass of one atom, molecule or formula unit in atomic mass units is the same number as the molar mass of the substance in grams, which ties mass to count. The suggested skill is choosing the right relationship for a problem, which is why one Check asks for the setup and not the answer. **The equation sheet** gives $n = m/M$ and Avogadro's number as a constant. It gives no equation for particles from moles; the lesson says so. No exclusions. Not needed and not in this lesson: the carbon-12 definitions of the mole and the amu, density, gas volumes, molarity (topic 3.7), reactions.
- **Notes for the writer.** This is the first lesson a student meets on the site, so it leans on nothing else. "Atom", "molecule" and "formula" get a clause of explanation the first time each appears. Do not use a dozen (eggs or anything else), grains of sand, pennies, a drop of water or any "how big is a mole" comparison: the lesson shows the size of a mole with the 28.09 g of silicon and nothing more. No density anywhere. Every setup shows its units, and the units cancel. The first Check that needs a power of ten carries the typing hint the site already uses: "(To type a power of ten such as 2.5 × 10⁻³, write 2.5e-3 or 2.5 x 10^-3.)"
- **Opening:** the silicon that computer chips are built on is not quite pure. As the silicon crystal is grown, the maker adds a trace of another element, phosphorus for example: about one phosphorus atom for every ten million silicon atoms. No tweezers place atoms like that, and nobody counts them out. The added element is measured out by mass. Chemists count atoms the same way, by weighing, and for the same reason: there are too many to count and each is too small to pick out. (Keep "for example" and "about": real doping levels vary widely. No calculation in the opening.)
- **Section "Counting by weighing":** two short paragraphs. A chemical formula is a statement about numbers of atoms: a molecule of propane, C₃H₈, is three carbon atoms and eight hydrogen atoms joined together. So chemists need to know how many particles a sample holds. But a balance reads mass, not number, and the numbers are beyond counting: the silicon wafer of the worked example below holds more than 10²⁴ atoms. The way out is to count by weighing. If you know the mass of one particle, the mass of the sample tells you how many particles are in it. The rest of the lesson builds the two tools that make this work: a counting unit sized for atoms, and the mass of that many particles.
- **Section "The mole":** a **mole** is a number of particles: $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ of them. That number is **Avogadro's number**, $N_A$, and the equation sheet gives it. The symbol for an amount in moles is $n$, and the unit is written mol. Always say which particle you are counting: a mole of silicon atoms, a mole of propane molecules. To go from moles to a number of particles, multiply by Avogadro's number, as a block: $\text{number of particles} = n \times N_A$. The equation sheet does not print this one; you have to know it. Example, as a block: $2.00\ \text{mol} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms/mol} = 1.20 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms}$. Going the other way, from particles to moles, you divide. Why so large a number? Because atoms are so small. A mole of silicon atoms is a lump of 28.09 g, small enough to hold in one hand. The next section says where the 28.09 comes from.
- **Section "Atomic mass units and molar mass":**
  - A gram is far too big a unit for one atom, so atoms are weighed in **atomic mass units**, amu. A hydrogen atom has a mass of about 1 amu.
  - Look at the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/) (the one link of this lesson, written exactly so). The decimal number in an element's box is the **average mass of one atom of that element, in amu**: 28.09 amu for silicon. It is an average because the atoms of one element do not all have the same mass; topic 1.2 explains.
  - The mole was sized so that the same number does a second job. One silicon atom: 28.09 amu, on average. One mole of silicon atoms: 28.09 g. The mass of one mole of a substance is its **molar mass**, $M$, in grams per mole: 28.09 g/mol for silicon.
  - Why it works, in three sentences: one gram is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ amu (so 1 amu is $1.661 \times 10^{-24}$ g, a number you never need to remember). Take a mole of silicon atoms and add up their masses, as a block: $28.09\ \text{amu} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 1.692 \times 10^{25}\ \text{amu}$, and that many amu is 28.09 g. Going from one atom to a mole multiplies the mass by Avogadro's number, which is exactly the step from amu to grams.
  - `<KeyIdea>`: the number in an element's box on the periodic table does two jobs. It is the average mass of one atom in amu, and it is the mass of one mole of atoms in grams.
- **Section "The molar mass of a compound":** a compound's formula says how many atoms of each element are in one particle of it, so its molar mass is the sum of the atomic masses, each multiplied by its subscript. Three examples, each calculation as a block:
  - Methanol, CH₃OH, the alcohol in windshield washer fluid, has one C, four H and one O: $12.01 + 4(1.008) + 16.00 = 32.04\ \text{g/mol}$. (4 × 1.008 = 4.032; the sum, 32.042, is kept to the two decimal places that 12.01 and 16.00 allow.) The two jobs of the number carry over from atoms to molecules: one methanol molecule has an average mass of 32.04 amu, and one mole of methanol molecules has a mass of 32.04 g.
  - **Parentheses.** A subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside. Aluminum hydroxide, Al(OH)₃, an antacid, has one Al, three O and three H: $26.98 + 3(16.00) + 3(1.008) = 78.00\ \text{g/mol}$ (26.98 + 48.00 + 3.024 = 78.004).
  - **Elements that come as molecules.** Under ordinary conditions seven elements are made of two-atom molecules: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂. A mole of oxygen gas is a mole of O₂ molecules, and its molar mass is $2(16.00) = 32.00\ \text{g/mol}$, not 16.00.
  - One sentence on the name of the particle: methanol and oxygen are made of molecules. A compound such as Al(OH)₃ is not; its formula gives the simplest group of atoms in the right ratio, called a **formula unit**, and a mole of it is a mole of formula units (topic 1.3 says more).
- **Section "Between grams and moles":** the equation sheet gives the relation, as a block: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$, with $n$ in moles, $m$ the mass in grams and $M$ the molar mass in g/mol. (On the sheet the molar mass is a bold $M$; a plain $M$ there means something else, molarity, which belongs to topic 3.7.) Rearranged, $m = nM$. The safest way to use it is to write the molar mass as a conversion factor and let the units decide whether it goes on top or underneath. Two examples, each as a block:
  - Grams to moles, 80.1 g of methanol: $80.1\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{32.04\ \text{g}} = 2.50\ \text{mol}$. The grams cancel and moles are left.
  - Moles to grams, 0.150 mol of Al(OH)₃: $0.150\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{78.00\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 11.7\ \text{g}$. The moles cancel and grams are left.

  This way of working, carrying the units and canceling them, is called **dimensional analysis**. If the units of your answer are wrong, the setup is wrong.

- **Worked example "Atoms in a silicon wafer":** computer chips are built on thin disks of silicon called wafers. A wafer 300 mm across has a mass of about 128 g. How many silicon atoms is that?   1. **Plan the route.** Grams to moles to atoms: two steps, through moles.
  2. **Grams to moles.** Silicon's molar mass is 28.09 g/mol. As a block: $128\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{28.09\ \text{g}} = 4.557\ \text{mol}$. That is 4.56 mol to three significant figures; carry the extra digit and round at the end.
  3. **Moles to atoms.** As a block, split with `aligned`: $4.557\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 2.74 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms}$.

- **Section "The empirical formula":** now the other direction. An analysis of an unknown compound gives masses or percents, and you want a formula. What composition can give you is the **empirical formula**: the formula with the lowest whole-number ratio of atoms. For a substance without molecules, such as SnO₂ or KBr, the formula you already write is usually the empirical formula: 1 to 2 and 1 to 1 cannot be reduced. For a substance made of molecules there is a second formula, the **molecular formula**, which counts the atoms in one molecule, and the two can differ. Hydrazine, a rocket fuel, has molecules of two nitrogen atoms and four hydrogen atoms: its molecular formula is N₂H₄, and its empirical formula, with the ratio cut down to lowest terms, is NH₂. For hydrogen sulfide the two are the same, H₂S, because 2 to 1 cannot be reduced. The molecular formula is always the empirical formula multiplied through by a whole number: 2 for hydrazine, 1 for hydrogen sulfide. Composition alone cannot tell you that whole number, because N₂H₄ and NH₂ have the same mass percents. The molar mass can.
- **Section "From composition to a formula":** four steps, as a numbered list.
  1. **Get masses.** If you are given percents, imagine a 100 g sample: each percent becomes that many grams.
  2. **Change each mass to moles**, dividing by the element's atomic mass. A formula counts atoms, not grams, so the comparison has to be made in moles.
  3. **Divide every mole amount by the smallest one.** That sets the smallest to 1.
  4. **Make the ratios whole.** If a ratio is within a few hundredths of a whole number, round it. If it is not, do not round: multiply every ratio by the small whole number that clears the fraction.

  Then the rule for step 4, as a sentence or two of plain words (no table): read the leftover decimal as a fraction. A leftover near .5 is a half, so double every ratio. Near .33 or .67 it is thirds, so triple them. Near .25 or .75 it is quarters, so multiply by four. Near .2, .4, .6 or .8 it is fifths, so multiply by five. Round only when a ratio is within a few hundredths of a whole number. Then check: after multiplying, every ratio should be within a few hundredths of a whole number. If one is not, the multiplier was wrong, so try the next.

  Then one paragraph on the molecular formula: if you also know the molar mass, divide it by the mass of one empirical formula. The answer is the whole number to multiply the empirical formula by. If it comes out as 1, the molecular formula is the empirical formula.
- **Worked example "The formula of hexamine":** hexamine is the white solid in the fuel tablets that hikers light to heat a meal. It contains only carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen: 51.40 percent C, 8.63 percent H and 39.97 percent N by mass. A separate measurement gives its molar mass, 140.2 g/mol. Find its empirical formula and its molecular formula.
  1. **Get masses.** In 100 g: 51.40 g of C, 8.63 g of H and 39.97 g of N.
  2. **Change to moles.** As a block, split with `aligned`, one element to a line: C: $\dfrac{51.40}{12.01} = 4.280\ \text{mol}$; H: $\dfrac{8.63}{1.008} = 8.562\ \text{mol}$; N: $\dfrac{39.97}{14.01} = 2.853\ \text{mol}$.
  3. **Divide by the smallest,** 2.853. C: 4.280 ÷ 2.853 = 1.50. H: 8.562 ÷ 2.853 = 3.00. N: 1.00.
  4. **Make the ratios whole.** 3.00 is whole. 1.50 is nowhere near a whole number, and the leftover .50 is a half, so double everything: C 3, H 6, N 2. The empirical formula is C₃H₆N₂.
  5. **Find the multiplier.** One C₃H₆N₂ has a mass of, as a block: $3(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 2(14.01) = 70.10\ \text{g/mol}$ (36.03 + 6.048 + 28.02). As a block: $\dfrac{140.2}{70.10} = 2.00$.
  6. **Write the molecular formula.** Twice C₃H₆N₂ is C₆H₁₂N₄.
  7. **See what rounding would have done.** Round 1.50 down to 1 and the formula becomes CH₃N, with a mass of 29.04; then 140.2 ÷ 29.04 = 4.83. Round it up to 2 and you get C₂H₃N, 41.05, and 140.2 ÷ 41.05 = 3.42. Neither is a whole number. A multiplier that will not come out whole is a sign that a ratio was rounded when it should have been multiplied.

- **Framework (1.4.A, skill 5.A):** the objective is to explain how the composition by mass of the elements in a sample is tied, in numbers, to the substances that make up a mixture. Two statements. (1) A pure substance contains atoms, molecules or formula units of a single kind; a mixture contains two or more kinds, in proportions that can vary. (2) Elemental analysis can find the relative numbers of atoms in a sample and can test its purity. The suggested skill is picking out the quantities a problem needs from the information given, so one Check asks which measurements are needed, not for a number. No exclusions; nothing on the equation sheet beyond $n = m/M$. **This is a short lesson; do not pad it.** Not taught here: how mixtures are separated (topic 3.9), solutions and molarity (topic 3.7), kinds of mixture (uniform or not).
- **Notes for the writer.** The lesson relies on topics 1.1 (moles and molar mass) and 1.3 (formula units, mass percent, the law of definite proportions). "Elemental analysis" gets one clause: a measurement of the mass of each element in a weighed sample. No brass, karat gold, sand and salt, table salt or any mixture of two salts, and no tablet.
- **Opening:** pure zinc oxide, ZnO, is 80.34 percent zinc by mass, in any sample. Its formula settles that. A tube of zinc-oxide sunscreen is another matter: the maker can stir in a little zinc oxide or a lot, so one brand is 5 percent zinc oxide and another is 25. No formula will tell you which. For a mixture, the composition is something you measure.
- **Section "One pile or more":** here is a test that works for any sample. Imagine sorting its particles into piles, identical ones together. If everything ends up in one pile, the sample is a **pure substance**. If you need two piles or more, it is a **mixture**, and nothing fixes how much goes in each pile. Identical here means the same kind: atoms of one element go in one pile whatever their isotope (topic 1.2), and so do molecules built from the same atoms joined the same way. What you sort is the unit the substance is built from: single atoms for an element such as argon, molecules for a substance made of molecules, formula units for a substance that has no molecules. An element made of molecules, such as O₂, is also one pile. A compound is still one pile, even though it contains more than one kind of **atom**, because its atoms come joined in one fixed pattern. That goes for an ionic compound too. Potassium bromide holds two kinds of ion, but always one K⁺ for each Br⁻, so you sort it by formula units' worth, as in topic 1.3's figure, not by separate ions, and each formula unit's worth is like every other. Place the figure `1-4-element-compound-mixture.svg` here and read the three boxes in a sentence each: the first box holds single atoms, all alike (one pile: an element); the second holds molecules, all alike, each built of two kinds of atom (one pile: a compound); the third holds two different kinds of molecule (two piles: a mixture, here of two compounds). Sorting by kinds of atom would call the second box a mixture, and it is not.

- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={3}>`: "Which of these samples is a pure substance?" Options: "Dry air: nitrogen molecules, oxygen molecules and argon atoms"; "Bronze: copper atoms with tin atoms scattered among them, in amounts that differ from one casting to the next"; "A quartz crystal: silicon and oxygen atoms bonded in a 1 to 2 ratio throughout"; "Seawater: water molecules with sodium ions, chloride ions and other ions among them". Why: sort each sample into piles. Quartz has two kinds of atom, but they are locked together in one fixed ratio, SiO₂, all the way through, so every formula unit is like every other: one pile, a pure compound. Dry air needs at least three piles: nitrogen molecules, oxygen molecules and argon atoms. Bronze needs two, copper atoms and tin atoms, in whatever proportion the foundry chose. Seawater is a mixture because it holds water molecules and dissolved ions in amounts that vary: the ions are not joined to the water in any fixed ratio, and one sea is saltier than another. Two kinds of atom do not make a mixture; two piles do.
- **Section "Fixed for a compound, measured for a mixture":** two short paragraphs.
  - A compound's composition by mass is fixed by its formula (topic 1.3). A mixture's is not fixed by anything: it depends on how the mixture was made. So a mass percent for a mixture cannot be worked out from a formula. It has to be **measured**, and a different batch can give a different number.
  - The atoms show the same thing. Bronze is copper with tin mixed in. Take a bronze that is 80.0 percent copper and 20.0 percent tin by mass. In 100.0 g there are, as a block split with `aligned`: $\dfrac{80.0\ \text{g}}{63.55\ \text{g/mol}} = 1.259\ \text{mol Cu}$ and $\dfrac{20.0\ \text{g}}{118.71\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.1685\ \text{mol Sn}$. That is 7.47 copper atoms for every tin atom: not a ratio of small whole numbers, and a bronze with 10.0 percent tin gives a different one, 16.8 to 1. A compound's atoms come in a fixed whole-number ratio. A mixture's need not, and the ratio changes from one batch to the next.
- `<KeyIdea>`: a pure compound has the composition its formula gives. A mixture has whatever composition it was made with, so its composition must be measured.
- **Section "What one element can tell you":** an **elemental analysis** measures the mass of each element in a weighed sample. When one of those elements is found in only one substance of the mixture, its mass leads straight to the mass of that substance: grams of the element, to moles of the element, to moles of the substance (using the formula's subscript), to grams of the substance. Divide by the mass of the sample and you have the mixture's composition.
- **Worked example "Zinc oxide in a sunscreen":** a mineral sunscreen is zinc oxide, ZnO, stirred into a lotion that contains no zinc. A chemist analyzes a 2.00 g sample of the sunscreen and finds 0.321 g of zinc in it. What percent of the sunscreen, by mass, is zinc oxide?
  1. **Decide what you need.** The mass of zinc oxide in the sample, and the mass of the sample. All the zinc is in the zinc oxide, so the zinc will give the first; the second is 2.00 g.
  2. **Grams of zinc to moles of zinc.** As a block: $\dfrac{0.321\ \text{g}}{65.38\ \text{g/mol}} = 4.910 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol Zn}$.
  3. **Moles of zinc to moles of zinc oxide.** Each formula unit of ZnO holds one zinc, so there are $4.910 \times 10^{-3}$ mol of ZnO.
  4. **Moles of zinc oxide to grams.** The molar mass of ZnO is 65.38 + 16.00 = 81.38 g/mol. As a block, split with `aligned`: $4.910 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \times 81.38\ \text{g/mol} = 0.3996\ \text{g ZnO}$.
  5. **Percent of the sample.** As a block: $\dfrac{0.3996\ \text{g}}{2.00\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 20.0\%$. The other 1.60 g is lotion.
  6. **Check by another road.** Pure ZnO is 80.34 percent zinc, so the zinc oxide holding 0.321 g of zinc has a mass of 0.321 ÷ 0.8034 = 0.3996 g. The same.
  7. **The answer to avoid.** 0.321 ÷ 2.00 is 16 percent. That is the percent of **zinc** in the sunscreen, not of zinc oxide: the oxygen in the zinc oxide has been left out.
- **Section "Testing purity":** the same measurement tests whether a sample is pure. A pure compound must show the mass percent its formula gives. A jar labeled zinc oxide should analyze at 80.34 percent zinc. If it comes out at 76 percent, the jar is not pure zinc oxide: something that holds less zinc, or none, is mixed in. And if the impurity holds none of that element, the element's mass tells you how much of the sample is the compound, just as in the worked example.

Tumbling has left the two fabrics with opposite charges, and opposite charges pull on each other. That same pull holds every atom together: a positive nucleus, negative electrons. This lesson is about where an atom's electrons are and how firmly that force holds each one.
- **Section "What an atom is made of":** an atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** at its center, made of **protons** (charge 1+) and **neutrons** (no charge). Around it are the **electrons** (charge 1−). Nearly all of the atom's mass is in the nucleus. The number of protons is the **atomic number**, the whole number in each box of the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/), and it decides which element the atom is: 14 protons is silicon. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so a silicon atom has 14 electrons. An **ion** has a different number of electrons from protons, and its charge is protons minus electrons: 56 protons with 54 electrons is a barium ion, Ba²⁺; 53 protons with 54 electrons is an iodide ion, I⁻. Neutrons change an atom's mass and nothing in this lesson (topic 1.2 is about them).
- **Section "Coulomb's law":** two charged particles push or pull on each other: opposite charges attract, like charges repel. **Coulomb's law** says how strongly. As a block: $F_{\text{coulombic}} \propto \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$. Here $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the two charges, $r$ is the distance between them, and $\propto$ means "is proportional to". Read two things from it, as a short list: **more charge, more force** (double either charge and the force doubles); **more distance, much less force** (the distance is squared, so doubling it cuts the force to a quarter, and tripling it cuts the force to a ninth). The equation sheet prints the law in exactly this form. It has no constant, so it cannot give a force in newtons: it is for comparing and explaining. In an atom every electron is attracted to the nucleus and repelled by every other electron. How much energy it takes to pull an electron out of an atom depends on those forces, and that is where this lesson ends up.
- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={2}>`: "An electron is 60 pm from a nucleus that holds 4 protons. Another electron is 120 pm from a nucleus that holds 8 protons. (A picometer, pm, is 10⁻¹² m.) No other electrons are near either one. How do the two forces of attraction compare?" Options: They are equal, because twice the charge makes up for twice the distance; The force on the first electron is twice as strong; The force on the second electron is twice as strong; The force on the first electron is four times as strong. Why: the second nucleus has twice the charge, which doubles the force. Its electron is twice as far away, and the distance is squared, which divides the force by 4. As a block: $\dfrac{F_2}{F_1} = \dfrac{8}{4} \times \left(\dfrac{60}{120}\right)^2 = 2 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. The second force is half the first, so the first is twice as strong. Forget to square the distance and the two effects seem to cancel (the first option). Look at charge alone and you get the third; at distance alone, the fourth. (The charges and distances are made up for this Check.)
- **Section "Shells and subshells":** the electrons in an atom cannot have just any energy. They occupy **shells** (also called energy levels), numbered 1, 2, 3 and so on. An electron in a higher-numbered shell is, on average, farther from the nucleus. Each shell is divided into **subshells** (also called sublevels), named by the shell's number and a letter: s, p, d or f. Shell 1 has one subshell, 1s. Shell 2 has two, 2s and 2p. Shell 3 has three: 3s, 3p and 3d. Shell 4 has four: 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f. Each kind of subshell holds a fixed maximum number of electrons. Give the table (four columns):

  | Subshell | Orbitals | Holds up to | First appears in |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | s | 1 | 2 electrons | shell 1 |
  | p | 3 | 6 electrons | shell 2 |
  | d | 5 | 10 electrons | shell 3 |
  | f | 7 | 14 electrons | shell 4 |

  (The "Orbitals" column is explained in the next section; say so in a clause.) So shell 1 holds 2 electrons in all, shell 2 holds 2 + 6 = 8, shell 3 holds 2 + 6 + 10 = 18, and shell 4 holds 2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32. In an atom with more than one electron, electrons in different subshells of one shell do not have quite the same energy: within a shell, s is lowest, then p, then d. The steps are large enough that the shells overlap: the 4s subshell fills before 3d. (The figure that shows the order comes at the end of the next section, because it draws each subshell as its orbitals.)
- **Section "Orbitals, briefly":** one short section, five or six sentences. This picture of shells and subshells comes from quantum mechanics, the physics of very small things. In it, each subshell is made of **orbitals**: regions around the nucleus where an electron of that subshell is likely to be found. An orbital holds at most two electrons (this limit comes from the Pauli exclusion principle). An s subshell is one orbital, a p subshell is three, a d subshell is five and an f subshell is seven, which is where the capacities 2, 6, 10 and 14 come from. Electrons repel each other, so the electrons of one subshell spread out: each takes an empty orbital of that subshell before any two share one (this is called Hund's rule). A p subshell with three electrons has one in each of its three orbitals. Give it a fourth and that electron has to share an orbital with another; topic 1.7 uses that fact. That build-up rule is the **Aufbau principle** (Aufbau is German for "building up"). - **Section "Ions":** to write the configuration of an ion, start from the atom's configuration, then change the number of electrons. The charge says how: 2+ means two electrons fewer than the atom, 1− means one more. An ion's configuration is not the atom's with a charge written beside it. Two rules, as a short list:
  - **An anion's extra electrons** go where the next electron would go: into the subshell that is being filled.
  - **A cation's lost electrons** come out of the highest-numbered shell first, because those electrons are farthest from the nucleus and held least tightly. Within that shell, p electrons leave before s electrons.

  The second rule matters most for the d-block metals. - **Section "Ionization energy and Coulomb's law":** **ionization energy** is the energy needed to remove an electron from an atom or ion in the gas phase. For silicon, as a block: $\ce{Si(g) -> Si+(g) + e-}$. This takes 787 kJ for every mole of silicon atoms. It is silicon's **first ionization energy**: the energy to remove the most loosely held electron from the neutral atom. The equation sheet has no entry for ionization energy; what it has is Coulomb's law, and that is what explains it. Three things decide how strongly an electron is held, as a short list:
  - **Nuclear charge:** the number of protons. More protons, more attraction.
  - **Distance:** the shell. An electron in a higher shell is farther from the nucleus, and the force falls with the square of the distance.
  - **Shielding:** the electrons that lie between an electron and the nucleus repel it, and so cancel part of the nucleus's pull. Core electrons shield a valence electron well: each cancels about one proton. Electrons in the same shell shield one another poorly, because they are not in between.

  What an electron feels once shielding is taken off is the **effective nuclear charge**. A rough estimate is the number of protons minus the number of electrons in the shells inside. For silicon's valence electrons that is 14 − 10, about +4. A stronger attraction means more energy to remove the electron: a larger ionization energy.
- `<KeyIdea>`: to explain how tightly an electron is held, name the levers: the nuclear charge, the distance (which shell) and the shielding by the electrons inside. A larger effective nuclear charge or a smaller distance means a stronger attraction, and a larger ionization energy.
- **Worked example "Three electrons in one silicon atom":** in a silicon atom, which takes the most energy to remove, and which the least: a 1s electron, a 2p electron or a 3p electron? Justify the order with Coulomb's law. (Note for the writer: this is the model for every explanation in the unit: the claim, then the Coulomb's-law reason, then the evidence.)
  1. **Place each electron.** Silicon is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². The 1s electron is in shell 1, the 2p electron in shell 2 and the 3p electron in shell 3, the valence shell.
  2. **Compare the distances.** Shell 1 is closest to the nucleus and shell 3 is farthest. The force falls with the square of the distance.
  3. **Compare the effective nuclear charges.** The nucleus holds 14 protons. A 1s electron has no shell inside it, so it feels nearly all of +14. A 2p electron has the two 1s electrons inside: about 14 − 2 = +12. A 3p electron has all ten core electrons inside: about 14 − 10 = +4.
  4. **Make the claim.** Both levers point the same way, so the order is 1s (most energy), then 2p, then 3p (least).
  5. **Write the answer.** "A 1s electron takes the most energy to remove and a 3p electron the least. The 1s electrons are closest to the nucleus, with no electrons inside them to shield its 14 protons. The 3p electrons are in the third shell, farthest from the nucleus, and the 10 core electrons shield them, so they feel an effective nuclear charge of only about +4. By Coulomb's law, a smaller charge at a greater distance gives a weaker attraction, so less energy is needed to remove the electron. The 2p electrons lie between the two on both counts."

A 1s electron takes more than 200 times as much energy to remove as a 3p electron, and a 2p electron about 13 times as much. - **Opening:** how firmly is a tent peg held? You find out by pulling it. One in loose sand near the surface comes out with two fingers; one driven deep into clay takes both hands. Chemists test the electrons of an atom the same way: pull one out and measure how much energy it took. Do that for every kind of electron in the atom and you have a photoelectron spectrum, the most direct look at an atom's electrons that this course offers.
- **Section "What the experiment measures":** in **photoelectron spectroscopy**, PES for short, a sample of an element is hit with light of one known, high energy: ultraviolet light or X-rays. The light knocks electrons out of the atoms, and the instrument measures how fast each one flies off. An electron that was held tightly uses up more of the light's energy in getting free and leaves slowly; one that was held loosely leaves fast. From the speed, the instrument works out how much energy it took to free that electron. That energy is the electron's **binding energy**. (Light arrives in packets called photons, and topic 3.12 deals with their energy; nothing is calculated here.) Binding energy is ionization energy (topic 1.5) under another name, for whichever electron was removed. It is reported in MJ/mol, megajoules for a mole of electrons removed; 1 MJ is 1000 kJ. Then one point that prevents a common misreading: in the picture used here, each atom that is hit loses one electron. One atom loses a 1s electron, another loses a 3p electron, and the spectrum gathers the results from an enormous number of atoms. So it shows the energy needed to remove an electron from each subshell of the **neutral atom**. It is not a record of stripping one atom electron after electron (that is topic 1.8).

  - **Two checks on a reading.** The heights add up to the number of electrons: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 2 = 14, silicon's atomic number.   One closing sentence: measured spectra follow the electron counts only roughly; the spectra you will be asked to read are drawn to the ideal, with heights in exact proportion.

- **Worked example "Explaining where a peak is":** the 3p peak is at 1.52 MJ/mol in argon and at 2.38 MJ/mol in potassium. Potassium also has a peak at 0.42 MJ/mol, lower than any peak of argon. Explain both observations with Coulomb's law. (Note for the writer: this is the model answer for the topic: the evidence, the comparison lever by lever, the Coulomb's-law reason, then the answer in sentences.)
  1. **Say what the positions mean.** A higher binding energy means more energy to remove the electron. So potassium holds its 3p electrons more tightly than argon holds its own, and the electron at 0.42 MJ/mol is the most loosely held electron in either atom.
  2. **Compare the 3p electrons, lever by lever.** Distance: third shell in both atoms. Shielding: the same ten electrons, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, lie inside the third shell in both. Nuclear charge: 19 protons in potassium, 18 in argon.
  3. **Give the reason.** Same shell, same shielding, one more proton: the effective nuclear charge on a 3p electron is about 19 − 10 = +9 in potassium and about 18 − 10 = +8 in argon. By Coulomb's law the larger charge attracts the electron more strongly, so it takes more energy to remove.
  4. **Turn to the new peak.** Potassium's configuration ends in 4s¹. That electron is in the fourth shell, farther from the nucleus than the third. All 18 of the other electrons lie inside it, so its effective nuclear charge is about 19 − 18 = +1. A small charge at a large distance is a weak attraction, and a weak attraction is a small binding energy.
  5. **Write the answer.** "Potassium's 3p peak is at a higher binding energy than argon's because potassium's nucleus has 19 protons to argon's 18, while the 3p electrons of both atoms are in the same shell and are shielded by the same 10 inner electrons. The greater effective nuclear charge attracts them more strongly, so more energy is needed to remove one. Potassium's peak at 0.42 MJ/mol is its 4s electron. It is in a shell farther from the nucleus and is shielded by 18 core electrons, so it is attracted weakly and takes little energy to remove."

- **Section "What a spectrum says about the model":** the shells and subshells of topic 1.5 are not just a bookkeeping scheme. A spectrum is the evidence for them. Look again at calcium's six binding energies, set out as one line: 390, then 42.7 and 34.0, then 4.65 and 2.90, then 0.59. Three points, as a short list:
  - **Groups mean shells.** The energies fall into four groups with wide gaps between them: from one group to the next the binding energy drops by a factor of about 5 to 9. Electrons come at a few distinct distances from the nucleus, not at every distance. Those groups are shells 1, 2, 3 and 4.
  - **Pairs mean subshells.** Inside the second group, and inside the third, there are two peaks, close together (they differ by less than a factor of 2) but separate. If the eight electrons of the second shell were all alike, the spectrum would show one peak there, standing for 8 electrons. It shows two, standing for 2 and 6. So a shell is split into subshells that differ a little in energy.
  - **Heights mean capacities.** Follow the spectra from hydrogen to calcium (the interactive diagram above has all twenty) and no peak stands for more than 6 electrons. Some peaks stop growing at 2 electrons and the others at 6, and once a peak has reached its limit the next electron starts a new peak. Those limits are the capacities of an s subshell and a p subshell.

- **Section "A table built on repeats":** line the elements up by atomic number and their properties do not drift steadily in one direction. They come round again. Lithium, element 3, is a soft metal that reacts with water; so is sodium, element 11; so is potassium, element 19. The [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/) stacks such repeats in columns, which is what "periodic" means. The cause is in the configurations of topic 1.5: lithium is 1s² 2s¹, sodium is [Ne] 3s¹ and potassium is [Ar] 4s¹. Each has one s electron outside a core with a noble gas's configuration. A row of the table begins when electrons start a new shell, and it ends at a noble gas, where that shell's s and p subshells are full (in the first row, where the shell has no p subshell, the 1s alone). Then the next element starts another shell, and the pattern of outer electrons starts over. Same outer configuration, similar properties: that is the idea the whole table is built on. - **Section "Three questions about any electron":** short, a recall of topic 1.5 turned toward rows and columns. By Coulomb's law the attraction between a nucleus and an electron grows with the charge and falls off quickly with distance. So for a valence electron ask three things, as a short list: how many protons are in the nucleus (**nuclear charge**); which shell the electron is in (**distance**); how many electrons lie between it and the nucleus (**shielding**). Core electrons shield well; electrons in the same shell shield one another poorly. Shielding and nuclear charge combine into the **effective nuclear charge**, estimated roughly as the protons minus the electrons in the shells inside: about +6 for sulfur's valence electrons (16 − 10) and about +1 for potassium's one valence electron (19 − 18). Then the two moves behind the trends in this lesson, as a two-item list:
  - **Across a period,** each element has one more proton than the last. In rows 2 and 3 every added electron goes into the same shell, where it shields the others poorly.   - **Down a group,** the effective nuclear charge, by the same rough count, stays about the same, because the added protons are offset by added core electrons. But the valence electrons are in a shell farther from the nucleus. They are pulled less hard.
- `<KeyIdea>`: every periodic trend is Coulomb's law answering the same three questions about the valence electrons: how much nuclear charge, how far away, how much shielding. Across a period the effective nuclear charge grows in the same shell. Down a group the shell gets farther out.

  - **Down a group it generally falls.** Down group 18: helium 2372, neon 2081, argon 1521, krypton 1351, xenon 1170 kJ/mol. Even a noble gas gives up an electron more easily when that electron starts farther out.

  - **Say "the first big jump", never "the largest jump".** Find it by the increase in kJ/mol from one value to the next, judged against the steps just before and after it, not by ratios. "Largest" fails too: a longer list has later jumps that are larger still (for strontium, +3074 at the core, then +3582 and +14,161 further on). Every list from which this lesson asks for the number of valence electrons includes at least the first core electron's value (the pairs of values in Check 2 are not such lists). For a group 1 element the jump is never described as "far larger than the step after it" (for cesium it is about twice), and such a list is never set without the either-or of the s block.
  - **No explanation may rest on "wants a noble-gas configuration", "wants a full octet" or "is stable".** The noble-gas configuration of an ion is the outcome. The reason is always which electron the nucleus holds weakly or strongly, and why.
  - Say that a nonmetal atom "has taken on" two or three electrons in an ionic compound. The lesson says once, in the section on charges, that a lone atom would not take the second or third and that the attraction between the ions in the solid pays for them (topic 2.3); do not go further into that.
  - Formulas in prose and in answer options are plain Unicode text (Ba₃N₂, In₂S₃), never math. A charge on an ion is written Sr²⁺, O²⁻. Charge sums are written in words or as plain text ("two 1+ charges and one 2− charge add up to zero").
  - No compound is named by rule. A compound is called "a compound of indium and sulfur" or by a name given in this brief. Do not explain how names are formed.
  - Later topics are named in plain text ("topic 2.1", "topic 2.3"); no links.
- **Opening:** some glow-in-the-dark paints and stickers go on shining for hours after the light is switched off. - **Section "Removing electrons one after another":** topic 1.7 used the first ionization energy, the energy to remove one electron from an atom. The **second ionization energy** is the energy to remove the next electron, from the 1+ ion; the third removes one more, from the 2+ ion; and so on. Each one is larger than the one before: the same nucleus now holds fewer electrons, they repel one another less, and those that remain are held more tightly. For strontium, as a short list or a sentence: first 549, second 1064, third 4138, fourth 5430 kJ/mol. Place the figure `1-8-successive-ionization-energies.svg` here. Read it with the student by the size of each increase: the second costs 515 kJ/mol more than the first, about twice as much. Then the pattern breaks: the third costs 3074 kJ/mol more than the second, nearly four times as much. After that the rise is smaller again, 1292 kJ/mol for the fourth. The first two electrons together cost 1613 kJ/mol, less than half of what the third costs alone.

- **Section "The jump marks the end of the valence electrons":** strontium is [Kr] 5s². Its first two ionization energies remove the two 5s electrons, the valence electrons. They are in the fifth shell, far from the nucleus, and the 36 core electrons shield them: by the rough count of topic 1.5 they feel about +2 (38 − 36). The third electron has to come out of the core, from the 4p subshell. That is one shell closer to the nucleus, with only 28 electrons inside it, so it feels about +10 (38 − 28). A much larger charge at a shorter distance is, by Coulomb's law, a much stronger attraction. That is the jump. The same reading works for other main-group elements: the first big jump in a list of successive ionization energies comes right after the last valence electron has gone. Then two sentences. A smaller step-up can appear inside the valence electrons, where removal moves from the p electrons to the s electrons. And a longer list shows further jumps each time a deeper shell or subshell is reached, some of them larger still; the first big jump is the one that counts the valence electrons. (Note for the writer: do not write "for every element", "always" or "the largest jump", and give no group 13 data and no longer list. The check script holds the values behind this: strontium's first twelve and sodium's first eleven, in each of which a later increase is larger than the first big jump; and boron, aluminum, gallium and indium, where the increase after the third electron is more than twice any before it, though for gallium and indium the fourth value is only about twice the third.)
- `<KeyIdea>`: for a main-group element, successive ionization energies rise step by step until the valence electrons are gone. The next electron must come from the core, closer to the nucleus and far less shielded, and it costs much more. Count the electrons removed before that first big jump: that is the number of valence electrons.

- **Section "From valence electrons to a charge":** two cases, as a two-item list, then the table.
  - **Metals at the left of the table** (groups 1 and 2, and the metals of group 13) have one, two or three valence electrons, far from the nucleus and well shielded. Each of those can be removed at a moderate cost; one more would have to come out of the core, at a much higher cost. So in an ionic compound the atom has usually lost exactly its valence electrons, and its charge is their number: 1+, 2+ or 3+.
  - **Nonmetals at the right** (groups 15, 16 and 17) have five, six or seven valence electrons, which feel a large effective nuclear charge. They are hard to remove: bromine's first ionization energy, 1140 kJ/mol, is about twice strontium's 549. And an extra electron that enters the same shell is attracted by that same large charge (the electron affinity of topic 1.7). A lone atom would not take a second or third electron; in the solid, the attraction between the ions pays for them (topic 2.3). The valence shell's s and p subshells hold eight electrons in all. In an ionic compound the atom has taken on electrons until they are full: three, two or one more, for a charge of 3−, 2− or 1−. There the count stops, because the next electron would have to start a new shell, far from the nucleus and shielded by all the others. The same holds for a metalloid such as tellurium, which this lesson uses as Te²⁻.

  The table (four columns):

  | Group | Valence electrons | Usual charge | Example |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | 1 | 1+ | K⁺ |
  | 2 | 2 | 2+ | Sr²⁺ |
  | 13 (metals) | 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
  | 15 (nonmetals) | 5 | 3− | P³⁻ |
  | 16 | 6 | 2− | O²⁻ |
  | 17 | 7 | 1− | Br⁻ |

  Under the table: for these groups the number of valence electrons is the last digit of the group number, and the charge of a nonmetal, or of a metalloid such as tellurium, is the group number minus 18. Each ion in the table has ended up with the electron count of a noble gas: K⁺ and P³⁻ with argon's 18, Sr²⁺ and Br⁻ with krypton's 36, Al³⁺ and O²⁻ with neon's 10. That is the outcome, not the reason. The reason is the jump: a nucleus holds its core electrons far too strongly to lose them in a reaction, and it has almost no hold on an electron that would have to begin a new shell. One closing sentence on the limits: the rule is for these main-group columns; most metals of the d block, and a few heavy metals such as thallium, tin and lead, have more than one common charge, which position alone does not give, and this lesson leaves them out.
- **Worked example "From ionization energies to a formula":** the electron sources of some particle accelerators are coated with a compound of tellurium and element X, a very soft metal from the s block of the periodic table. Light striking the coating knocks electrons out of it. The first three ionization energies of X are 376, 2234 and 3203 kJ/mol. What charge does X have in the compound? Justify the answer, and give the compound's formula.
  1. **Find the first big jump.** Look at the increases: the second is 1858 kJ/mol above the first, and the third is 969 kJ/mol above the second. An s-block metal has one valence electron or two (topic 1.5). With two, the first increase would be the small one and the second the big one, as for strontium (515, then 3074). Here it is the other way round: the big jump comes between the first and the second.
  2. **Count the valence electrons.** One electron comes off before the jump, so X has one valence electron. X is in group 1.
  3. **State the charge, with the evidence.** In its compounds X is X⁺. Removing one electron costs 376 kJ/mol; removing a second would cost 2234 kJ/mol, nearly six times as much.
  4. **Give the Coulomb's-law reason.** The first electron is the valence electron: alone in the outer shell, shielded by every other electron in the atom, feeling an effective nuclear charge of about +1. The second would come from the core, a shell closer to the nucleus and far less shielded, so the nucleus attracts it much more strongly.
  5. **Find the other ion.** Tellurium is in group 16, with six valence electrons. It takes on two more: Te²⁻, with 52 + 2 = 54 electrons.
  6. **Balance the charges.** Each X⁺ carries 1+ and each Te²⁻ carries 2−. Two X⁺ ions for every Te²⁻ ion add up to zero. The formula is X₂Te.
  7. **Name the element.** The data are cesium's, the group 1 metal of period 6. Cesium is [Xe] 6s¹: its 6s electron feels about +1 (55 − 54), and the 5p electron that would go next feels about +9 (55 − 46) from one shell closer. The compound is Cs₂Te, cesium telluride.

  (Notes for the writer: keep "from the s block" in the question. Without it three values cannot decide, because a group 13 metal's first three have the same shape, a large increase and then a smaller one. Older tables print about 3400 kJ/mol for the third value; the conclusion is the same. Cesium is outside the explorer of topic 1.7, which stops at period 5, so do not send the student there to look up 376 kJ/mol.)
- **Check 2**, `<Check answer={4}>`: "Here are the first and second ionization energies of four elements, in kJ/mol. Beryllium: 900 and 1757. Strontium: 549 and 1064. Barium: 503 and 965. Why: for potassium the second is about 7.3 times the first. For beryllium, strontium and barium it is about twice the first (2.0, 1.9 and 1.9 times). The reason is where the second electron comes from. Beryllium, strontium and barium are in group 2: each has two valence electrons, so its second electron is still a valence electron. Potassium, [Ar] 4s¹, is in group 1 and has only one. Its second electron has to come from the core, from the 3p subshell, a shell closer to the nucleus and much less shielded. - **Section "Balancing the charges":** an ionic compound has no charge overall, so the positive and negative charges in it add up to zero. Its formula gives the smallest whole-number ratio of ions that does that (the formula unit of topic 1.3), with the metal written first and no charges shown. Three cases, as a short list:
  - **Equal charges, one of each.** Strontium and oxygen: Sr²⁺ and O²⁻. The formula is SrO.
  - **Unequal charges, where one is a multiple of the other.** Lithium and nitrogen: Li⁺ and N³⁻. Three 1+ charges balance one 3− charge. The formula is Li₃N.
  - **Unequal charges, where neither is a multiple of the other.** Barium and nitrogen: Ba²⁺ and N³⁻. The smallest total that both 2 and 3 divide is 6. Three Ba²⁺ ions carry 6+ and two N³⁻ ions carry 6−. The formula is Ba₃N₂.

  One sentence: always finish by adding the charges up; if the total is not zero, the formula is wrong.
- **Check 3**, `<Check answer={2}>`: "Using the usual charge of each element, what is the formula of a compound of indium and sulfur?" Options (plain text): InS; In₂S₃; In₃S₂; InS₃. Why: indium is a metal in group 13, with three valence electrons, so its usual ion is In³⁺. Sulfur is in group 16 and forms S²⁻. The smallest total that both 3 and 2 divide is 6: two In³⁺ ions carry 6+ and three S²⁻ ions carry 6−, so the formula is In₂S₃. Check the others by adding up: InS leaves 1+ over, InS₃ leaves 3− over, and In₃S₂, the right numbers on the wrong ions, leaves 5+ over. (Note for the writer: keep the words "using the usual charge of each element" and "a compound", not "the compound", in the question. No setting; lesson 1.2 already has indium in a touchscreen.)
- **Section "One column, one kind of formula":** the charge comes from the number of valence electrons, and the main-group elements of one column have the same number (helium, with two, is the one exception). So the elements of one column form compounds with the same pattern of formula. Take group 16: oxygen, sulfur, selenium and tellurium each have six valence electrons and each form a 2− ion. With lithium they give Li₂O, Li₂S, Li₂Se and Li₂Te. With strontium they give SrO, SrS, SrSe and SrTe. Place the figure `1-8-one-column-like-compounds.svg` here. It works for a column of metals too: magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium form MgO, CaO, SrO and BaO. Chemists call such compounds analogous. One sentence of caution: elements in a column tend to do this; it is a strong pattern, not a law without exceptions.

- **Check 4**, `<Check answer={1}>`: "The compound of barium and nitrogen is Ba₃N₂. Which pair of elements would you expect to form a compound whose formula has the same pattern, three of the first to two of the second?" Options: Barium and phosphorus; Sodium and phosphorus; Magnesium and sulfur; Aluminum and fluorine. Why: a formula's pattern comes from the two charges, and the charges come from the two columns. Phosphorus is in nitrogen's column, group 15, so it forms a 3− ion as nitrogen does. Barium, in group 2, is 2+ as before. The charges are the ones in Ba₃N₂, and so is the pattern: Ba₃P₂. - **Section "Which elements form ionic compounds":** short, five or six sentences. Whether two elements bond, and how, comes down to how strongly each nucleus attracts valence electrons: its own, and the other atom's. A strontium atom holds its two valence electrons weakly: its first ionization energy is 549 kJ/mol and its electronegativity 1.0. An oxygen atom holds its own valence electrons tightly, with a first ionization energy of 1314 kJ/mol, more than twice strontium's, and attracts other electrons strongly, with an electronegativity of 3.4. Bring the two elements together and strontium's valence electrons end up on oxygen. The Sr²⁺ and O²⁻ ions that result attract each other, and that attraction holds the solid together (topic 2.3). So a metal from the left of the table and a nonmetal from the right generally form an ionic compound. Two nonmetals both hold their electrons tightly, so neither loses any: they share (topic 2.1, which uses electronegativity to sort out the kinds of bond).
- **Trap:** **Counting to the jump one electron too far** (a jump between the second and third ionization energies means two valence electrons, not three: count the electrons removed before the jump). **Looking for the largest number in place of the first big jump** (every ionization energy is larger than the one before; look at how much each rises over the one before it, and find the first rise that stands out from the steps beside it). **Explaining a charge with "it wants a noble-gas configuration"** (that is the outcome: the reason is that core electrons are held far too strongly to lose, and an electron that would start a new shell is hardly attracted at all). **Putting the right numbers on the wrong ions** (In₃S₂ in place of In₂S₃: add up the charges to check). **Leaving a formula unreduced** (Sr₂O₂ balances, but the formula is the smallest ratio, SrO). **Using the column rule on a transition metal** (it gives the charge for main-group columns only).
- **Key points:** each successive ionization energy is larger than the one before, and for a main-group element the first big jump comes when the valence electrons are gone and the next electron must come from the core; the number of electrons removed before that first big jump is the number of valence electrons, which for these groups (1, 2 and 13 to 17) is the last digit of the group number; in ionic compounds the metals of groups 1, 2 and 13 have usually lost their valence electrons, giving charges of 1+, 2+ and 3+, and the nonmetals of groups 15, 16 and 17 have gained enough to fill the valence shell's s and p subshells, giving 3−, 2− and 1−; the formula of an ionic compound is the smallest whole-number ratio of ions whose charges add up to zero; the main-group elements of one column have the same number of valence electrons, so they tend to form compounds with the same pattern of formula; a metal that holds its valence electrons weakly and a nonmetal that attracts electrons strongly generally form an ionic compound.
