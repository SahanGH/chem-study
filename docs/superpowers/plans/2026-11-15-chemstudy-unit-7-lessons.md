## Rules for writing a lesson (Tasks 2–9, 11–14)

Read `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-4-ideal-gas-law.mdx` first: it is the model for a lesson with calculations (display math with `aligned`, numeric Checks, a "why" in block form). `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-8-representations-of-solutions.mdx` is the model for particle drawings.

**The file.** `src/content/lessons/unit-7/<file name in the task>`. The name must be `<unit>-<number>-<slug>.mdx` matching the topic, or the build fails. Front matter is exactly three fields:

```mdx
---
topic: "7.1"
minutes: 12
summary: One sentence, 20 to 200 characters, saying what the lesson teaches.
---
```

Put the summary in double quotes if it contains a colon. The title comes from `src/data/units.ts`. Do not write an `# H1`.

**The seven parts, in order.** The build fails if a lesson lacks a worked example, a trap box, key points, practice-next or three checks.

1. Opening: two or three sentences, no heading, starting from something concrete.
2. Explanation: `##` sections, one idea each. At least one `<KeyIdea>`.
3. `<Worked title="...">` with numbered steps.
4. `<Check>` questions spread through the lesson, each placed right after the idea it tests. Never all at the end.
5. `<Trap>`: the specific mistakes for this topic, as a short list with the mistake in bold.
6. `<KeyPoints>`: four to six bullets. This is the review sheet, so each bullet must stand on its own.
7. `<PracticeNext />` as the last line, with no attributes.

**Markup.**

- Math is KaTeX inside `$...$` or a `$$` block. Formulas and equations use `\ce{...}`: `$\ce{N2O4}$`, `$\ce{Ca^2+}$`. The equilibrium arrow is `<=>` inside `\ce{}`: `$\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$`. Units in math are upright: `$\text{mol/L}$`, `$\text{atm}$`. In prose, plain Unicode is fine for a simple formula (N₂O₄, SO₄²⁻) or symbol (K, Q, K_sp written as `$K_{sp}$`) when it needs no math.
- A choice check: `<Check answer={2}>`, the question, a blank line, a `-` list of options, a blank line, then `<div slot="why">...</div>`. `answer` is the 1-based number of the correct option.
- A numeric check: `<Check numeric="0.250">` (add `unit="..."` when there is one). **The number is text in quotes**, so its trailing zeros (significant figures) survive. A very small or large answer is written in e-notation, `numeric="1.3e-5"`. The student's answer is accepted within 2 percent.
- A figure: `<Figure src="/figures/<name>.svg" alt="..." caption="..." />`.
- Inside a block (`<Worked>`, `<Trap>`, `<KeyPoints>`, `<KeyIdea>`), leave a blank line after the opening tag and before the closing tag.
- Excluded material goes in a paragraph that begins "**Beyond the exam:**". There is no `<Beyond>` block in lessons.
- Refer to another lesson by topic number in plain text ("topic 5.2", "topic 7.3"), with no links.
- **Phone width.** Math in a sentence cannot wrap, so every equation and calculation goes on its own line (`$$ ... $$` with blank lines around it), split with `aligned` when long, including inside a Check's `<div slot="why">`. Keep each line to about 28 characters of rendered math; a long substitution fraction carries numbers only, with the units stated in words beside it. Tables have at most five columns and short cells; an ICE table has a label column and at most three species. No lesson may scroll sideways at 320 px with every answer open.
- **Astro drops the space between text and an inline element on the next line.** Keep an inline tag on the same line as the word before it.

**Voice.** A teacher talking, not a textbook. Start from something concrete. Short sentences. No "in this section we will". Say "you". Explain why before how. One new term at a time, in bold the first time, defined in the same sentence.

**Chemistry.**

- Teach what the brief's "Framework" line lists, and nothing the exam excludes unless the brief says to, in a "Beyond the exam" paragraph.
- Use the brief's numbers exactly. They were checked when this plan was written. Recompute each as you write it; if one disagrees, stop and say so; do not quietly change it.
- Do not add a worked example or a check that the brief does not list. Do not drop one.
- **A Check must not be answered word for word by the sentence, caption or key idea just above it.** If the brief places a Check right after a sentence that states its answer, keep the Check, word the correct option differently, and report it.
- K has no units. Concentrations in K_c are in mol/L, pressures in K_p in atm.
- Never take an example, a data table or a sentence from a textbook.

**Static figures.** Hand-written SVG in `public/figures/`, in the style of Unit 3's (`3-8-concentration-boxes.svg` for particles, `3-4-pressure-volume.svg` for graphs): a `viewBox` 480 wide (taller where a figure needs it), a `#FDFCF9` background rectangle, `font-family="system-ui, sans-serif" font-size="18"`, ink `#26231F`, secondary `#6B655D`, accent `#4F706C`, light accent fill `#E3EAE9`.

- Two things in one figure that must be told apart differ in shape, shading or dash pattern as well as color. Label directly; do not rely on a legend alone.
- Particle drawings keep the counts the brief gives exactly, so every Q and K read from them is right. Cations dark, anions pale, as in Unit 3.
- Plot curves and place points from computed coordinates, not by eye.
- No line may pass behind or touch a particle or label it does not belong to.
- The alt text says what the figure shows in words and must be true of the figure as drawn, including counts.
- **Render every figure and look at it** before reporting: `node <scratchpad>/unit3/svg-shot.cjs <a new output folder> 390 <name.svg>`. Fix overlaps and any text that would be under 12 px when the figure is 320 px wide.

### Task 1: The approach-to-equilibrium explorer

**Files:**
- Create: `src/lib/equilibrium-approach.ts`, `src/components/diagrams/EquilibriumApproach.svelte`, `src/components/lesson/EquilibriumApproach.astro`
- Modify: `src/styles/diagram.css`, `src/pages/[unit]/[topic].astro` (`EquilibriumApproach` in `blocks`)
- Test: `tests/unit/equilibrium-approach.test.ts`, `tests/e2e/diagram-equilibrium-approach.spec.ts`

**The model.** A made-up reversible reaction A ⇌ B in a closed container. Each direction is first order, as in topic 5.2: forward rate = k_f[A], reverse rate = k_r[B]. The total concentration is 1.00 M. Lesson 7.1 comes before K is defined (topic 7.3), so the student chooses rate constants, not K.

**Contract: `src/lib/equilibrium-approach.ts`** (no DOM, so it is tested directly).

- `RATE_CONSTANTS`: three pairs, in s⁻¹, each with an `id`: `forward-favored` (k_f = 0.8, k_r = 0.2), `even` (0.5, 0.5), `reverse-favored` (0.2, 0.8). Each pair sums to 1 s⁻¹, so every choice settles on the same time scale. A comment says so.
- `STARTS`: `only-a` ([A]₀ = 1.00 M), `half` (0.50 M of each), `only-b` ([A]₀ = 0).
- `TIME = { min: 0, max: 8, step: 0.25, start: 1 }` in s.
- `concentrationA(pair, start, t)`: the exact solution, [A] = [A]_eq + ([A]₀ − [A]_eq) e^{−(k_f + k_r)t}, with [A]_eq = k_r/(k_f + k_r) × 1.00 M. Used to draw the curves.
- `shownState(pair, start, t)`: **everything the readout shows, in whole numbers**, so that the numbers a student reads agree with each other exactly:
  - `a`: [A] in hundredths of a mole per liter, rounded half up from `concentrationA`;
  - `b`: 100 − `a` (so the two always add to 1.00 M);
  - `forward`: k_f × `a`, in thousandths of M/s (k_f in tenths × `a`, exact);
  - `reverse`: k_r × `b`, likewise;
  - `direction`: `'forward'` if `forward` > `reverse`, `'reverse'` if less, `'equal'` if equal.
- `curvePoints(pair, start)`: about 160 points across the time axis, for the concentrations and for the rates.
- Readout sentences (a true minus sign is never needed; decimals with a leading zero):
  - forward: "At 1 s: [A] = 0.49 M and [B] = 0.51 M. Forward rate 0.392 M/s, reverse rate 0.102 M/s: A is turning into B faster than B turns back."
  - reverse: "At 1 s: [A] = 0.13 M and [B] = 0.87 M. Forward rate 0.104 M/s, reverse rate 0.174 M/s: B is turning into A faster than A turns back."
  - equal: "At 8 s: [A] = 0.20 M and [B] = 0.80 M. Forward rate 0.160 M/s, reverse rate 0.160 M/s: the rates are equal, so the concentrations have stopped changing. Both reactions are still running."
  - Times read "0 s", "0.25 s", "1 s", "2.5 s" (no trailing zeros).
- Every input outside its range, or not on a slider step, throws an error that names the value and the range.

The suggested skill is explaining how a picture at the level of particles accounts for what is seen at the level of the whole sample, so the lesson says plainly that the particles react in both directions all the time and that "direction" belongs to the whole mixture. - **Section "Two rates, one net change":** at every moment a reversible reaction has two rates: the rate of the forward reaction and the rate of the reverse reaction. A measurement of a concentration does not show either one by itself. What it shows is the difference between them, the **net** change. Give the three cases as a two-column table (Comparison | What you observe): forward rate greater than reverse rate | net conversion of reactants to products: reactant concentrations fall, product concentrations rise; reverse rate greater than forward rate | net conversion of products to reactants: product concentrations fall, reactant concentrations rise; the two rates equal | no net change: equilibrium. - **Opening:** every equilibrium constant in a data table began as an experiment. - **Section "Measure, then substitute":** the routine, as a numbered list: (1) write the expression for K from the balanced equation (topic 7.3); (2) get every quantity into the right form: mol/L for K_c, so divide moles by the volume of the container, or atm for K_p; (3) substitute the **equilibrium** values, raising each to its coefficient; (4) report K with no unit and the right number of significant figures. - **Worked example "K_c from an equilibrium mixture":** nitrogen trifluoride, NF₃, is a gas the electronics industry uses to clean its equipment. A 5.00 L nickel reactor held at a certain high temperature is found to contain, at equilibrium, 0.200 mol of NF₃, 0.0500 mol of N₂ and 0.150 mol of F₂. Calculate K_c. (The amounts are made up for this example.)
  1. **Write the expression.** As a block: $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{N2}][\ce{F2}]^3}{[\ce{NF3}]^2}$.
  2. **Change moles into mol/L.** Divide each amount by 5.00 L: [NF₃] = 0.200 mol ÷ 5.00 L = 0.0400 M; [N₂] = 0.0500 mol ÷ 5.00 L = 0.0100 M; [F₂] = 0.150 mol ÷ 5.00 L = 0.0300 M.

  4. **Report it.** K_c = 1.69 × 10⁻⁴, with no unit, to three significant figures like the data.
  5. **See what the two slips would have cost.** Leave the cube off [F₂] and the answer comes out as 0.19, about 1100 times too large. Put in the moles without dividing by the volume and it comes out as 4.22 × 10⁻³, 25 times too large: the volumes do not cancel in this expression.

- **Section "First, which way? Compare Q with K":** before any algebra, find out which way the mixture will go. Work out Q from the concentrations as they are now (topic 7.3) and set it beside K.
  - **Q less than K:** the ratio of products to reactants is too small for equilibrium. The reaction runs toward products, using up reactants, until Q has risen to K.
  - **Q greater than K:** too much product for equilibrium. The reaction runs toward reactants until Q has fallen to K.
  - **Q equal to K:** the mixture is at equilibrium already. Both directions are still running, at the same rate, and the amounts stay as they are.

  Say why: K is the one value of the ratio at which the forward and reverse rates balance (topics 7.2 and 7.3), and Q always moves toward it. Two quick cases: a mixture with no products at all has Q = 0, so it can only go forward; a mixture with no reactants can only go backward. Place the figure `7-7-q-and-k.svg` here. - **Worked example "The sealed cell: a perfect square":** the cell in the opening holds 0.220 M CS₂ and 0.220 M CO₂ and no COS. $\ce{CS2(g) + CO2(g) <=> 2COS(g)}$ (block), $K_c = 4.30$ at the cell's temperature. Find the three concentrations at equilibrium.
  1. **Which way?** There is no COS, so Q = 0, less than K. The reaction runs forward.
  2. **Set up the table.**

     | | CS₂ | CO₂ | COS |
     |---|---|---|---|
     | Initial (M) | 0.220 | 0.220 | 0 |
     | Change (M) | −x | −x | +2x |
     | Equilibrium (M) | 0.220 − x | 0.220 − x | 2x |

  3. **Put the equilibrium row into K.** As a block: $K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{COS}]^2}{[\ce{CS2}][\ce{CO2}]} = \dfrac{(2x)^2}{(0.220 - x)^2} = 4.30$.

  - **Leave x out only where it is added to or subtracted from a much larger number.** Never drop an x that stands alone, such as the x² on top.

- **Section "Counting gives Q":** each shape in a particle drawing is one particle. Count molecules, not atoms: two circles joined together are one molecule. The lesson's reaction, as a block: $\ce{X2(g) + Y2(g) <=> 2XY(g)}$, with $Q = \dfrac{[\ce{XY}]^2}{[\ce{X2}][\ce{Y2}]}$. - **Worked example "Finding the equilibrium box":** at the same temperature (K = 16), a box holds 3 X₂, 6 Y₂ and 4 XY. Is it at equilibrium? If not, what will it hold when it is?
  1. **Count.** 3 X₂, 6 Y₂, 4 XY: molecules, not atoms.
  2. **Find Q.** As a block: $Q = \dfrac{4^2}{(3)(6)} = 0.89$.
  3. **Compare.** 0.89 is less than 16, so the box is not at equilibrium, and the reaction runs forward: X₂ and Y₂ turn into XY.
  4. **Step forward, one event at a time.** Each event uses one X₂ and one Y₂ and makes two XY. Give the table (five columns):

     | | X₂ | Y₂ | XY | Q |
     |---|---|---|---|---|
     | Start | 3 | 6 | 4 | 0.89 |
     | After 1 event | 2 | 5 | 6 | 3.6 |
     | After 2 events | 1 | 4 | 8 | 16 |

  5. **Stop when Q = K.** After two events Q = 8²/((1)(4)) = 16. The equilibrium box holds 1 X₂, 4 Y₂ and 8 XY. (A third event would use up the last X₂ and overshoot.)
  6. **Check the atoms.** X: 2(3) + 4 = 10 at the start, and 2(1) + 8 = 10 at equilibrium. Y: 2(6) + 4 = 16 at the start, and 2(4) + 8 = 16 at equilibrium. Nothing made, nothing lost.

  After the example, two sentences: a real sample holds an enormous number of molecules, so its equilibrium does not have to land on whole numbers; drawings are built so that it does. With real concentrations you would use the table with x from topic 7.7.
- **Check 2**, `<Check numeric="8" unit="molecules">`: "At the same temperature (K = 16), a box starts with 12 XY molecules and nothing else. How many XY molecules does it hold at equilibrium?" Why: with no X₂ or Y₂ the reaction can only run backward. Each reverse event turns two XY into one X₂ and one Y₂. After one event the box holds 1 X₂, 1 Y₂ and 10 XY, and Q = 10²/((1)(1)) = 100, still above 16. After two events it holds 2 X₂, 2 Y₂ and 8 XY, and, as a block, $Q = \dfrac{8^2}{(2)(2)} = 16 = K$. - **Section "When the two sides have different numbers of particles":** counts go straight into Q only when the volume cancels, and it cancels only when the equation has the same number of particles on each side. - **Worked example "Extra NO₂ goes in":** the mixture above is in a cylinder with its piston locked, so the volume is fixed, and the temperature is held constant. NO₂ is injected, which raises [NO₂] from 0.175 M to 0.265 M. Which way does the reaction run?

**Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "7.12"`, `minutes: 13`, summary: "Why a salt is less soluble in a solution that already holds one of its ions, how to calculate by how much, and how chemists use the effect in the lab." (No colon in the summary.)
- **Framework (7.12.A, skill 2.F):** a salt dissolves less in a solution that already contains one of its own ions than it does in pure water. This common-ion effect can be explained without numbers by Le Châtelier's principle, and it can be calculated from K_sp. The learning objective asks for the solubility of the salt, the value of K_sp, or both, given the concentration of the common ion. The suggested skill is explaining how a change to an experimental procedure changes the result, so the lesson ends in the lab. K_sp is not on the equation sheet. The small-x approximation and its 5 percent check were taught in topic 7.7; this lesson gives a one-line reminder and uses them.
- **Values:** every K_sp is at 25 °C, from the table in the plan (CRC Handbook values). Calculations use sulfates, iodates and fluorides only. Molar masses: Pb 207.2 g/mol, Ca(IO₃)₂ 389.88 g/mol.
- **Calculated is not measured.** A solubility worked out from K_sp is called calculated. Where a problem starts from a solubility, word it as "suppose a measurement finds ...": the given was chosen to agree with the K_sp table.
- **Phone width:** every dissolving equation and every calculation goes on its own line as a block equation, also inside a Check's "why"; split a long one with `aligned` (the two-part mass calculations of the last worked example, for one) so it fits 320 px.
- **Opening:** a lab has to find out how much lead is dissolved in a sample of waste water. One way is to add sulfate ions: the lead comes out of the water as a white solid, lead(II) sulfate (a solid that forms out of a solution is a **precipitate**; topic 4.7), which can be filtered off, dried and weighed. The chemist doing it adds more sulfate than the lead can use. That is not carelessness. The extra sulfate pushes lead out of the solution that would otherwise have stayed in it, and this lesson shows how.
- **Section "An ion that is already there":** take a saturated solution of lead(II) sulfate, as a block equation: $\ce{PbSO4(s) <=> Pb^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}$, with K_sp = [Pb²⁺][SO₄²⁻] = 2.53 × 10⁻⁸ (block equation). In pure water each mole that dissolves gives one mole of each ion, so (topic 7.11) the calculated molar solubility is $s = \sqrt{2.53 \times 10^{-8}} = 1.59 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}$ (block equation). Now dissolve the same solid in a solution of sodium sulfate instead of in pure water. The water already holds sulfate ions before any lead(II) sulfate has dissolved. Sulfate is a **common ion**: an ion that the dissolving salt and the solution have in common. The result: less lead(II) sulfate dissolves than in pure water. This is the **common-ion effect**, and it works for either ion: lead(II) nitrate solution, which supplies Pb²⁺, lowers the solubility of PbSO₄ too. A salt with no ion in common, such as sodium nitrate, does not have this effect. What has not changed is K_sp. It is a constant for the salt at that temperature, whatever else is in the water. Topic 7.11 called K_sp a constant and solubility an amount that K_sp fixes for pure water; here the water is not pure, and the amount is different. Place the figure here.

- **Section "Two ways to see why":** first, without numbers. A saturated solution is an equilibrium, so Le Châtelier's principle (topic 7.9) applies. Sulfate is a product of the dissolving equation. Add a product and the equilibrium shifts toward reactants, and here the reactant is the solid: Pb²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions come out of solution as solid PbSO₄. Less lead is left dissolved, which is to say the salt is less soluble. Second, with numbers (topic 7.10). Take the saturated solution in pure water, where [Pb²⁺] = [SO₄²⁻] = 1.59 × 10⁻⁴ M, and dissolve enough sodium sulfate in it to raise [SO₄²⁻] to 5.00 × 10⁻³ M. At that moment, as a block equation: $Q = (1.59 \times 10^{-4})(5.00 \times 10^{-3}) = 7.95 \times 10^{-7}$. That is about 30 times K_sp (2.53 × 10⁻⁸). Q > K_sp, so the reaction runs toward the solid: PbSO₄ precipitates, and [Pb²⁺] falls until Q is back down to K_sp. The two explanations are one explanation told twice; the second gives a number.
- `<KeyIdea>`: a common ion lowers a salt's solubility. It does not change K_sp. The ion concentrations still multiply out to the same K_sp, so when one of them is large the other must be small.
- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={3}>`: "A saturated solution of lead(II) iodide, PbI₂, is filtered, so that it is clear and holds no solid. Which addition makes yellow solid lead(II) iodide appear in it?" Options: A little distilled water; A few crystals of potassium nitrate, KNO₃; A few crystals of potassium iodide, KI; More of the same saturated solution. Why: potassium iodide dissolves and adds I⁻, an ion that lead(II) iodide also gives. For PbI₂(s) ⇌ Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) (block equation), Q = [Pb²⁺][I⁻]² rises above K_sp, so the reaction runs toward the solid and lead(II) iodide precipitates. Potassium nitrate has no ion in common with it. Water lowers both ion concentrations, so Q falls below K_sp. More of the same solution has the same concentrations, so Q does not change. (No calculation is made with this salt here.)
- **Section "How much less soluble?":** the calculation is the one from topic 7.11 with one difference: the common ion does not start at zero. Set it out as a table with a row for the start, a row for the change and a row for equilibrium, as in topic 7.7. The one-line reminder from topic 7.7: when the amount that dissolves is tiny beside the number it is added to, leave it out of the sum, solve, and then check that it is under 5 percent of that number, stating the percentage. The unknown is called s throughout this lesson, never x.
- **Worked example "Calcium iodate in an iodate solution":** calculate the molar solubility of calcium iodate, Ca(IO₃)₂ (K_sp = 6.47 × 10⁻⁶), in 0.200 M potassium iodate, KIO₃.
  1. **Write the equation and the expression.** Ca(IO₃)₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 IO₃⁻(aq) and K_sp = [Ca²⁺][IO₃⁻]² (block equations).
  2. **Set up the table.** Potassium iodate is soluble, so before any calcium iodate dissolves [IO₃⁻] = 0.200 M and [Ca²⁺] = 0. Let s be the molar solubility: s mol/L of Ca(IO₃)₂ dissolves, giving s of Ca²⁺ and 2s of IO₃⁻. (Three columns; the solid is not in the table.)

     | | [Ca²⁺] | [IO₃⁻] |
     |---|---|---|
     | Start | 0 | 0.200 |
     | Change | +s | +2s |
     | Equilibrium | s | 0.200 + 2s |

  3. **Substitute.** As a block equation: $K_{sp} = (s)(0.200 + 2s)^2 = 6.47 \times 10^{-6}$.
  4. **Approximate.** The salt is only slightly soluble and the iodate already there holds it back further, so 2s should be tiny beside 0.200. Leave it out of the sum, as a block equation: $(s)(0.200)^2 = 6.47 \times 10^{-6}$, so $s = \dfrac{6.47 \times 10^{-6}}{0.0400} = 1.62 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}$.
  5. **Check the approximation.** 2s = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M, and 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.200 = 0.16 percent. That is far under 5 percent, so the approximation holds.
  6. **Read the result.** A liter of this solution dissolves 1.62 × 10⁻⁴ mol of calcium iodate, about 0.063 g. It then holds 1.62 × 10⁻⁴ M Ca²⁺, and its iodate concentration is still 0.200 M to three significant figures: nearly every iodate ion in it came from the potassium iodate. That iodate went in as 0.200 M, not 0.400 M: the 2 in the equation belongs to the iodate that comes from the calcium iodate (the 2s), and the exponent 2 applies to the whole iodate concentration. (Do not close this example with a comparison to pure water.)
- **Check 2**, `<Check numeric="8.50e-5" unit="M">`: "Strontium fluoride, SrF₂, has K_sp = 4.33 × 10⁻⁹ at 25 °C. Calculate its molar solubility in 0.150 M strontium nitrate, Sr(NO₃)₂." Why: SrF₂(s) ⇌ Sr²⁺(aq) + 2 F⁻(aq) (block equation). This time the common ion is the cation. At equilibrium [Sr²⁺] = 0.150 + s and [F⁻] = 2s. In block form: $K_{sp} = (0.150 + s)(2s)^2 \approx (0.150)(4s^2)$, so $s = \sqrt{\dfrac{4.33 \times 10^{-9}}{4 \times 0.150}} = 8.50 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}$. Check: 8.50 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.150 = 0.057 percent, far under 5 percent. - **Check 3**, `<Check numeric="3.17e-8">`: "Suppose a measurement finds that the molar solubility of silver iodate, AgIO₃, at 25 °C in 0.0160 M potassium iodate, KIO₃, is 1.98 × 10⁻⁶ M. What value of K_sp does that give?" Why: AgIO₃(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + IO₃⁻(aq) (block equation). At equilibrium [Ag⁺] = 1.98 × 10⁻⁶ M, all of it from the silver iodate, and [IO₃⁻] = 0.0160 + 1.98 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0160 M. In block form: $K_{sp} = (1.98 \times 10^{-6})(0.0160) = 3.17 \times 10^{-8}$. - **Section "The common ion in the lab":** back to the opening. When an ion is to be collected as a solid and weighed, every bit of it that stays dissolved is an error in the result. The common-ion effect is the chemist's tool for leaving less behind: the other ion of the solid is added in excess, and the extra is a common ion. The question to be able to answer about any such procedure is the one the worked example asks: if a step is changed, what happens to the result, and why? (Keep rinsing out of this section's text: Check 4 is about rinsing, and the student should have to reason it out.)
- **Worked example "Just enough sulfate, or extra?":** the waste-water sample of the opening holds 0.350 g of dissolved lead as Pb²⁺. The lead is precipitated as PbSO₄ (K_sp = 2.53 × 10⁻⁸), and in each case the final volume of the mixture is 200.0 mL. Procedure A adds exactly as many moles of sulfate as there are moles of lead. Procedure B adds extra sodium sulfate (about twice what the lead needs), so that [SO₄²⁻] = 0.0100 M once the solid has formed. How much lead is still dissolved, and so never reaches the balance, in each?
  1. **Procedure A: the concentration of lead left.** With equal moles of the two ions, whatever stays dissolved has [Pb²⁺] = [SO₄²⁻], just as in pure water. As a block equation: $[\ce{Pb^2+}] = \sqrt{2.53 \times 10^{-8}} = 1.59 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}$.
  2. **Procedure A: the mass of lead left.** As a block equation: $(1.59 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L})(0.2000\ \text{L}) = 3.18 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol}$, and $(3.18 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol})(207.2\ \text{g/mol}) = 6.59 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{g}$, which is 6.59 mg.
  3. **Procedure B: the concentration of lead left.** The sulfate concentration at equilibrium is given, so no approximation is needed. As a block equation: $[\ce{Pb^2+}] = \dfrac{K_{sp}}{[\ce{SO4^2-}]} = \dfrac{2.53 \times 10^{-8}}{0.0100} = 2.53 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}$.
  4. **Procedure B: the mass of lead left.** As a block equation: $(2.53 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{mol/L})(0.2000\ \text{L}) = 5.06 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol}$, and $(5.06 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol})(207.2\ \text{g/mol}) = 1.05 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{g}$, which is 0.105 mg.
  5. **Say what the change of procedure does to the result.** Lead that stays dissolved goes through the filter and is never weighed. Procedure A loses 6.59 mg of the 350 mg of lead, so the lead it reports is about 2 percent too low. Procedure B loses 0.105 mg, about one sixtieth as much, an error of 0.03 percent. Adding excess sulfate does not change K_sp; it changes how much lead K_sp allows to stay dissolved.
- **Check 4**, `<Check answer={2}>`: "The procedure says to rinse the lead(II) sulfate on the filter with cold, dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄(aq). A student rinses it with the same volume of distilled water instead, then dries and weighs the solid. Compared with following the procedure, the mass of lead the student reports is:" Options: higher, because the water leaves sulfate ions behind on the solid, which add to its mass; lower, because more of the solid dissolves in water than in the acid; the same, because K_sp has the same value in water and in the acid; the same, because a precipitate cannot dissolve again once it has formed. Why: a solid on a filter is rinsed to wash away the solution clinging to it, and every rinse dissolves a little of the solid as well. Dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄(aq), holds sulfate ions, SO₄²⁻, a common ion, so very little PbSO₄ dissolves in it. Distilled water has no common ion, so each rinse carries more of the solid through the filter. The solid left to weigh is lighter, and the lead reported is lower. K_sp is indeed the same in both liquids; the solubility is not. And a precipitate is at equilibrium with the liquid around it, so it can always dissolve. (This step is not described in the text above, so the student must reason it through.)
