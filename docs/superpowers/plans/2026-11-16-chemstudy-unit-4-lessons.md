**Goal:** Put Unit 4 (Chemical Reactions) on chem-study.com:
- nine lessons, 4.1 to 4.9;

## Rules for writing a lesson (Tasks 1–4, 7–11)

Read `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-4-ideal-gas-law.mdx` first: it is the model for a lesson with calculations (display math with `aligned`, numeric Checks, a "why" in block form). `src/content/lessons/unit-3/3-8-representations-of-solutions.mdx` is the model for particle drawings, and `src/content/lessons/unit-7/7-3-reaction-quotient-and-equilibrium-constant.mdx` for a lesson that offers equations as options.

**The file.** `src/content/lessons/unit-4/<file name in the task>`. The name must be `<unit>-<number>-<slug>.mdx` matching the topic, or the build fails. Front matter is exactly three fields:

```mdx
---
topic: "4.1"
minutes: 9
summary: One sentence, 20 to 200 characters, saying what the lesson teaches.
---
```

Put the summary in double quotes if it contains a colon. The title comes from `src/data/units.ts`. Do not write an `# H1`.

**The seven parts, in order.** The build fails if a lesson lacks a worked example, a trap box, key points, practice-next or three checks.

1. Opening: two or three sentences, no heading, starting from something concrete.
2. Explanation: `##` sections, one idea each. At least one `<KeyIdea>`.
3. `<Worked title="...">` with numbered steps.
4. `<Check>` questions spread through the lesson, each placed right after the idea it tests. Never all at the end.
5. `<Trap>`: the specific mistakes for this topic, as a short list with the mistake in bold.
6. `<KeyPoints>`: four to six bullets. This is the review sheet, so each bullet must stand on its own.
7. `<PracticeNext />` as the last line, with no attributes.

**Markup.**

- Math is KaTeX inside `$...$` or a `$$` block. Formulas and equations use `\ce{...}`, with state symbols: `$\ce{Ag3PO4(s)}$`, `$\ce{Sr^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) -> SrCO3(s)}$`. The arrow is `->`; a reaction that runs both ways uses `<=>`. Electrons in a half-reaction are `e-`: `$\ce{Fe^3+ + e- -> Fe^2+}$`. Units in math are upright: `$\text{mol}$`, `$\text{g/mol}$`. In prose, plain Unicode is fine for a simple formula (BaSO₄, Fe³⁺) when it needs no math.
- **A chemical equation goes on its own line** (a `$$` block with blank lines around it), never inside a sentence, unless it is very short. **A long equation is split** over two or three lines with `aligned`, breaking at the arrow or at a plus sign: a complete ionic equation always needs it. The brief says "split" where it must be; split any other block longer than about 28 characters of rendered math too.
- A choice check: `<Check answer={2}>`, the question, a blank line, a `-` list of options, a blank line, then `<div slot="why">...</div>`. `answer` is the 1-based number of the correct option. **An option is never math alone**: when the options are equations, list them in the question as blocks lettered A to D and make the options "Equation A" to "Equation D"; a simple formula in an option is plain Unicode text with a few words.
- A numeric check: `<Check numeric="0.250">` (add `unit="..."` when there is one). **The number is text in quotes**, so its trailing zeros (significant figures) survive. A very small or large answer is written in e-notation, `numeric="2.32e-3"`. The student's answer is accepted within 2 percent.
- A figure: `<Figure src="/figures/<name>.svg" alt="..." caption="..." />`.
- Inside a block (`<Worked>`, `<Trap>`, `<KeyPoints>`, `<KeyIdea>`), leave a blank line after the opening tag and before the closing tag.
- Excluded material goes in a sentence or paragraph that begins "**Beyond the exam:**". There is no `<Beyond>` block in lessons.
- Refer to another lesson by topic number in plain text ("topic 3.7", "topic 8.5"), with no links.
- **Phone width.** Math in a sentence cannot wrap, so every equation and calculation goes on its own line, split with `aligned` when long, including inside a Check's `<div slot="why">`. A long substitution carries numbers only, with the units stated in words beside it, or is split over two lines. Tables have at most five columns and short cells. No lesson may scroll sideways at 320 px with every answer open.
- **Astro drops the space between text and an inline element on the next line.** Keep an inline tag on the same line as the word before it.

**Voice.** A teacher talking, not a textbook. Start from something concrete. Short sentences. No "in this section we will". Say "you". Explain why before how. One new term at a time, in bold the first time, defined in the same sentence.

**Chemistry.**

- Teach what the brief's "Framework" line lists, and nothing the exam excludes unless the brief says to, in a "Beyond the exam" sentence. "Oxidizing agent" and "reducing agent" appear once, in lesson 4.7's; Lewis acids and bases once, in lesson 4.8's.
- Use the brief's reactions and numbers exactly. They were checked when this plan was written. Recompute each number and rebalance each equation as you write it; if one disagrees, stop and say so; do not quietly change it.
- Do not add a worked example or a check that the brief does not list. Do not drop one. **Do not add a reaction of your own**, even as a passing example: every reaction in this plan was confirmed to go as described, and an added one was not.
- **A Check must not be answered word for word by the sentence, caption or key idea just above it.** If the brief places a Check right after a sentence that states its answer, keep the Check, word the correct option differently, and report it.
- **Nothing rests on memory the question does not supply.** A question says whether an acid or a base is strong or weak, and whether a compound is soluble, unless that follows from the one rule (sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts are soluble).
- **Never write "$M_aV_a = M_bV_b$"** or any shortcut that assumes a 1 : 1 ratio. Every calculation goes through moles and the ratio of the balanced equation.
- **Keep the hedges**: evidence of a reaction is "possible" evidence; processes are "typically" classified as physical or chemical. **Do not rule on whether dissolving a salt is physical or chemical.**
- **"Only", "always", "every" and "never"** in a statement of fact must be literally true. State the condition or soften the claim.
- Atomic masses are the exam's periodic table's, to the figures it prints. $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K).
- Never take an example, a data table or a sentence from a textbook.

**Static figures.** Hand-written SVG in `public/figures/`, in the style of Unit 3's (`3-8-concentration-boxes.svg` for particles, `3-4-pressure-volume.svg` for graphs): a `viewBox` 480 wide (taller where a figure needs it), a `#FDFCF9` background rectangle, `font-family="system-ui, sans-serif" font-size="18"`, ink `#26231F`, secondary `#6B655D`, accent `#4F706C`, light accent fill `#E3EAE9`.

- Two things in one figure that must be told apart differ in shape, shading or dash pattern as well as color. Label directly; do not rely on a legend alone.
- **Particle drawings keep the counts the brief gives exactly**, so that atoms and charge are conserved between a "before" and an "after" box. Cations dark, anions pale, as in lesson 3.8. Dissolved ions are drawn apart; a precipitate is a cluster of touching ions with unlike charges next to each other; water molecules are left out, and the caption says so.
- Place points and particles from computed coordinates, not by eye.
- No line may pass behind or touch a particle or label it does not belong to.
- The alt text says what the figure shows in words and must be true of the figure as drawn, including counts.
- **Render every figure and look at it** before reporting: `node <scratchpad>/unit3/svg-shot.cjs <a new output folder> 390 <name.svg>`. Fix overlaps and any text that would be under 12 px when the figure is 320 px wide.

- **Opening:** strontium carbonate is a white powder, a raw material of the ceramic magnets in loudspeakers and small electric motors. - **Section "Every change has an equation":** an equation puts what you start with on the left of an arrow and what you end with on the right. Any change can be written this way. Each formula carries a **state symbol**; give the small table:

  | Symbol | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | (s) | solid |
  | (l) | liquid |
  | (g) | gas |
  | (aq) | dissolved in water |

  Three examples, each as a block equation with one line of words before it:

  - Dissolving. The calcium chloride pellets in a closet moisture absorber dissolve in the water they pull out of the air (split over two lines): $\ce{CaCl2(s) -> Ca^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq)}$.

- **Section "Balanced means the same atoms and the same charge":** a change rearranges atoms. It does not make them or destroy them. So a correct equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, and that is why mass is conserved. Electrons are not made or destroyed either, so the total charge is the same on both sides too. Count the baking soda equation with the student: 2 Na, 2 H, 2 C and 6 O on the left; 2 Na, 2 H, 2 C and 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 O on the right; no charges on either side. Then the dissolving equation: 1 Ca and 2 Cl on each side, and the charge, as a block: $(+2) + 2(-1) = 0$, the same as the uncharged solid on the left.
- `<KeyIdea>`: a balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element, and the same total charge, on both sides. Nothing is made and nothing is lost. The atoms are only rearranged.
- **Section "Balancing by inspection":** balancing by inspection means counting and adjusting until both sides agree. There is no formula for it. The rules, as a numbered list:
  1. Write the correct formula and state of every substance first. After that, change coefficients only. Change a subscript and you have written a different substance.
  2. Start with an element that appears in only one formula on each side.
  3. Leave for last an element that stands alone, such as O₂, or that appears in several formulas.
  4. A polyatomic ion that is the same on both sides can be counted as one unit.
  5. If a fraction turns up, multiply every coefficient by its denominator.
  6. Finish by counting every element on both sides, and the charge. Use the smallest whole numbers.
- **Worked example "Balancing by inspection":** pyrite, FeS₂, is the brassy yellow mineral known as fool's gold. Roasted in air, it turns into iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃, and sulfur dioxide gas, which is one way sulfuric acid makers get their sulfur dioxide. Write the balanced equation.
  1. **Write the formulas and states.** As a block, split over two lines: $\ce{FeS2(s) + O2(g) -> Fe2O3(s) + SO2(g)}$. From here on, only coefficients change.
  2. **Start with iron.** It is in one formula on each side. Fe₂O₃ holds 2 Fe, so put a 2 in front of FeS₂.
  3. **Then sulfur.** 2 FeS₂ hold 4 S, so put a 4 in front of SO₂.
  4. **Leave oxygen for last.** It is in both products, and on the left it stands alone as O₂. The right now has 3 O in Fe₂O₃ and 8 O in 4 SO₂: 11 O atoms, which is eleven halves of an O₂. As a block, split over two lines: $\ce{2FeS2 + 11/2 O2 -> Fe2O3 + 4SO2}$.
  5. **Clear the fraction.** Multiply every coefficient by 2, as a block, split over two lines: $\ce{4FeS2(s) + 11O2(g) -> 2Fe2O3(s) + 8SO2(g)}$.
  6. **Count.** Fe: 4 and 4. S: 8 and 8. O: 22 on the left, and 6 + 16 = 22 on the right. No charges on either side. The coefficients 4, 11, 2 and 8 have no common factor, so these are the smallest whole numbers.

Oxygen last: 3 + 1 = 4 O on the right, so 2 O₂. Balanced, as a block split over two lines: $\ce{NH4+(aq) + 2O2(g) -> NO3-(aq) + 2H+(aq) + H2O(l)}$.   |---|---|
  | A dissolved ionic compound, such as Na₂CO₃(aq) | A solid, a liquid or a gas, such as SrCO₃(s), H₂O(l) or CO₂(g) |
  | A strong acid in water, such as HNO₃(aq) | A weak acid in water |
  | A strong base in water, such as KOH(aq) | A weak base in water, such as NH₃(aq) |

  Then, in short paragraphs:
  - A **strong acid** is one in which essentially all of the molecules break up into ions in water: nitric acid in water is H⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions, with essentially no HNO₃ molecules left. A **weak acid** gives up only some of its protons to water, for most weak acids a small fraction, so it is written whole. The same goes for bases: a **strong base** is fully separated into ions that include OH⁻, and a **weak base**, such as ammonia, takes protons from water only in part, so it is written whole too.
  - The common strong acids are HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (its first proton) and HClO₄. The common strong bases are the hydroxides of the group 1 metals (LiOH, NaOH, KOH and the rest) and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂. Topic 8.2 returns to them.   - Water is a liquid, and it is never split into ions.
  - When you split a compound, the subscript becomes the number of ions: one Sr(NO₃)₂ gives one Sr²⁺ and two NO₃⁻. A polyatomic ion stays whole.
  - Which ionic compounds dissolve? The rule to use is to treat every sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salt as soluble (topic 4.7). For any other compound, the question says. From here on the lesson says "by the rule".

- **Section "Three forms of one reaction":** a reaction in solution can be written three ways.
  - The **molecular equation** writes every substance as a whole formula, as if nothing were split. It shows what was mixed.
  - The **complete ionic equation** writes every substance the way it is really present, with the dissolved ionic compounds, strong acids and strong bases as separate ions.
  - Ions that are dissolved and separate before and after, unchanged, are **spectator ions**. Take them out of both sides, and what is left is the **net ionic equation**: only the particles that changed.

  The order never varies: molecular equation, complete ionic equation, find the spectator ions, net ionic equation.
- **Worked example "From a mixture to its net ionic equation":** in a laboratory, a few drops of sodium phosphate solution, Na₃PO₄, are added to a solution of silver nitrate, AgNO₃, and a yellow solid forms at once: silver phosphate, which is insoluble. Write the molecular, complete ionic and net ionic equations. (The ions are Ag⁺, NO₃⁻, Na⁺ and PO₄³⁻.)
  1. **Write the formulas of the products.** The ions trade partners. Ag⁺ with PO₄³⁻ needs three Ag⁺ for the charges to cancel: Ag₃PO₄, the yellow solid. Na⁺ with NO₃⁻ makes NaNO₃. Sodium nitrate is soluble, by the rule, so it stays dissolved.
  2. **Balance the molecular equation.** Three Ag need 3 AgNO₃, and then three NO₃ need 3 NaNO₃, which also balances the 3 Na. As a block, split over two lines: $\ce{3AgNO3(aq) + Na3PO4(aq) -> Ag3PO4(s) + 3NaNO3(aq)}$.
  3. **Write the complete ionic equation.** Split the three dissolved ionic compounds and leave the solid whole. As a block, split over three lines: $\ce{3Ag+(aq) + 3NO3-(aq) + 3Na+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq) -> Ag3PO4(s) + 3Na+(aq) + 3NO3-(aq)}$.
  4. **Find the spectator ions.** 3 Na⁺ and 3 NO₃⁻ are on both sides, dissolved and unchanged.
  5. **Write the net ionic equation.** As a block, split over two lines: $\ce{3Ag+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq) -> Ag3PO4(s)}$.
  6. **Check atoms and charge.** 3 Ag, 1 P and 4 O on each side. Charge, as a block: $3(+1) + (-3) = 0$, and the solid on the right has no charge.

  After the example, place the figure, then one short paragraph on which form to use. The molecular equation tells you what to take off the shelf and weigh. The complete ionic equation tells you everything that is in the beaker. The net ionic equation tells you what actually happened, and it is the one the exam asks for most.

  The same thing happens with acids and bases. Three short cases, each with its equations as blocks:
  - **Strong acid with strong base.** Calcium nitrate, a fertilizer, can be made by adding nitric acid (strong) to limewater, a dilute solution of calcium hydroxide (a strong base). - **Section "When nothing happens":** sometimes every ion is a spectator. A gardener dissolves two fertilizers in one watering can: ammonium sulfate and potassium nitrate. The can holds four kinds of ion: NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻, K⁺ and NO₃⁻. Trade partners and you get ammonium nitrate and potassium sulfate. - **Trap:** **Splitting a weak acid, a weak base or water into ions** (a weak acid gives up only some of its protons, and a weak base takes only some, so each is written whole: benzoic acid stays HC₇H₅O₂). **Splitting a solid** (a precipitate is not dissolved: Ag₃PO₄(s) stays whole). **Changing a subscript to make an equation balance** (that changes the substance; change coefficients only). **Leaving a spectator ion in, or striking out an ion that changed** (an ion is a spectator only if it is dissolved and separate on both sides). **Checking atoms and forgetting charge** (2 NH₄⁺ + 3 O₂ → 2 NO₃⁻ + 8 H⁺ has the same atoms on both sides and is not balanced). **Leaving coefficients that share a factor** (2 H⁺ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H₂O becomes H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O). **Leaving off the states** ((s), (l), (g) and (aq) are part of the answer). **Forcing a reaction** (if every ion is dissolved and unchanged on both sides of the complete ionic equation, everything cancels and the answer is "no reaction").
- **Key points:** every change, physical or chemical, can be written as a balanced equation with a state symbol on each formula; balanced means the same number of atoms of each element and the same total charge on both sides, reached by changing coefficients and never subscripts; in a complete ionic equation, dissolved ionic compounds, strong acids and strong bases are written as separate ions, while solids, liquids, gases, weak acids, weak bases and water keep their whole formulas; spectator ions are dissolved and unchanged on both sides, and removing them leaves the net ionic equation, which shows only what changed; mixtures that look different can share one net ionic equation, such as H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) for nitric acid or hydrochloric acid with a strong base whose salt is soluble; when every ion is dissolved and unchanged on both sides of the complete ionic equation, everything cancels and the answer is "no reaction".

- **Framework (4.3.A, skill 3.B):** one statement. A balanced equation, in any of its forms (molecular, complete ionic, net ionic), can be translated into a particle drawing. The learning objective is to represent a given reaction or physical process with a consistent particle model. The framework gives no rules for the drawing: the whole requirement is in the word "consistent". The rules below are what consistent means, and the exam marks drawings by them. The objective covers physical processes too, so one Check draws one. A short lesson: the three figures carry it.
  - **Not in this lesson:** finding the limiting reactant by calculation (topic 4.5). Here the reactant that runs out is found by counting particles in a box, and the lesson calls it "the one that ran out". No moles, no masses.
  - **Conventions, from topic 3.8:** one circle for each atom or one-atom ion; a polyatomic ion is one labeled particle; cations dark, anions pale; dissolved ions apart from one another; water molecules left out of every box, and the caption says so.
- **Opening:** light a gas burner and countless molecules are being pulled apart and put back together in front of you. You cannot see one of them. A chemical equation is one way to say what they are doing. A particle drawing is another: a before-and-after picture of a few of the molecules, small enough to count. On the exam you go both ways, from an equation to a drawing and from a drawing to an equation.
- **Section "What the drawing has to agree with":** a drawing is consistent with an equation when it obeys the same rules the equation does. As a list:
  1. **The same atoms before and after.** Count each element in both boxes.
  2. **Particles react in the ratio of the coefficients.**
  3. **Whatever did not react is still there.** A reactant that is left over stays in the "after" box.
  4. **Dissolved ionic compounds are drawn as separate ions**, never as joined units, and in the numbers the formula gives (topic 3.8).
  5. **A precipitate is drawn as a cluster**: its ions packed together in one place, each cation next to anions.
  6. **Spectator ions stay in the picture**, still dissolved.
  7. **The charges add up to the same total before and after.**

  Then read the first figure with the student. Ethene, C₂H₄, is a small hydrocarbon gas. (It is the gas that makes fruit ripen.) It burns in oxygen, as a block, split over two lines: $\ce{C2H4(g) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}$. Place the figure `4-3-before-and-after.svg` here, then:
  - **Before:** 2 C₂H₄ and 7 O₂.
  - **After:** 4 CO₂, 4 H₂O and 1 O₂.
  - **The atoms.** Before: 4 C, 8 H and 14 O. After: 4 C in the CO₂; 8 H in the H₂O; and 8 + 4 + 2 = 14 O. Nothing made, nothing lost.
  - **The ratio.** 2 C₂H₄ and 6 O₂ were used up, and 4 CO₂ and 4 H₂O formed. Divide 2, 6, 4 and 4 by 2 and you have the coefficients: 1, 3, 2 and 2.
  - **The leftover.** The ethene ran out first. The one O₂ molecule that had nothing to react with is still in the box. It is not part of the equation, and it belongs in the picture.

  That is also how to read a pair of drawings backward into an equation: count what was used up and what formed, leave out what is the same in both boxes, and reduce to the smallest whole numbers.

- **Section "Between the particles, or inside them":** two kinds of attraction are at work in a sample of a molecular substance (topic 3.1 drew the line).

- **Check 3**, `<Check answer={4}>`: "Sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄, is a white salt that dissolves in water. Which statement is true of the particles when it dissolves?" **Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "4.5"`, `minutes: 15`, summary: "How the coefficients of a balanced equation turn an amount of one substance into an amount of another, for masses, gases and solutions, and how to find the limiting reactant." (No colon in the summary.)
- **Framework (4.5.A, skill 5.C):** three statements. Atoms are conserved in a reaction, so the amount of a product can be worked out from the amount of a reactant, and the amount of a reactant from the amount of a product. The coefficients of the balanced equation give the proportions in which the substances react and form, and calculations with them go through the mole. These calculations are combined with the ideal gas law and with molarity, for gases and for solutions. The learning objective is to explain how the amounts of reactants and products change, from the balanced equation; the skill is explaining what happens to one quantity when another changes. The unit's introduction asks for ratios other than 1 : 1 and names two things students get wrong: finding the limiting reactant, and comparing masses where moles should be compared. No statement of this topic names the **limiting reactant**, so it is taught here as a routine with no "Beyond the exam" label. **Percent yield** is named nowhere in Unit 4: it gets one short section, said to be less common on the exam. The equation sheet gives $n = m/M$, the molarity relation, $PV = nRT$ and 22.4 L/mol at STP; it gives nothing for a limiting reactant or a yield, and the lesson says so. The lesson leans on the mole and molar mass (topic 1.1), the ideal gas law (topic 3.4) and molarity (topic 3.7), each named once in plain text. This is the longest lesson of the unit: four worked examples, each short, and five Checks. Left out: dilution (topic 3.7), hydrates, combustion analysis, gravimetric analysis as a named method. Two of the reactions are driven by electricity (the smelter and the brine cell): the lesson says so in a clause, names topic 9.8, and teaches nothing about it. **Never write "$M_aV_a = M_bV_b$" or any formula that skips the moles.**
- **Opening:** at an aluminum smelter a white powder, aluminum oxide, goes in with carbon, and liquid aluminum is tapped off. The smelter's buyers have one question to answer before they order a shipload: how many tonnes of the powder must reach the gate for every tonne of metal that leaves it? Nobody found the answer by trying. It is read off a balanced equation, and this lesson shows how. (Do not call the equation a recipe, here or anywhere in the lesson.)
- **Section "What the coefficients say":** the smelter's overall reaction, as a block, split after the arrow: $\ce{2Al2O3(s) + 3C(s) -> 4Al(l) + 3CO2(g)}$. (Electricity drives it, which is a matter for topic 9.8. The amounts follow the equation all the same.) Read it at the particle level: 2 formula units of Al₂O₃ and 3 carbon atoms give 4 aluminum atoms and 3 CO₂ molecules. Count the atoms: 4 Al, 6 O and 3 C on each side. Nothing is made or lost, which is why the amounts are tied together. Nobody counts out single atoms, so scale every number up by the same factor, 6.022 × 10²³: the equation now reads 2 mol, 3 mol, 4 mol, 3 mol (the mole, topic 1.1). **The coefficients are a ratio of moles.** They are not a ratio of grams, because a mole of one substance does not weigh what a mole of another does. Show the table (five columns):

  | | Al₂O₃ | C | Al | CO₂ |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Particles | 2 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
  | Moles | 2 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
  | Grams | 203.92 | 36.03 | 107.92 | 132.03 |

  The grams come from the molar masses (Al₂O₃ 101.96 g/mol, C 12.01, Al 26.98, CO₂ 44.01). They look nothing like 2 : 3 : 4 : 3. But add them: 203.92 + 36.03 = 239.95 g of reactants, and 107.92 + 132.03 = 239.95 g of products. Mass is conserved, because the atoms are. Any two entries of the Moles row make a conversion factor, a **mole ratio**: 4 mol Al for every 2 mol Al₂O₃, or 3 mol C for every 4 mol Al. Amounts are proportional: twice the alumina gives twice the aluminum, as long as there is carbon enough for it.
- `<KeyIdea>`: the coefficients of a balanced equation compare moles (and particles), not grams. To get from one substance to another, you must pass through moles.
- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={3}>`: "Oil refineries strip poisonous hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, out of their fuels and turn it into sulfur by reacting it with sulfur dioxide (as a block equation): $\ce{2H2S(g) + SO2(g) -> 3S(l) + 2H2O(g)}$. Which statement does this equation support?" Options: Every 2 g of H₂S that reacts uses up 1 g of SO₂; The total number of moles of substances is the same before and after the reaction; For every 1 mol of SO₂ that reacts, 3 mol of sulfur atoms form; H₂S and SO₂ molecules are used up in equal numbers. Why: the coefficients count particles, and so moles: 2 mol of H₂S reacts with 1 mol of SO₂ and gives 3 mol of S and 2 mol of H₂O. In grams, 2 mol of H₂S is 2(34.08) = 68.16 g and 1 mol of SO₂ is 64.06 g: nearly equal masses, nothing like 2 to 1. Moles of substances are not conserved: 3 mol goes in and 5 mol comes out. What is conserved is atoms: 3 S, 4 H and 2 O on each side. (Correct option is the third; it is not the longest.)
- **Section "The route through moles":** every calculation in this lesson has the same three legs, as a numbered list: (1) turn the amount you are given into moles; (2) cross from that substance to the one you want with the mole ratio from the coefficients; (3) turn those moles into the kind of amount the question asks for. Only the first and last legs change from problem to problem, and there are three ways in and out of moles: a mass, through the molar mass ($n = m/M$, topic 1.1); a gas, through $PV = nRT$ (topic 3.4); a solution, through its molarity (topic 3.7). The middle leg is always the same, and it is the only one that uses the equation. Say that the route runs both ways: from a reactant to a product, or back from the product you want to the reactant you need. And say that it starts from a **balanced** equation (topic 4.2): a ratio taken from an unbalanced equation cannot be trusted. Place the figure `4-5-route-through-moles.svg` here.

- **Worked example "Alumina for a kilogram of aluminum":** how many kilograms of aluminum oxide does the smelter need for each 1.00 kg of aluminum it makes? The equation, as a block, split after the arrow: $\ce{2Al2O3(s) + 3C(s) -> 4Al(l) + 3CO2(g)}$. (This one runs backward along the route: from the product wanted to the reactant needed.)
  1. **Into moles.** 1.00 kg is 1.00 × 10³ g. As a block: $n = \dfrac{1.00 \times 10^{3}\ \text{g}}{26.98\ \text{g/mol}} = 37.06\ \text{mol Al}$.
  2. **Across by the ratio.** The equation has 2 mol Al₂O₃ for every 4 mol Al. As a block, split with `aligned` so the result is on its own line: $37.06\ \text{mol Al} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol Al}_2\text{O}_3}{4\ \text{mol Al}} = 18.53\ \text{mol Al}_2\text{O}_3$. (Write the ratio with `\ce{Al2O3}`; put the substance wanted on top, so the unit "mol Al" cancels.)
  3. **Out of moles.** The molar mass of Al₂O₃ is 2(26.98) + 3(16.00) = 101.96 g/mol. As a block, split with `aligned`: $m = (18.53\ \text{mol})(101.96\ \text{g/mol}) = 1.89 \times 10^{3}\ \text{g} = 1.89\ \text{kg}$.
  4. **Does it make sense?** Nearly 2 kg of powder for each kilogram of metal: the rest of the powder's mass is oxygen, which leaves as CO₂. Real smelters do use about 1.9 tonnes of alumina for each tonne of aluminum.
  5. **What the slips would give.** A 1 : 1 ratio gives 3.78 kg, twice too much. The ratio upside down (4 over 2) gives 7.56 kg.

- **Section "When a gas is involved":** for a gas, the way into or out of moles is the ideal gas law (topic 3.4): $n = PV/RT$ going in, $V = nRT/P$ coming out (each as a block), with $R$ = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), the temperature in kelvin and the pressure in atm. At STP only (273.15 K and 1.0 atm), there is a shortcut on the equation sheet: one mole of any ideal gas fills 22.4 L. Nothing else is new: the mole ratio is used exactly as before.
- **Worked example "Chlorine from salt water":** a chemical plant passes electricity through brine, a concentrated solution of sodium chloride, and gets three products at once, as a block, split after the arrow: $\ce{2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g) + Cl2(g)}$. (Sodium hydroxide, NaOH, is a strong base; it stays dissolved.) What volume of chlorine gas, measured at 25.0 °C and 1.00 atm, comes from 142 g of NaCl?
  1. **Into moles.** The molar mass of NaCl is 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol. As a block: $n = \dfrac{142\ \text{g}}{58.44\ \text{g/mol}} = 2.430\ \text{mol NaCl}$.
  2. **Across by the ratio.** 1 mol Cl₂ for every 2 mol NaCl. As a block, split with `aligned` so the result is on its own line: $2.430\ \text{mol NaCl} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Cl}_2}{2\ \text{mol NaCl}} = 1.215\ \text{mol Cl}_2$.
  3. **Kelvin.** As a block: $T = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.2\ \text{K}$.
  4. **Out of moles, by the gas law.** As a block, split with `aligned`, numbers only in the fraction, with the units said in words beside it (moles, kelvin, atm): $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(1.215)(0.08206)(298.2)}{1.00} = 29.7\ \text{L}$.
  5. **Two things to notice.** The gas is not at STP, so 22.4 L/mol does not apply: it would have given 27.2 L. And the equation makes one H₂ for every Cl₂, so the cell gives off 29.7 L of hydrogen as well: equal moles of any two gases fill equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure (topic 3.4). (Other slips: a 1 : 1 ratio gives 59.4 L, and 25.0 in place of 298.2 gives 2.49 L.)
- **Section "When a solution is involved":** for a dissolved substance the way into moles is the molarity (topic 3.7): moles = molarity × volume, **with the volume in liters** (as a block: $n = M \times V$). That multiplication at the start is the only new step. Going out the other way, divide: volume = moles ÷ molarity.
- **Worked example "Zinc out of the rinse water":** a plating shop coats steel with zinc from a bath of zinc sulfate, ZnSO₄, and its rinse water ends up holding some of that zinc sulfate. Before the water is let go, the zinc is taken out of it: sodium hydroxide, a strong base, is added, and the zinc leaves the solution as a solid. What forms first is zinc hydroxide, which is insoluble. As a block, split over two lines after the arrow: $\ce{ZnSO4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Zn(OH)2(s) + Na2SO4(aq)}$. A technician treats a sample of the rinse water with 50.0 mL of 0.240 M NaOH, a dose matched to the zinc in the sample: two OH⁻ for every Zn²⁺. What mass of zinc does this dose take out of the water?
  1. **Into moles.** 50.0 mL is 0.0500 L. As a block, split with `aligned`: $n = (0.240\ \text{mol/L})(0.0500\ \text{L}) = 0.0120\ \text{mol NaOH}$.
  2. **Across by the ratio.** Each NaOH supplies one OH⁻, and the equation uses 2 mol of NaOH for every 1 mol of ZnSO₄, which holds 1 mol of Zn²⁺. As a block, split with `aligned` so the result is on its own line: $0.0120\ \text{mol NaOH} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Zn}^{2+}}{2\ \text{mol NaOH}} = 6.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol Zn}^{2+}$.
  3. **Out of moles.** The molar mass of zinc is 65.38 g/mol. As a block, split with `aligned`: $m = (6.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol})(65.38\ \text{g/mol}) = 0.392\ \text{g}$ of zinc.
  4. **What the slips would give.** A 1 : 1 ratio gives 0.785 g, twice too much. The ratio upside down gives 1.57 g. Leaving the volume as 50.0 in place of 0.0500 gives 12.0 mol of NaOH and 392 g of zinc from a few spoonfuls of solution: an answer that size is a sign that milliliters went in as liters.

  After the example, two sentences: here the dose was matched to the zinc, so the amount of zinc sulfate never entered the calculation. The next section is about what happens when the two amounts do not match.

- **Section "The limiting reactant":** so far only one amount mattered: the other reactant was plentiful, or matched to it. Usually both amounts are fixed, and they are almost never in exactly the ratio the equation uses. Then one reactant is used up while some of the other is still there. The one that would be used up first is the **limiting reactant**: when it is gone the reaction stops, so it alone decides how much product can form. The other is the reactant **in excess**, and some of it is left over. Start with particles. Place the figure `4-5-limiting-reactant-particles.svg` here and read it with the student: the refinery reaction of Check 1, $\ce{2H2S(g) + SO2(g) -> 3S(l) + 2H2O(g)}$ (as a block), with 6 H₂S and 4 SO₂ in the box. Every 2 H₂S take 1 SO₂, so the 6 H₂S take 3 SO₂. There are 4, so the H₂S runs out first and 1 SO₂ is left, with 9 S and 6 H₂O. Notice which one ran out: H₂S, though the box held more H₂S molecules than SO₂ molecules. To use up 4 SO₂ would have taken 8 H₂S. **The limiting reactant is not necessarily the one there is less of. It is the one that runs out first, given the ratio in the equation.** (One clause beside the figure: liquid sulfur is really mostly rings of eight atoms, and the figure draws single atoms to keep the count plain.) Then the routine, as a numbered list: (1) turn both amounts into moles; (2) pick one reactant and use the mole ratio to find how many moles of the other it would need; (3) compare that with what is there: if there is enough of the other, the reactant you picked is limiting, and if not, the other one is; (4) calculate the product from the limiting reactant, by the usual route; (5) for the leftover, subtract what the limiting reactant used from what was there. Never compare grams with grams: say that this is the classic slip of the topic (the framework's advice about the exam names it). The equation sheet has nothing on this; the routine has to be known.

- **Worked example "Which gas runs out?":** a working sulfur unit holds its two gases at 2 to 1, so that neither is wasted. On a test run, the unit takes in 70.0 kg of H₂S and 79.5 kg of SO₂ each hour. Which gas is limiting? What mass of sulfur can form each hour, and how much of the other gas is left? The equation, as a block: $\ce{2H2S(g) + SO2(g) -> 3S(l) + 2H2O(g)}$.
  1. **Both into moles.** Molar masses: H₂S, 2(1.008) + 32.06 = 34.08 g/mol; SO₂, 32.06 + 2(16.00) = 64.06 g/mol. As two blocks: $n_{\ce{H2S}} = \dfrac{7.00 \times 10^{4}\ \text{g}}{34.08\ \text{g/mol}} = 2054\ \text{mol}$ and $n_{\ce{SO2}} = \dfrac{7.95 \times 10^{4}\ \text{g}}{64.06\ \text{g/mol}} = 1241\ \text{mol}$.
  2. **What would the H₂S need?** 1 mol SO₂ for every 2 mol H₂S. As a block, split with `aligned` so the result is on its own line: $2054\ \text{mol H}_2\text{S} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol SO}_2}{2\ \text{mol H}_2\text{S}} = 1027\ \text{mol SO}_2$.
  3. **Compare.** The unit has 1241 mol of SO₂, more than the 1027 mol needed. So the H₂S runs out first: **H₂S is the limiting reactant**, and SO₂ is in excess. (Asked the other way round, the answer is the same: 1241 mol of SO₂ would need 2482 mol of H₂S, and there is only 2054 mol.)
  4. **Product, from the limiting reactant.** 3 mol S for every 2 mol H₂S: 2054 × 3/2 = 3081 mol S. As a block, split with `aligned`: $m = (3081\ \text{mol})(32.06\ \text{g/mol}) = 9.88 \times 10^{4}\ \text{g} = 98.8\ \text{kg}$.
  5. **Leftover.** SO₂ left = 1241 − 1027 = 214 mol. As a block, split with `aligned`: $m = (214\ \text{mol})(64.06\ \text{g/mol}) = 1.37 \times 10^{4}\ \text{g} = 13.7\ \text{kg}$.
  6. **Look at the two shortcuts.** By mass there is less H₂S (70.0 kg against 79.5 kg). By moles there is less SO₂ (1241 mol against 2054 mol). The shortcuts point at different gases, so at least one of them is wrong, and neither is a test. The test is step 2: moles, compared through the coefficients.
  7. **Check the books.** The water formed is 2054 mol (2 mol H₂O for every 2 mol H₂S), and its molar mass is 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol, so it weighs 37.0 kg. Then 98.8 + 37.0 + 13.7 = 149.5 kg comes out, and 70.0 + 79.5 = 149.5 kg went in.

  Notes for the writer: the masses were chosen so that every step shown agrees with the unrounded calculation to the figures printed (the script checks both). Taking SO₂ as limiting by mistake would give 119 kg of sulfur.

- **Section "Percent yield":** short, four or five sentences and one block. The amount of product the limiting reactant allows is the **theoretical yield**: it comes from a calculation. What is actually collected is the **actual yield**: it comes from a balance. The **percent yield** compares them, as a block: $\text{percent yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$. Both yields must be in the same unit. Real yields fall short for ordinary reasons: many reactions stop before the limiting reactant is quite gone (topic 7.1), some of the reactants go into other reactions, and some product is lost in collecting it. The refinery's sulfur unit is an example: the calculation above promises 98.8 kg of sulfur an hour, and a unit of this kind might deliver 95.5 kg, which as a block is $\dfrac{95.5\ \text{kg}}{98.8\ \text{kg}} \times 100 = 96.7\%$. (Real sulfur units recover between about 94 and 98 percent, depending on how many catalyst beds they have.) Say plainly that the equation sheet does not give this formula and that the exam asks for it less often than for the rest of this lesson.

- **Section "Counting by reacting":** you cannot count the acid molecules in a spoonful of wine, but you can count what it takes to react with all of them. A **titration** finds the amount of a dissolved substance by reacting it with a measured amount of something else. Two names: the **analyte** is the substance being measured; the **titrant** is the solution added to it, whose concentration is known. The titrant goes in a **buret**, a long marked tube with a tap, so the volume run out can be read to a hundredth of a milliliter. Volume times molarity is moles (topic 3.7), so the buret reading is really a count of the titrant's particles. For the count to mean anything, the reaction has to pass two tests, as a short list: it goes to completion, so no analyte is left unreacted; and the titrant reacts with the analyte and with nothing else in the sample. - **Section "The equivalence point and the endpoint":** take a simpler acid than the wine's first. Lactic acid, HC₃H₅O₃, is the acid of yogurt and sour milk. It is a weak acid: in water only a small part of it is in the form of ions (topic 8.3). It has one proton to give. Sodium hydroxide is a strong base: in water all of it is Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions. The hydroxide ion takes the proton from every lactic acid molecule it meets, weak acid or not, so the reaction goes to completion (as a block, split after the arrow): $\ce{HC3H5O3(aq) + OH^-(aq) -> C3H5O3^-(aq) + H2O(l)}$. The Na⁺ ions take no part (spectator ions, topic 4.2). With volumes in milliliters it is handy to count in **millimoles** (mmol), thousandths of a mole: milliliters times molarity gives millimoles. Now define the two points:
  - The **equivalence point** is the moment when the titrant added has exactly used up the analyte. It is set by amounts and by the balanced equation, and by nothing else. A flask holding 1.50 mmol of lactic acid reaches it when 1.50 mmol of OH⁻ has gone in, because the equation is 1 to 1. An acid with two protons to give would take twice as much.
  - Nothing marks that moment by itself: the acid, the base and the products are all colorless. So a few drops of an **indicator** go into the flask, a dye that has one color in acid and another in base. Bromothymol blue is yellow in acid and blue in base. For nearly the whole titration the flask is yellow. Over the last few drops it shades toward green, and the drop that uses up the last of the acid turns it blue. The change you see is the **endpoint**.
  - The two are different kinds of thing. The equivalence point is a fact about amounts, and you calculate with it. The endpoint is an event, and you watch for it. A titration is designed so that the second happens at the first, to within a drop. How an indicator is chosen to do that, and how the acidity of the flask changes on the way, belong to topic 8.5.
- `<KeyIdea>`: the equivalence point is where the amounts of titrant and analyte exactly match the balanced equation. The endpoint is the color change you see. A good titration makes the endpoint fall on the equivalence point.

- **Section "Precipitation: ions join into a solid":**

- **Section "Oxidation–reduction: electrons move":**

  - Each iodide ion gave up one electron, and the bromine molecule took the two of them. The species that loses electrons is **oxidized**; the species that gains them is **reduced**. Electrons go from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced. The two always happen together: an electron that leaves one species has to land on another.
  - Here the charges show it. More often they do not, because in molecules electrons are shared, and nothing in the equation carries a charge. So chemists keep the books with oxidation numbers.
  - One sentence, as its own paragraph: "**Beyond the exam:** the species that is reduced is also called the oxidizing agent, because it takes electrons from something else, and the species that is oxidized is called the reducing agent. The exam does not test these two names."
- **Section "Oxidation numbers":**
  - An **oxidation number** is the charge an atom would have if the shared electrons in each of its bonds were handed over completely to the more electronegative atom (topic 2.1). For a one-atom ion it is the real charge. For an atom in a molecule it is bookkeeping, not a real charge.
  - The rules, as a numbered list, **in order: when two rules disagree, the one higher on the list wins.** Say that neither the exam's periodic table nor its equation sheet gives them.
    1. An atom of an element on its own (Co, O₂, Br₂) is 0.
    2. A one-atom ion is its charge: Cd²⁺ is +2 and I⁻ is −1. That covers the ions of an ionic compound too: in KBr, potassium is +1 and bromine is −1.
    3. The oxidation numbers of all the atoms add up to the charge of the species: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for a polyatomic ion.
    4. Fluorine in a compound is −1.
    5. Hydrogen is +1 when it is bonded to a nonmetal, as in H₂O, NH₃ and the OH of a hydroxide. It is −1 when it is bonded directly to a metal, as in a metal hydride such as NaH.
    6. Oxygen is −2, except in peroxides, where it is −1.
  - Five short examples, each sum as a block:
    - V₂O₅, the catalyst of sulfuric acid plants: oxygen is −2, so $2x + 5(-2) = 0$ and each vanadium is +5.
    - The iodate ion, IO₃⁻, the form of iodine added to table salt in many countries: $x + 3(-2) = -1$, so iodine is +5. The sum is the ion's charge, not zero.
    - Sodium hydride, NaH: here the hydrogen is bonded directly to a metal. Sodium forms Na⁺, +1, so hydrogen is −1.
    - Oxygen difluoride, OF₂: fluorine is −1 (rule 4 comes before rule 6), so $x + 2(-1) = 0$ and oxygen is +2.
    - Calcium peroxide, CaO₂: calcium forms Ca²⁺, +2, so $(+2) + 2x = 0$ and each oxygen is −1. That is the −1 of rule 6.

- **Section "Acids give protons, bases take them":**

**Brief**

- **Front matter:** `topic: "4.9"`, `minutes: 10`, summary: "How to split an oxidation–reduction reaction into two half-reactions, one losing electrons and one gaining them, and rebuild the balanced equation by making the electrons cancel." (No colon in the summary.)
- **Framework (4.9.A, skill 5.E):** one statement. A balanced equation for an oxidation–reduction reaction can be built from half-reactions, and the student represents a balanced redox equation that way. The framework says nothing more: it gives no steps, and it does not say whether the half-reactions are given or written by the student. This is a short lesson; do not pad it.
  - **Taught with no label, because the exam uses them:** electrons written in the half-reactions and made to cancel; writing a simple half-reaction (a metal and its ion, a halogen and its ion); combining half-reactions that are given.
  - **Shown once, and said to be less common on the exam:** balancing one half-reaction in acidic solution with H₂O and H⁺ (the second worked example). No Check asks the student to do it. Nothing in basic solution.
  - **Not in this lesson:** the rules for oxidation numbers (topic 4.7; one step of the second worked example uses them as a cross-check and names the topic); the terms oxidizing agent and reducing agent; reduction potentials, cells, or which reactions happen by themselves (Unit 9).
  - The lesson relies on topic 4.2 for one idea: a balanced equation has the same atoms and the same total charge on both sides.
- **Opening:** some factories make fine metal meshes without cutting anything. A thin sheet of nickel is printed with a pattern in a protective coating and then sprayed with a solution of iron(III) chloride. Wherever the nickel is bare, it dissolves, and what is left is the mesh. Nothing is being worn away by force: iron(III) ions are pulling electrons out of nickel atoms. This lesson is about keeping count of those electrons, because the count is what balances the equation.
- **Section "A redox reaction is two halves":**
  - Topic 4.7: in an oxidation–reduction (redox) reaction, electrons pass from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced. Here nickel atoms lose electrons and become Ni²⁺ ions, which dissolve; Fe³⁺ ions gain electrons and become Fe²⁺ ions. (The chloride ions are spectators.)
  - A **half-reaction** shows one of those two changes by itself, with the electrons written in, as e⁻. Each as a block:
    - Oxidation, electrons lost: $\ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e^-}$
    - Reduction, electrons gained: $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e^- -> Fe^2+(aq)}$
  - In an **oxidation** half-reaction the electrons are a product, on the right. In a **reduction** half-reaction they are a reactant, on the left. Oxidation is a loss of electrons and a rise in oxidation number (nickel, 0 to +2); reduction is a gain of electrons and a fall (iron, +3 to +2).
  - A half-reaction is balanced like any equation, in atoms and in charge. Count the charge with each electron as −1: for nickel, 0 on the left and (+2) + 2(−1) = 0 on the right; for iron, (+3) + (−1) = +2 on the left and +2 on the right.
  - Neither half happens alone. Electrons do not pile up anywhere: every electron one species loses, another species gains at once. The two always come together.
- **Section "Writing a simple half-reaction":** three steps, as a numbered list: (1) write the species before and after; (2) balance the atoms; (3) add electrons to the more positive side until the two sides have the same charge. Two examples, each as a block.
  - A metal and its ion: cobalt atoms becoming cobalt(II) ions. $\ce{Co(s) -> Co^2+(aq) + 2e^-}$. The right side would be +2, so 2 electrons go there.
  - A halogen and its ion: bromine becoming bromide ions. Bromine is Br₂, so the atoms come first: $\ce{Br2(aq) -> 2Br^-(aq)}$. Now the left is 0 and the right is −2, so 2 electrons go on the left: $\ce{Br2(aq) + 2e^- -> 2Br^-(aq)}$.
- **Check 1**, `<Check answer={3}>`: "In Japan and in Oklahoma, iodine is made from brine, salty water pumped up from deep underground, which holds iodide ions, I⁻. Chlorine gas is bubbled through the brine and turns the iodide into iodine, I₂. Which of these is the correctly balanced half-reaction for what happens to the iodide?" Then the four half-reactions as blocks, lettered A to D, each on its own line:
  - A: $\ce{I^-(aq) -> I2(aq) + e^-}$
  - B: $\ce{2I^-(aq) + 2e^- -> I2(aq)}$
  - C: $\ce{2I^-(aq) -> I2(aq) + 2e^-}$
  - D: $\ce{I2(aq) + 2e^- -> 2I^-(aq)}$

  Options: Equation A; Equation B; Equation C; Equation D. Why: atoms first. I₂ holds two iodine atoms, so two I⁻ are needed. Then the charge: the left is 2(−1) = −2 and I₂ is 0, so 2 electrons go on the right, and both sides are −2. That is C. Iodide loses electrons, so this is an oxidation, and the electrons are a product. A has one iodine atom on the left and two on the right. B has the electrons on the wrong side: −4 on the left against 0 on the right. D is balanced, but it shows iodine turning into iodide, the reverse of what happens to the iodide here. The chlorine takes the electrons, as a block: $\ce{Cl2(g) + 2e^- -> 2Cl^-(aq)}$.
- **Section "Making the electrons cancel":** the electrons lost in one half-reaction are the same electrons gained in the other, so the two numbers must be equal. The routine, as a numbered list:
  1. Write the two half-reactions, or take them as given.
  2. Multiply one or both by whole numbers, so that the electrons lost equal the electrons gained.
  3. Add them. Cancel the electrons, and anything else that appears on both sides.
  4. Check the result: the same atoms on both sides, the same total charge on both sides, and no electrons left.
- `<KeyIdea>`: electrons lost must equal electrons gained. Multiply the half-reactions until the electrons match, then add them, and the electrons cancel. A finished redox equation shows no electrons, and both its atoms and its charge balance.
- **Worked example "Etching nickel with iron(III)":** write the balanced equation for the reaction of the opening: nickel metal dissolves in a solution of iron(III) ions, forming Ni²⁺ and Fe²⁺.
  1. **Write the oxidation half-reaction.** As a block: $\ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e^-}$.
  2. **Write the reduction half-reaction.** As a block: $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e^- -> Fe^2+(aq)}$.
  3. **Match the electrons.** Each nickel atom loses 2 electrons, and each Fe³⁺ ion gains only 1. So it takes two Fe³⁺ ions for each nickel atom: multiply the reduction by 2. As a block: $\ce{2Fe^3+(aq) + 2e^- -> 2Fe^2+(aq)}$.
  4. **Add, and cancel the electrons.** Two electrons on the right of one half and two on the left of the other cancel. As a block, split over two lines: $\ce{Ni(s) + 2Fe^3+(aq) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2Fe^2+(aq)}$.
  5. **Check.** Nickel: 1 and 1. Iron: 2 and 2. Charge: 2(+3) = +6 on the left, and (+2) + 2(+2) = +6 on the right. No electrons are left.
  6. **See what skipping step 3 costs.** Adding the two half-reactions as first written gives Ni + Fe³⁺ → Ni²⁺ + Fe²⁺ (as a block). Its atoms balance, and that is what makes it tempting. But the charge is +3 on the left and +4 on the right. An equation whose charge does not balance is not balanced (topic 4.2), and here the missing charge is an electron that nickel lost and nothing gained: the nickel atom gave up two electrons, and the one Fe³⁺ ion took only one.

- **Section "When oxygen and hydrogen are in the half-reaction":**
  - Say first that this is **less common on the exam**, where half-reactions like the next one are usually given to you. It is shown once so that you can follow where such a half-reaction comes from.
  - Many species that are oxidized or reduced in water hold oxygen or hydrogen atoms, and those atoms have to go somewhere. In an **acidic solution** there is plenty of H₂O and plenty of H⁺ to balance them with. The order, as a numbered list: (1) balance the atoms other than O and H; (2) balance O by adding H₂O; (3) balance H by adding H⁺; (4) balance the charge by adding electrons.
- **Worked example "Chlorine taking iron out of well water":** well water often carries dissolved iron(II) ions, Fe²⁺, which later leave orange stains on sinks and laundry. A water treatment plant adds chlorine, which forms hypochlorous acid, HOCl, in the water (a weak acid, so it is written whole). The HOCl turns the Fe²⁺ into Fe³⁺ and is itself turned into chloride ions, Cl⁻. Write the balanced equation, balancing it the way it is done for acidic solution, with H₂O and H⁺. (Note for the writer: well water is close to neutral, so do not call the water acidic; the method is what is being shown.)
  1. **Write the simple half-reaction.** Iron(II) loses an electron. As a block: $\ce{Fe^2+(aq) -> Fe^3+(aq) + e^-}$.
  2. **Start the other with what is known.** HOCl becomes Cl⁻: $\ce{HOCl -> Cl^-}$ (as a block). Chlorine is already balanced, 1 and 1.
  3. **Balance oxygen with water.** One O on the left, none on the right, so add one H₂O to the right: $\ce{HOCl -> Cl^- + H2O}$ (as a block).
  4. **Balance hydrogen with H⁺.** One H on the left, two on the right, so add one H⁺ to the left: $\ce{HOCl + H^+ -> Cl^- + H2O}$ (as a block).
  5. **Balance the charge with electrons.** The left is +1 and the right is −1, so add 2 electrons to the left. As a block, split over two lines: $\ce{HOCl(aq) + H^+(aq) + 2e^- -> Cl^-(aq) + H2O(l)}$. Both sides are now −1. A cross-check from topic 4.7: chlorine is +1 in HOCl and −1 in Cl⁻, a fall of 2, which is 2 electrons gained.
  6. **Match the electrons.** The iron half-reaction loses 1 and this one gains 2, so multiply the iron half-reaction by 2. As a block: $\ce{2Fe^2+(aq) -> 2Fe^3+(aq) + 2e^-}$.
  7. **Add, and cancel the electrons.** As a block, split over two or three lines: $\ce{HOCl(aq) + H^+(aq) + 2Fe^2+(aq) -> Cl^-(aq) + H2O(l) + 2Fe^3+(aq)}$.
  8. **Check.** Chlorine: 1 and 1. Oxygen: 1 and 1. Hydrogen: 2 and 2. Iron: 2 and 2. Charge: 0 + (+1) + 2(+2) = +5 on the left, and (−1) + 0 + 2(+3) = +5 on the right.

- **Trap:** **Adding the half-reactions without matching the electrons** (Ni + Fe³⁺ → Ni²⁺ + Fe²⁺ has balanced atoms and an unbalanced charge, +3 against +4). **Putting the electrons on the wrong side** (they are a product in an oxidation and a reactant in a reduction). **Adding electrons before the atoms are balanced** (two iodide ions make one I₂, so the half-reaction starts with 2 I⁻). **Multiplying only part of a half-reaction** (the multiplier applies to every species in it, H⁺ and H₂O included). **Leaving electrons in the final equation** (they must cancel completely). **Checking the atoms and skipping the charge** (in a redox equation the charge is the check that catches the mistake).
