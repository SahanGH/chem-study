## 4. What each part asks

As Unit 1's test taught, the task table was made after an avoid list that
indexes the pages' text as well as their questions, and **every task was
compared with the pages themselves before any writer started**
(`scratchpad/unit6-test/avoid-list.md` and `task-table.md`; twenty-four first
drafts were dropped in that comparison). Each task is a kind of question the
site has not set: two students' setups with slips to name; data that cannot
be right; which measurement is enough; a ranking that changes with its
basis; a claim weighed against data; a table read backward.

**Part A: Systems, signs and dissolving (6.1)**

- **A1.** A table for three salts (LiBr, KBr, LiF) gives the energy taken in to separate the particles and the energy released as ion–water attractions form. Say which solutions warm and which cool, find the net energy for each, and weigh a student's claim that "the salt whose ions attract water most strongly warms the water most" (LiF releases the most and still cools).
- **A2.** Two liquids are mixed three times: acetone with chloroform (warms), an alcohol with an alkane (cools), two alkanes (no change that can be measured). For each, name the attractions before and after and explain the observation; say which mixing is neither exothermic nor endothermic.
- **A3.** Four trials of one solid dissolved in water (masses of solid and water vary). One recorded temperature change has the wrong sign and one is the wrong size. Find both, say what each should be near, and say what must be the same in every trial and what need not be.
- **A4.** Two observations in words. (i) Air is squeezed quickly in a pump and its barrel warms, with nothing reacting. (ii) Hydrogen peroxide breaks down in a flask joined to a gas syringe: the flask warms and the plunger moves out. For each, name the system, and say whether energy crosses as heat, as work or as both, and which way. Then say which of two statements about (ii) undercounts the energy the system lost.

**Part B: Energy diagrams, and heat transfer (6.2, 6.3)**

- **B1.** A table gives the starting and final levels of five two-level diagrams, drawn with different zeros. Find the two that show the same change, the two that are one change and its reverse, and rank all five by what a thermometer in the surroundings would do.
- **B2.** Magnesium carbonate breaks down in a kiln. Two students each give an energy diagram in words: one for one mole, one for a 1.00 kg batch. Say whether the two disagree. A third sketch has three slips (the axis labeled "temperature"; the levels labeled with the kiln, not the substances; the arrow drawn from the products). Name each.
- **B3.** A made-up substance Q has three levels on one axis: solid, liquid and gas, for one mole at one temperature. Read from the table the energy of melting, of vaporizing, of subliming and of depositing; say which arrow is longest and why; say what a diagram for 0.25 mol would show.
- **B4.** A table gives the kinetic energy, in made-up units, of each particle of a small body (5 particles) and a large one (15) before they touch. Three "after" tables are offered. Only one conserves the total and gives both bodies the same average. Find it, say what is wrong with each of the others, and say which body ends with more energy.
- **B5.** Two bodies in an insulated bag: a table of both temperatures every two minutes. Say when thermal equilibrium is reached, in which interval the net transfer was fastest and why (in terms of collisions), which one entry cannot be right, and what the two temperature changes do and do not say about the two bodies.
- **B6.** An egg in hot water: a table gives the energy passing from water to egg, and from egg to water, each second, at six times. Find the net rate at one time and which way it goes; find the row that shows thermal equilibrium and say what is still happening; weigh a student's claim that both columns should read zero at the end.

**Part C: Heat capacity and calorimetry (6.4)**

- **C1.** Hot tea (taken as water) is poured into a cold stainless steel flask liner. Two students each set up the final temperature, each with a different slip (one gives both the same ΔT; one drops a sign and gets a temperature above the tea's). Name each slip, say without new arithmetic which answer cannot be right, then find the final temperature.
- **C2.** A table of c and molar mass for six metals. The stem says that for most metals the molar heat capacity lies between 24 and 27 J/(mol·°C). Find each molar heat capacity; find the one mis-copied c; tell it from beryllium's low value, which is real; estimate the molar mass of an unknown metal from its c.
- **C3.** A student wants the specific heat capacity of ethylene glycol and has a nickel slug of known c, a balance, a thermometer and a foam cup. From a list of eight possible measurements, pick the ones needed and the ones that are not. Then: the slug cools in the air on its way to the cup; say whether the value of c comes out high or low.
- **C4.** A foam cup is calibrated: warm water is poured into cool water in the cup and the final temperature is a little below what the two waters alone would give. Find the cup's heat capacity. Then say what share of the energy the cup took, and weigh the claim "leaving the cup out changes the answer by less than 1 percent."
- **C5.** Four materials for storing heat (water, concrete, cast iron, dry sand), with c and density. Rank them by energy stored per kilogram and per liter for the same temperature rise; say which material changes rank most and why; find the volume of one that stores what a given tank of water stores.

**Part D: Energy of phase changes (6.5)**

- **D1.** A table of energy added against temperature for 0.500 mol of an unknown metal, through its melting. Read the melting point, the molar enthalpy of fusion from the flat and the solid's molar heat capacity from the slope. Two candidates melt within 7 °C of each other (lead, cadmium): say which reading tells them apart, and name the metal.
- **D2.** Three cooling records of samples sold as pure lauric acid. One holds at one temperature, one freezes over a range of several degrees, one shows the temperature rising with nothing heating it and no dip before. Say which is consistent with a pure substance, what the range suggests, and which record cannot be right. Then, for the first: with twice the mass losing energy at the same rate, what changes on the record.
- **D3.** Butane leaves a camping canister as vapor, and the steel canister cools. From the mass of butane that vaporized, find the energy it took in; predict the fall in the canister's temperature if all of it came from the steel and the liquid left inside; compare with a smaller observed fall and say what that shows.
- **D4.** Xenon gas at 25.0 °C is cooled and condensed at its boiling point. Two students set up the energy removed, each with slips (one uses the gas's heat capacity through the flat and multiplies grams by a molar enthalpy; one adds joules to kilojoules and gives a positive total). Name each slip and say how each answer compares with the right one, then find the energy and its sign.

**Part E: Enthalpy of reaction (6.6)**

- **E1.** Three stove fuels (methanol, hexamine, hexane), each with its equation and ΔH as written, two of them written for 2 mol of fuel. Rank them per mole of reaction, per mole of fuel and per gram. Two nearly tie on one basis and are far apart on another: explain.
- **E2.** Bleach and sodium sulfite solution, both 0.500 M, are mixed in five cups in different proportions with the total volume fixed. A table gives the five temperature rises. Read backward: which cup has neither reactant left over, the mole ratio of the reaction, and ΔH per mole of reaction from that cup. Predict the rise for a sixth proportion. Say in a sentence where the energy that warmed the water came from.
- **E3.** Potassium hydroxide pellets are dissolved in a tank. Given the energy released per mole, find the least mass of water that keeps the solution below a set temperature; say which assumption makes the real temperature lower; say what changes, ΔH or ΔT, if twice the water is used.
- **E4.** Sucrose is oxidized to carbon dioxide and water, with ΔH given. Find the mass that supplies a stated energy, then the energy per gram, and weigh a food label's "17 kJ per gram" against it.
- **E5.** An antacid tablet contains magnesium hydroxide and inert filler. It reacts with excess acid in a foam cup; ΔH for the reaction is given. From the temperature rise find the amount that reacted and the mass percent of magnesium hydroxide in the tablet. Say what happens to the answer if some energy escapes, and why the amount of acid need not be known exactly.

**Part F: Bond enthalpies (6.7)**

- **F1.** Three data for hydrazine are offered: ΔH_f of the gas, ΔH_f of the liquid, and ΔH for the gas burning to nitrogen and steam. With the table's other bonds, find the N–N bond enthalpy from each datum that can be used, say which cannot and why, and say why the two results differ a little.
- **F2.** Propene and cyclopropane have the same atoms. From the table, estimate ΔH for turning propene into cyclopropane (the estimate says energy is released). Measured enthalpies of combustion of the two are given: find what they say (energy is absorbed). Weigh the estimate against the data, and say which C–C value the ring does not fit.
- **F3.** Hydrogen and bromine vapor react in steps: Br₂ → 2 Br; Br + H₂ → HBr + H; H + Br₂ → HBr + Br; 2 Br → Br₂. Each step breaks one bond, forms one, or both. Give the sign and size of each step by inspection, say which step can only take energy in and which can only release it, add the two middle steps to get H₂ + Br₂(g) → 2 HBr, and say why this estimate is not an average. Then reconcile it with ΔH_f of HBr(g), which is for liquid bromine.
- **F4.** Ethane reacts with each halogen: C₂H₆ + X₂ → C₂H₅X + HX. From a table of the bonds that change (X–X, C–X, H–X for the four halogens), find each estimate using only the bonds that change, rank the four, say where the sign changes, and say which bond's trend drives the ranking. One C–X value in the table is mis-copied (larger than the one above it): find it from the trend.

**Part G: Enthalpy of formation (6.8)**

- **G1.** A student has copied a table of eight formation values. Three entries cannot be right: a vapor listed below its own liquid, an element in its standard state with a value, and a compound's solid listed above its liquid. Find them and say why each is impossible, without any reference table.
- **G2.** Carbon disulfide is in the table as liquid and as gas. Find the enthalpy of vaporizing it at 25 °C. Its liquid burns to CO₂ and SO₂: find ΔH°. Then, without recomputing, give ΔH° for the vapor burning, and say which of the two releases more and why.
- **G3.** "Green steel": iron(III) oxide is reduced with hydrogen to iron and steam, and ΔH° is measured. Find ΔH_f of iron(III) oxide from it. Then weigh a claim: "the products include steam, which has a large negative ΔH_f, so the reaction must release energy."
- **G4.** A table of ΔH_f for H₂S(g), H₂Se(g) and H₂Te(g). Write the formation equation of each with every element in its standard state; say which compounds release energy when they break down into their elements, and how much per mole; rank the three; say what the trend suggests about the bonds down the group.
- **G5.** A table gives ΔH_f for two solids and their aqueous ions (magnesium sulfate, ammonium chloride). Find ΔH° of dissolving for each; predict the temperature change when a stated mass of one dissolves in a stated mass of water; a student measures a smaller change: give two reasons that fit the direction.

**Part H: Hess's law (6.9)**

- **H1.** Four equations for the oxides of manganese are given, with values. The target, MnO + ½ O₂ → MnO₂, can be reached two ways: by two of them, or by the other two (one needs a factor of ⅓). Do both, show they agree, and say which equations were not needed for each route.
- **H2.** Three equations for sulfur and its oxides are given with their enthalpy changes as symbols (ΔH₁, ΔH₂, ΔH₃). Write ΔH for two targets in symbols. Then: only the signs of the three are known. For which target is the sign certain, and for which is one more number needed?
- **H3.** Sulfur hexafluoride. From a list of five equations with values, choose and arrange the steps that take SF₆(g) apart into gaseous atoms; find the total and the average S–F bond enthalpy; say which listed equation was not needed and why the result is called an average.
- **H4.** Three laboratories report ΔH for P + 3/2 Cl₂ → PCl₃, for PCl₃ + Cl₂ → PCl₅ and for P + 5/2 Cl₂ → PCl₅. The three cannot all be right. Show it. Say why the three numbers alone cannot show which is wrong. A fourth and fifth value (through POCl₃) are added: say which of the three they clear, and what one further measurement would settle the rest.

Ten tasks sit near lesson text and are marked for the originality check:
A4, B2, B6, C4, D4, E5, F2, F3, G5 and H3.

## 5. Beyond the exam

Nothing should need the label. No question asks for the difference between
ΔH and the energy of the system, a calculation of work, a bomb calorimeter,
the term "state function", activation energy or anything about a profile's
peak (Unit 5), entropy or why an endothermic change happens (Unit 9),
lattice energy by name, or a Born–Haber cycle. Part A's question on work is
in words. Part H's route through gaseous atoms is a sequence of steps, which
topic 6.9 asks for.

## 6. Keeping the questions original, and the values honest

- Every question is written fresh from the framework's learning objectives,
  with its own substances, numbers and setting.
- **The writers read no textbook and none of the old course.** They read the
  framework notes, the site's Unit 6 lessons and practice, the avoid list,
  the task table and their part's pool.
- **One pool per part** (`scratchpad/unit6-test/pools.md`), so that two
  parts do not pick the same substance. Where the proposal shared one
  (sulfur dioxide in G and H; magnesium behind E5 and G5), the writers are
  told which part keeps it.
- **An independent originality check**, one per session, compares every new
  question with the teacher's notes, review sheet and practice (never the
  exam) and the site's own lessons and practice. No textbook can be read
  from this session.
- **Values are honest, and none could be looked up.** This session reaches
  no reference table, so every published value in the pools is from memory,
  graded by how sure its author was. So:
  - a stem gives every value its question uses ("use these values"), as the
    lessons say an exam question does;
  - the number check recomputes every answer from the stem's own values, so
    an answer is right for the question as printed;
  - each fact-checker judges every value against what it knows and says how
    sure it is; a value two checkers doubt is replaced or the question says
    "take";
  - bond enthalpies are lesson 6.7's table where it has them; water is 4.18
    J/(g·°C); molar masses are the exam table's, to two decimal places;
  - every value the checkers could not confirm is listed for the teacher.
- Made-up data (a particle table, a rate of energy passing, a laboratory's
  report) say that they are made up, and are consistent with one another.
- **What the practice's fact-checks kept finding**, so that the test does
  not repeat it: a printed step that does not give the printed answer;
  joules set equal to kilojoules; a rule with "always" or "only" that fails
  on the page's own example; "averages" given as the reason for a gap where
  no value is an average; a scoring note a grader could read two ways.

## 7. How each session is made

1. **The avoid list, the task table and the pools** (done).
2. **Writers, in parallel**, one per part, files only.
3. **A number check.** Each writer leaves a script that recomputes every
   number in its part from the question's own data and asserts each against
   the page; the controller runs every script.
4. **The parts' substances compared across both sessions.**
5. **An independent fact-check** per part, by a fresh agent that solves
   every question before reading its answer, scores its own answer with the
   rubric, and applies each rule the page states to every example on it.
6. **The originality check**, one agent per session.
7. **Fixes** (any correction to a number recomputed by the controller), **a
   second check**, a build, the unit and browser tests, and each page
   measured at 412, 390, 360 and 320 px with every answer open, after every
   round of fixes.
8. **A pull request**, merged when its check passes on GitHub.

**Models.** Writers and fact-checkers on Fable; the originality checks and
the fixers on Sonnet.
