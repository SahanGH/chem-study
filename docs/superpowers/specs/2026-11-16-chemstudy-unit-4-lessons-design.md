## 2. What students get

Nine lessons, one per topic of the 2024 framework:

| Topic | Lesson | What it carries |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Introduction for Reactions | Physical and chemical change, seen from the bench; the four kinds of evidence of a reaction, and why each one alone can mislead |
| 4.2 | Net Ionic Equations | A balanced equation for any change, physical or chemical; atoms and charge conserved; balancing; the molecular, complete ionic and net ionic forms, and which substances are written as ions |
| 4.3 | Representations of Reactions | Drawing a reaction with particles that agree with the equation: atoms conserved, particles in the ratio of the coefficients, ions apart in solution, a precipitate as a cluster, the excess reactant still there |
| 4.4 | Physical and Chemical Changes | The same distinction at the particle level: bonds broken and formed, or only the attractions between particles; dissolving a salt as the case that can be argued both ways |
| 4.5 | Stoichiometry | What the coefficients say; from an amount of one substance to an amount of another, through moles; masses, gas volumes and solution volumes; the limiting reactant; percent yield in brief |
| 4.6 | Introduction to Titration | Analyte and titrant; the equivalence point, set by the amounts; the endpoint, which is what you see; a concentration from a titration, with the ratio of the balanced equation |
| 4.7 | Types of Chemical Reactions | Acid–base (protons move), oxidation–reduction (electrons move, shown by oxidation numbers) and precipitation (an insoluble compound forms); combustion; telling them apart |
| 4.8 | Introduction to Acid–Base Reactions | Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases; water as both; conjugate pairs, and comparing their strengths |
| 4.9 | Oxidation–Reduction (Redox) Reactions | Half-reactions; building a balanced redox equation from them by making the electrons cancel; checking atoms and charge |

Each lesson is built like the lessons of the other units, with the same
blocks and the same voice. Each ends with `PracticeNext`, which says practice
is on its way until the Unit 4 practice is published.

Topics 4.3, 4.6 and 4.9 have one essential-knowledge statement each, and 4.1
and 4.4 two. Their lessons are short, not padded.

**What the lessons can lean on.** Units 2 and 3 are on the site: ionic and
covalent bonds (2.1), the forces between particles and ion–dipole attraction
(3.1), the ideal gas law (3.4), molarity (3.7) and particle drawings of
solutions (3.8). Unit 1 is being written at the same time: the mole and molar
mass (1.1). A lesson names such a topic in plain text. Units 6, 8 and 9 are
not on the site, so a lesson that needs pH, a strong or weak acid, or an
energy change says what it needs in a sentence and names the topic it
belongs to.

## 3. Staying inside the framework

**The framework was read from page pictures**, not from extracted text,
before anything else was written (`scratchpad/ced/unit4-framework.md`).
Nothing of substance is lost in the extracted text for this unit, but its
tables pair topics with the wrong skills.

Lessons are written fresh from the framework's learning objectives and
essential knowledge. **No textbook is used.**

**The teacher's guided notes**
(`D:\AP_Chem_Prep\Units\unit04-chemical-reactions\u04-notes.tex`) are read by
the controller, not by the writers: the notes follow the Zumdahl textbook.
Each brief carries what the notes stress:

- one observation is never proof of a reaction: bubbles can be dissolved air,
  a color change can be dilution, and a temperature change comes with
  dissolving too; good evidence is a combination, plus a plausible product;
- a net ionic equation is reached in a fixed order: the full equation, the
  complete ionic equation, the ions that do not change, the net equation;
- a weak acid, a weak base and water are never written as ions;
- different-looking reactions can share one net ionic equation;
- "no reaction" is an answer;
- a particle drawing conserves atoms and charge, keeps dissolved ions apart,
  draws a precipitate as a cluster and leaves the excess reactant in the
  picture;
- a physical change keeps the substance and changes its arrangement; a
  chemical change makes new substances; give the particle-level reason, not
  only the label;
- ratios come from the coefficients, and amounts are compared in moles,
  never in grams;
- write the balanced equation before dividing: a 1 : 1 ratio assumed for an
  acid that gives up two protons halves or doubles the answer;
- the equivalence point is a matter of amounts; the endpoint is what the
  indicator shows;
- the species that is oxidized loses electrons and its oxidation number
  rises.

The notes do not teach balancing an equation, conjugate pairs,
half-reactions, mass-to-mass stoichiometry or titration by anything but a
strong acid and a strong base. Those parts rest on the framework alone.

**What the framework leaves out of Unit 4** (three statements):

| Topic | Not assessed | In the lessons |
|---|---|---|
| 4.7 | The meaning of "reducing agent" and "oxidizing agent" | Named once, in a sentence beginning "**Beyond the exam:**" in lesson 4.7. Lessons speak of the species that is oxidized and the species that is reduced. |
| 4.7 | Memorizing solubility rules beyond the one the framework states | Lessons teach that one rule. Any other solubility is given in the question, and one sentence says the exam does the same. |
| 4.8 | Lewis acids and bases | Named once, in a "Beyond the exam" sentence in lesson 4.8. |

**Things the framework does not name but the exam uses**, taught without a
"Beyond the exam" label:

- balancing an equation by inspection, state symbols, and the term
  "spectator ion" (4.2);
- which substances are written as separate ions: dissolved ionic compounds,
  and strong acids and strong bases. Lesson 4.2 lists the common strong acids
  and bases once and says topic 8.2 returns to them; every question still
  says whether an acid or base is strong or weak (4.2);
- the limiting reactant and the reactant in excess (4.5);
- the rules for assigning oxidation numbers (4.7);
- the indicator as the usual source of an endpoint (4.6);
- that the stronger acid of two has the weaker conjugate base (4.8).

**Taught briefly, and said to be less common on the exam:** percent yield
(4.5); balancing a half-reaction in acidic solution with water and H⁺ (4.9).

**Left out of the lessons:** titration curves, pH at the equivalence point
and the choice of indicator (topic 8.5, named in a sentence); balancing in
basic solution; the activity series; the classification into synthesis,
decomposition and replacement (the framework's three kinds are acid–base,
redox and precipitation); gravimetric analysis as a named method;
combustion analysis; hydrates; dilution as a routine of its own (it is in
topic 3.7).

**The equation sheet** gives, for this unit, $n = m/M$, the molarity
relation, $PV = nRT$ and 22.4 L/mol at STP. It gives no solubility rules, no
rules for oxidation numbers, no titration formula and nothing for a limiting
reactant or a yield. Lessons say so where it matters.

**Values.**

- Atomic masses are the AP exam's table's, to the figures it prints.
- A reaction a lesson describes is one that happens as described: the
  products, the colors, the precipitates and which species is in excess are
  real. Where a lesson states that a compound is insoluble, it is.
- Constants are the site's: $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K), 22.4 L/mol at STP.
- The proton is written H⁺ where a lesson speaks of proton transfer in
  general, as the framework does, and H₃O⁺ where the acid's reaction with
  water is the point (4.8).

## 4. Keeping the lessons original

- **Avoid lists before any brief is written.** Two were made
  (`scratchpad/unit4/`): every worked example, practice item and exercise of
  both Tro books in the chapters that cover Unit 4 (about 330 entries), with
  the stock choices of textbooks in general; and the 84 examples of the
  teacher's notes with the reactions and numbers of the teacher's practice.
  The brief-writers get the lists, never the sources.
- **The stock examples are out**, among them: silver nitrate with sodium
  chloride and lead(II) nitrate with potassium iodide for precipitation;
  hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide for neutralization and titration;
  zinc or magnesium with copper(II) ions, and iron(II) with permanganate or
  dichromate, for redox; the combustion of methane, propane, butane, octane
  and ethanol; ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen, and iron(III) oxide with
  carbon monoxide, for stoichiometry; sandwiches and pizzas for the limiting
  reactant; table salt and sugar dissolving, dry ice and boiling water for
  physical change.
- **Every scenario is searched for before it is used**: the briefs are
  checked for originality against both Tro books, the teacher's notes and
  practice, and the site's own lessons before the plan is committed, and
  every replacement is searched for before it goes in.
- **The finished prose is checked again.**

Some things cannot be new: the net ionic equation of every strong acid with
every strong base is the same, and the rules for oxidation numbers are the
rules. The lessons state those in their own words, with their own examples
around them.

## 5. Checks that test something

Written into every brief, as for Unit 1:

- A Check is an application. It is never answered by the sentence, caption
  or key idea just above it.
- The correct option is not always first, and is not the longest.
- For a numeric Check, the answer a common wrong method gives lies outside
  the grader's 2 percent: a 1 : 1 ratio where the equation says otherwise,
  grams compared where moles were needed, the wrong reactant taken as
  limiting, milliliters used as liters.
- An answer option is never math alone. Equations offered as options are
  lettered A to D in the question.
- "Only" and "every" are checked by name.

## 6. Diagrams

### 6.1 Interactive diagrams (Svelte, like the other units)

**Limiting reactant explorer (lesson 4.5).**

- One real reaction of two reactants with unequal coefficients, chosen clear
  of the sources when the briefs were checked: nitrogen monoxide with
  fluorine, 2 NO + F₂ → 2 NOF. With fluorine in excess a few percent of the
  nitrosyl fluoride can react further; the diagram leaves that out and says
  so, and nothing calls nitrosyl fluoride the only product.
- The student chooses how many particles of each reactant go in, in steps
  that can react completely.
- Two boxes of particles, before and after: the product, and whatever is
  left of the reactant in excess.
- Under them, a table of the amounts before, the change and the amounts
  after, and a sentence naming the limiting reactant and why: it would be
  used up first.
- Every count is exact: atoms are conserved between the two boxes.
- The counts and the sentences come from a tested module,
  `src/lib/limiting-reactant.ts`.

**Titration explorer (lesson 4.6).**

- A flask of an acid of known volume and a buret of a base of known
  concentration. The student chooses one of two acids, one that gives up one
  proton per molecule (lactic acid) and one that gives up two (tartaric
  acid), at the same concentration.
- A slider adds titrant.
- One graph: the amount of acidic protons still to be neutralized and the
  amount of hydroxide in excess, in millimoles, against the volume added.
  The equivalence point is where the first reaches zero. Acidic protons,
  not "acid left": partway through, molecules of the acid with two protons
  have lost none, one or both.
- A drawn flask with its indicator, bromothymol blue: yellow before the
  equivalence point, blue at it and past it. The real indicator begins to
  shade toward green a few drops early (a calculation of the pH near each
  equivalence point shows it); the slider's steps are wider than that, and
  the lesson says that the endpoint is where the color change is seen, close
  to the equivalence point but not the same thing.
- The readout says where the titration stands (before, at or past the
  equivalence point), with the amounts.
- What it shows: the equivalence point is set by amounts and by the ratio in
  the balanced equation; the acid with two protons needs twice the base.
- No pH and no titration curve: those belong to topic 8.5.
- Every number comes from a tested module, `src/lib/titration.ts`.

Both follow the rules of the existing diagrams: drawn in their starting
state when the site is built; readable without JavaScript and in print;
usable by keyboard; a text description for screen readers; drawn text of
12 px or more from a 412 px phone up; every number shown comes from a module
with unit tests.

## 7. How it is made

The Unit 7 pipeline:

1. **The framework, from page pictures.** Done.
2. **Briefs**, one per lesson, each with a number-check script.
3. **A fact-check of the briefs** on Fable 5.2, and **an originality check**
   on Sonnet. Replacements are searched for before they are used.
4. **The plan**, with this design, in one pull request.
5. **The code, test-first**, each diagram by one builder that runs unit
   tests only; then a TypeScript review and a code review.
6. **Writers, in parallel**, one per lesson, files only.
7. **One build and one test run**, and a look at each lesson at 412, 390,
   360 and 320 px with every answer open, and at each figure.
8. **A fact-check per lesson** on Fable 5.2, by a fresh agent that solves
   before it reads, and **an originality check** per group on Sonnet.
9. **Fixes** by the lesson's own writer, then the build and tests again,
   then **a second check** of what changed.
10. **The pull request**, left for the teacher to merge.

**Beside Unit 1.** Unit 4 is written in its own working copy of the
repository (a git worktree), on branches that start from `main`, so that the
two units never share a working folder. Only one build or browser-test run
goes at a time on the machine.
