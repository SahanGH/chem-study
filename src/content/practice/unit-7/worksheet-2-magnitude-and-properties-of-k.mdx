Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through the worksheet: when you combine reactions, their equilibrium constants multiply where their enthalpy changes add.

You will need these four rules for what happens to $K$ when the equation changes.

- Reverse the reaction: the new constant is $1/K$.
- Multiply every coefficient by $n$: the new constant is $K^n$.
- Divide every coefficient by $n$: the new constant is $K^{1/n}$.
- Add two reactions: the new constant is $K_1K_2$.

For a given equation, only a change in temperature changes $K$.

<Question number={2} topic="7.5">

A reaction has $K = 10^{35}$, yet mixing the reactants produces no observable change over several hours.

<Part label="a">

Is the value of $K$ necessarily wrong?

<Answer>

No.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain.

<Answer>

$K$ describes only where equilibrium lies, not the rate of getting there. A very high activation energy can make the reaction immeasurably slow, so the mixture stays far from equilibrium while $K$ is still right about where it is headed. (Unit 9 calls this kinetic control.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

What single change would speed it up without altering $K$?

<Answer>

Adding a catalyst.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={4} topic="7.6">

Combining reactions *adds* their $\Delta H$ values (Hess's law, topic 6.9). Here, combining reactions *multiplies* their $K$ values. Explain why the two behave differently.

<Answer>

Enthalpy is an additive quantity: energies of successive steps sum. An equilibrium constant is a ratio of concentration (or pressure) terms raised to powers, and combining reactions multiplies those ratios together. Looking ahead: Unit 9 gives the deeper link. The standard free energy change, $\Delta G^\circ$, adds when reactions are combined, as $\Delta H$ does, and at a fixed temperature it is proportional to $\ln K$. Adding logarithms corresponds to multiplying the constants themselves.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="7.6">

At a given temperature, $K = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$ for $\ce{A + B <=> C}$.

<Part label="a">

$K$ for $\ce{C <=> A + B}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$4.0 \times 10^{2}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$K$ for $\ce{2A + 2B <=> 2C}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
(2.5 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 6.25 \times 10^{-6}
$$

That is $6.3 \times 10^{-6}$ to two significant figures.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Which side is favored for the original reaction?

<Answer>

The reactants, A and B ($K$ is less than 1).

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Which side is favored for the reverse?

<Answer>

The products of the reversed equation, which are again A and B. Reversing the equation changes the labels, not the mixture.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="7.6">

A student claims that doubling the concentration of a reactant doubles $K$. Explain what actually happens.

<Answer>

$K$ does not change. Suppose the mixture was at equilibrium. Doubling a reactant concentration changes $Q$, making it smaller than $K$, so the reaction makes more products until $Q$ equals $K$ again. The concentrations at the new equilibrium differ from the old ones, but their combination in the $K$ expression gives the identical value. Only a change in temperature alters $K$ itself.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="7.5">

Two reactions at the same temperature have $K_1 = 5 \times 10^{-4}$ and $K_2 = 5 \times 10^{4}$.

<Part label="a">

Which lies further toward products?

<Answer>

The second.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

If the second reaction is reversed, how does its $K$ compare with $K_1$?

<Answer>

Reversing gives

$$
1/(5 \times 10^{4}) = 2 \times 10^{-5}
$$

That is smaller than $K_1$. Both constants are far below 1, so both reactions favor their reactants. If the two equations have the same form, the reversed second reaction lies even further toward its reactants than the first does.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Does either value tell you which reaction is faster?

<Answer>

No.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
