Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through these questions: only temperature changes the value of $K$.

You will need these rules.

- Add a reactant that is a gas or is dissolved: the equilibrium shifts right.
- Add a product that is a gas or is dissolved: it shifts left.
- Compress the mixture: it shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
- Add an inert gas at constant volume: no shift.
- Add a catalyst: no shift.
- Temperature is the only change that alters $K$.

<Question number={2} topic="7.9">

Explain, in terms of partial pressures, why adding an inert gas at constant volume causes no shift.

<Answer>

The inert gas raises the total pressure but occupies the same container, so the partial pressure, and the concentration, of every reacting species is unchanged. Since $Q$ depends only on those, $Q$ is unchanged and there is nothing to drive a shift.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="7.9">

Explain why compressing the vessel shifts this equilibrium to the right.

$$
\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}
$$

<Answer>

Reducing the volume raises every concentration, but the reactant side has 4 moles of gas against 2 on the product side. The denominator of $Q$ is affected more strongly, so $Q$ falls below $K$ and the system responds by shifting toward the side with fewer gas molecules.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Part label="e">

Why is temperature the only disturbance that changes $K$?

<Answer>

Every other disturbance that causes a shift changes concentrations or partial pressures, which alters $Q$; the system then shifts until $Q$ returns to the same $K$. Temperature changes the constant itself, so the system settles at a mixture that fits a different $K$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={5} topic="7.10">

For this reaction, $K = 4.5$.

$$
\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}
$$

For each mixture, calculate $Q$ and state the direction of change.

<Part label="a">

$$
[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = [\ce{HI}] = 0.50\ \text{M}
$$

<Answer>

$Q = 1.0$; the reaction runs toward products

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$[\ce{H2}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{I2}] = 0.30\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.40)^2}{(0.40)(0.30)} = \frac{0.16}{0.12} \\
&= \mathbf{1.3}
\end{aligned}
$$

$Q < K$, so the reaction runs toward **products**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.10\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.30\ \text{M}$

<Answer>

$Q = 9.0$; the reaction runs toward reactants

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

$[\ce{H2}] = 0.20\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{I2}] = 0.20\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.424\ \text{M}$

<Answer>

$Q = 4.5$; already at equilibrium

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={6} topic="7.10">

Extra product is added to a system at equilibrium. Explain the resulting shift using $Q$ rather than Le Châtelier's principle.

<Answer>

Adding a product that is a gas or is dissolved increases the numerator of $Q$ while $K$ is unchanged, so $Q$ rises above $K$. A mixture with $Q > K$ holds too many products relative to equilibrium, so the reverse reaction dominates until $Q$ falls back to $K$.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="7.10">

When is comparing $Q$ with $K$ the right tool and Le Châtelier's principle the wrong one?

<Answer>

When the mixture was never at equilibrium: for example, substances mixed in a flask at arbitrary starting concentrations. Le Châtelier's principle describes only the response to *disturbing* an existing equilibrium, whereas $Q$ versus $K$ predicts the direction of change for any mixture whatsoever.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={8} topic="7.8">

A sealed flask is drawn at three successive times, each picture showing the particles in it. The first two pictures differ. The second and third are identical.

<Part label="a">

What can you conclude?

<Answer>

Equilibrium was reached by the second picture.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

What must be true of the total number of atoms of each element across all three?

<Answer>

It is conserved.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

If a diagram of the mixture at equilibrium shows 8 product particles and 2 reactant particles for $\ce{A <=> B}$, estimate $K$.

<Answer>

About 4: $K = [\ce{B}]/[\ce{A}] = 8/2$. Both kinds of particle share one volume, and the equation has one particle on each side, so the volume cancels and the counts can stand in for the concentrations.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why is "the reaction stopped" the wrong description of the last two pictures?

<Answer>

Both directions continue at equal rates. The particle counts hold steady because formation and consumption are balanced, not because motion or reaction has ceased.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={9} topic="7.9">

For this reaction, $\Delta H = -198\ \text{kJ}$.

$$
\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}
$$

A manufacturer wants to maximize the yield of $\ce{SO3}$. Recommend a temperature and a pressure, and note the practical tension.

<Answer>

Compressing the gases into a smaller volume raises the pressure and favors the product side, which has fewer moles of gas (2 against 3), so a high pressure helps. The forward reaction is exothermic, so a *low* temperature increases $K$ and the yield. The tension is that a low temperature also makes the reaction very slow, so in practice a moderate temperature is used together with a catalyst. That accepts a somewhat lower equilibrium yield in exchange for reaching equilibrium in a reasonable time.

</Answer>

</Question>
