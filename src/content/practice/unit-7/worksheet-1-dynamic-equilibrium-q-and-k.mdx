Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through the worksheet: at equilibrium the forward and reverse *rates* are equal, not the amounts.

You will need this relationship. Each concentration is raised to its coefficient in the balanced equation, and pure solids and pure liquids are left out.

$$
K = \frac{[\text{products}]^{\text{coef}}}{[\text{reactants}]^{\text{coef}}}
$$

$Q$ has the same form, with the concentrations at any moment.

## Questions

<Question number={1} topic="7.1">

Describe what is happening at equilibrium at the particle level, and state precisely what is equal.

<Answer>

Both the forward and reverse reactions continue at the particle level: molecules keep reacting in both directions. What is equal is the *rate* of the forward and reverse reactions, so every product formed is matched by one converting back. Concentrations therefore stop changing, though they are generally not equal to one another.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="7.1">

A student looking at a graph of concentration against time says, "The reactant and product curves level off at the same height, so the concentrations are equal at equilibrium." Identify both errors.

<Answer>

The curves generally level off at *different* heights: $K$ fixes the value of the $K$ expression, not the concentrations themselves. And even if they happened to coincide, that would be a coincidence rather than a definition. Equilibrium is defined by equal rates, not equal concentrations.

</Answer>

</Question>

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={5} topic="7.3">

Why are pure solids and pure liquids omitted, and what decides whether water appears?

<Answer>

A pure solid or liquid has a fixed density and molar volume, so its "concentration" does not change as the reaction proceeds. Including it would merely multiply $K$ by a constant. Water is omitted when it is a liquid, $\ce{H2O(l)}$, whether the pure liquid or the solvent, but included when it is a gas, $\ce{H2O(g)}$. The state symbol decides, so it must be read.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="7.4">

Calculate $K$ from each set of equilibrium concentrations.

<Part label="a">

$\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}$ with $[\ce{H2}] = 0.20\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{I2}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.60\ \text{M}$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
K &= \frac{(0.60)^2}{(0.20)(0.40)} = \frac{0.36}{0.080} \\
&= \mathbf{4.5}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$ with $[\ce{N2}] = 0.50\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{H2}] = 0.30\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{NH3}] = 0.20\ \text{M}$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
K &= \frac{(0.20)^2}{(0.50)(0.30)^3} = \frac{0.040}{0.0135} \\
&= \mathbf{3.0}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

In part (b), what is the most likely arithmetic slip?

<Answer>

Failing to cube the hydrogen concentration: using 0.30 or 0.90 instead of $(0.30)^3 = 0.027$, which changes the answer by a factor of tens.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="7.3">

$Q$ and $K$.

<Part label="a">

How does the expression for $Q$ differ from that for $K$?

<Answer>

It does not: the algebraic form is identical. The difference is the concentrations substituted. $Q$ uses values at any moment, $K$ uses equilibrium values.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$K = 4.5$ for $\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}$. A vessel holds $[\ce{H2}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{I2}] = 0.30\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$. Calculate $Q$ and state the direction of change.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.40)^2}{(0.40)(0.30)} = \frac{0.16}{0.12} \\
&= \mathbf{1.3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $Q < K$, there are too few products, so the reaction proceeds to the **right**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Repeat for $[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.10\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.30\ \text{M}$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.30)^2}{(0.10)(0.10)} = \frac{0.090}{0.010} \\
&= \mathbf{9.0}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $Q > K$, there are too many products, so the reaction proceeds to the **left**.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="Converting between the two constants" topic="7.3" beyond>

This question is about $K_p$ and $K_c$. Use $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K).

<Part label="a">

Write the relationship.

<Answer>

$K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For $\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$, $\Delta n$ is: <Blank />

<Answer>

$-2$: 2 mol of gas on the product side minus 4 mol of gas on the reactant side.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

For the reaction in part (b), $K_c = 0.50$ at 500 K. Find $K_p$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_p &= 0.50[(0.08206)(500)]^{-2} \\
&= \frac{0.50}{(41.03)^2} \\
&= \mathbf{3.0 \times 10^{-4}}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

For which reactions are $K_p$ and $K_c$ numerically equal?

<Answer>

Those with $\Delta n = 0$: the same number of moles of gas on each side.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
