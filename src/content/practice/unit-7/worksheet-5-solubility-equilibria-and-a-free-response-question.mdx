Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through the solubility questions: $K_{sp}$ is a constant, and solubility is an amount that can change.

You will need these relationships. In them, $s$ is the molar solubility of the salt in pure water.

For a salt $\ce{MX}$:

$$
K_{sp} = s^2
$$

For a salt $\ce{MX2}$ or $\ce{M2X}$:

$$
K_{sp} = 4s^3
$$

Adding a common ion to a saturated solution raises $Q$ above $K_{sp}$, so less of the salt stays dissolved.

Use these values of $K_{sp}$, at 25 °C.

| Salt | $K_{sp}$ |
|---|---|
| $\ce{AgCl}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| $\ce{BaSO4}$ | $1.1 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| $\ce{CaF2}$ | $3.5 \times 10^{-11}$ |
| $\ce{PbI2}$ | $9.8 \times 10^{-9}$ |
| $\ce{Ag2CrO4}$ | $1.1 \times 10^{-12}$ |
| $\ce{BaCrO4}$ | $1.2 \times 10^{-10}$ |

The last question is a free-response question, the kind the AP exam asks. Write out every part first, then open the answers. Its scoring rubric comes after the last part, so you can score yourself.

## Questions

<Question number={1} topic="7.11">

Write the dissolving equation and the $K_{sp}$ expression for each salt.

<Part label="a">

$\ce{AgCl}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{AgCl(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}][\ce{Cl-}]
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{CaF2}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CaF2(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Ca^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_{sp} = [\ce{Ca^2+}][\ce{F-}]^2
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$\ce{Ag2CrO4}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ag2CrO4(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{2Ag+(aq) + CrO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}]^2[\ce{CrO4^2-}]
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why does the solid never appear in the expression?

<Answer>

It is a pure solid, and the concentration of a pure solid does not depend on how much of it there is.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} topic="7.11">

Calculate the molar solubility of each salt in pure water.

<Part label="a">

$\ce{AgCl}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$1.3 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{BaSO4}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$\ce{CaF2}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
4s^3 &= 3.5 \times 10^{-11} \\
s^3 &= 8.8 \times 10^{-12} \\
s &= \mathbf{2.1 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

$\ce{PbI2}$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$1.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="7.11">

$\ce{CaF2}$ has $K_{sp} = 3.5 \times 10^{-11}$. $\ce{BaCrO4}$ has $K_{sp} = 1.2 \times 10^{-10}$.

<Part label="a">

Which has the larger $K_{sp}$?

<Answer>

$\ce{BaCrO4}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate both molar solubilities.

<Answer>

$\ce{CaF2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \sqrt[3]{8.8 \times 10^{-12}} \\
&= 2.1 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\ce{BaCrO4}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \sqrt{1.2 \times 10^{-10}} \\
&= 1.1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Which is actually more soluble, and by what factor?

<Answer>

$\ce{CaF2}$, about 19 times:

$$
\frac{2.1 \times 10^{-4}}{1.1 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 19
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

State the rule this illustrates.

<Answer>

$K_{sp}$ values may be compared directly only for salts that produce the same total number of ions, because otherwise $s$ enters the expression raised to different powers.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} topic="7.12">

This question is about the common-ion effect.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the solubility of $\ce{AgCl}$ in 0.10 M $\ce{NaCl}$.

<Answer>

$$
[\ce{Cl-}] \approx 0.10\ \text{M}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \frac{K_{sp}}{[\ce{Cl-}]} \\
&= \frac{1.8 \times 10^{-10}}{0.10} \\
&= \mathbf{1.8 \times 10^{-9}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: what was left out is $s$, $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$ M. That is about 0.000002 percent of 0.10 M, far under 5 percent.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

By what factor is this solubility lower than the solubility in pure water?

<Answer>

About 7000:

$$
\frac{1.3 \times 10^{-5}}{1.8 \times 10^{-9}} \approx 7000
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain the effect using Le Châtelier's principle.

<Answer>

The added $\ce{Cl-}$ is a product of the dissolving equation, so adding it shifts the equilibrium left and less $\ce{AgCl}$ dissolves.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain the same effect using $Q$.

<Answer>

Start from a saturated solution of $\ce{AgCl}$, where $Q = K_{sp}$. Raising $[\ce{Cl-}]$ raises $Q$ above $K_{sp}$. With $Q > K_{sp}$ the system shifts toward the reactant side, here toward undissolved solid, until $Q$ falls back to $K_{sp}$, leaving less dissolved.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

What happened to $K_{sp}$?

<Answer>

Nothing.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="7.12">

Calculate the solubility of $\ce{CaF2}$ in 0.025 M $\ce{NaF}$, and state the assumption you make.

<Answer>

Assume $[\ce{F-}] \approx 0.025\ \text{M}$, since the salt that dissolves contributes negligibly beside the $\ce{NaF}$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
3.5 \times 10^{-11} &= s(0.025)^2 \\
s &= \mathbf{5.6 \times 10^{-8}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: what was left out is $2s$, $1.1 \times 10^{-7}$ M. That is about 0.0004 percent of 0.025 M, far under 5 percent.

In pure water the solubility is $2.1 \times 10^{-4}$ M, nearly 4000 times as much.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="7.12">

A student says, "The common ion lowers $K_{sp}$, which is why less dissolves." Rewrite the sentence correctly.

<Answer>

The common ion does not change $K_{sp}$, which depends only on temperature. It shifts the equilibrium *position*: more of the common ion and correspondingly less of the other ion, with the ion concentrations, each raised to its coefficient, still multiplying out to the same $K_{sp}$. So less of the salt dissolves.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="Hydrogen and iodine" topic="7.3, 7.7, 7.9, 7.10" points={10}>

At 700 K, $K = 50.0$ for this reaction:

$$
\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}
$$

<Part label="a">

Write the equilibrium expression.

<Answer>

$$
K = \frac{[\ce{HI}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A flask initially contains 1.00 M $\ce{H2}$ and 1.00 M $\ce{I2}$. Set up an ICE table and find all three equilibrium concentrations.

<Answer>

Every value is in M. The rows are labeled I, C and E, for Initial, Change and Equilibrium:

| | H₂ | I₂ | HI |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| C | −x | −x | +2x |
| E | 1.00 − x | 1.00 − x | 2x |

$$
\begin{aligned}
K &= \frac{(2x)^2}{(1.00 - x)^2} = 50.0 \\
\frac{2x}{1.00 - x} &= 7.07 \\
x &= 0.780
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] &= \mathbf{0.220}\ \text{M} \\
[\ce{HI}] &= \mathbf{1.56}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why the quadratic formula was not required here.

<Answer>

The top of the expression is a perfect square, and so is the bottom, because the two reactants start equal. Taking the square root of both sides turns it into a linear equation in $x$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Verify your answer by substituting back into $K$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(1.56)^2}{(0.220)^2} &= \frac{2.434}{0.0484} \\
&= \mathbf{50.3}
\end{aligned}
$$

This agrees with 50.0 to rounding.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

A second flask at the same temperature contains $[\ce{H2}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{I2}] = 0.30\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{HI}] = 0.40\ \text{M}$. Calculate $Q$ and predict the direction of change.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.40)^2}{(0.40)(0.30)} \\
&= 1.3
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $Q < K$, the reaction proceeds to the **right** until $Q$ rises to 50.0.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="f">

State the effect on $K$ of (i) adding more $\ce{H2}$ to the equilibrium mixture of part (b), and (ii) raising the temperature, given that the forward reaction is exothermic.

<Answer>

(i) None. Adding $\ce{H2}$ changes $Q$, and the system shifts right until $Q$ returns to the same $K$.

(ii) $K$ decreases, because for an exothermic forward reaction heat behaves as a product and adding it pushes the equilibrium toward reactants.

</Answer>

</Part>
