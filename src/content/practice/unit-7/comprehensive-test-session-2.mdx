It covers topics 7.7 to 7.12: calculating equilibrium concentrations, particle pictures of equilibrium, Le Châtelier's principle and $Q$, and solubility and the common-ion effect. Session 1 covers reversible reactions, the reaction quotient and the equilibrium constant, calculating $K$, and the size and rules of $K$.

There are 21 questions worth 111 points. Give yourself about 110 minutes. You may use a calculator. Write out each answer in full before you open the worked answer and the scoring rubric.

## Part E: Calculating equilibrium concentrations

In this part you start from a mixture and a value of $K$ and work out what is there at equilibrium. First find which way the mixture runs, from $Q$ against $K$. Then set up a table with an unknown $x$ and solve it: by a square root, by the small-x shortcut with its 5 percent check, or, in one question, by the quadratic formula.

<Question number={1} title="Which way three mixtures run" topic="7.7" points={5}>

For the made-up reaction

$$
\ce{2A(g) + B(g) <=> 2G(g)}
$$

$K_c$ = 40.0 at a certain temperature. Three sealed flasks are held at that temperature. Each was filled with a different mixture of the three gases. The table gives the concentrations just after filling.

| Flask | $[\ce{A}]$ (M) | $[\ce{B}]$ (M) | $[\ce{G}]$ (M) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.100 | 0.200 | 0.400 |
| 2 | 0.200 | 0.100 | 0.400 |
| 3 | 0.300 | 0.0500 | 0.300 |

<Part label="a">

Write the expression for $Q_c$.

<Answer>

Product on top, reactants on the bottom, each raised to its coefficient:

$$
Q_c = \frac{[\ce{G}]^2}{[\ce{A}]^2[\ce{B}]}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate $Q_c$ for each flask. For each, state whether the mixture runs toward products, runs toward reactants, or is already at equilibrium, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

Flask 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_c &= \frac{(0.400)^2}{(0.100)^2(0.200)} \\
&= \frac{0.160}{0.00200} \\
&= \mathbf{80.0}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is greater than 40.0: there is too much G for equilibrium. Flask 1 **runs toward reactants**, using up G, until $Q_c$ has fallen to 40.0.

Flask 2:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_c &= \frac{(0.400)^2}{(0.200)^2(0.100)} \\
&= \frac{0.160}{0.00400} \\
&= \mathbf{40.0}
\end{aligned}
$$

That equals $K_c$, so flask 2 is **already at equilibrium**. Both reactions keep running, at the same rate, and the concentrations stay as they are.

Flask 3:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_c &= \frac{(0.300)^2}{(0.300)^2(0.0500)} \\
&= \frac{0.0900}{0.00450} \\
&= \mathbf{20.0}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is less than 40.0: there is too little G. Flask 3 **runs toward products**, until $Q_c$ has risen to 40.0.

Flasks 1 and 2 hold the same concentration of G and simply swap A and B. They behave differently because $[\ce{A}]$ is squared and $[\ce{B}]$ is not. Without the exponents, every flask gives 20.0, and flasks 1 and 2 would wrongly seem to run toward products.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

For flask 1, write the change row of a table of initial, change and equilibrium concentrations, in terms of $x$, for A, B and G.

<Answer>

Flask 1 runs toward reactants, so A and B are formed (plus) and G is used up (minus). The sizes follow the coefficients 2, 1 and 2: **+2x for A, +x for B and −2x for G**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for the expression with $[\ce{G}]$ squared on top and $[\ce{A}]$ squared on the bottom. An expression without the exponents earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for each flask: the value of $Q_c$ (80.0, 40.0, 20.0) and the direction that follows from comparing it with 40.0 (toward reactants, at equilibrium, toward products). A direction with no $Q_c$ earns 0 points for that flask. An expression without the exponents gives 20.0 for every flask and earns 0 points here.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for +2x for A, +x for B and −2x for G. The signs of a reaction running forward, or a plain x for every gas, earn 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Methanol to dimethyl ether" topic="7.7" points={6}>

Over a hot catalyst, two molecules of methanol vapor join and give off one molecule of water, forming dimethyl ether, $\ce{CH3OCH3}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2CH3OH(g) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{CH3OCH3(g) + H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

At a certain temperature $K_c$ = 9.00. A sealed 2.00 L vessel is filled with 0.700 mol of methanol and nothing else, and held at that temperature. (The values are made up for practice.)

<Part label="a">

State which way the reaction runs, and set up a table of initial, change and equilibrium concentrations.

<Answer>

The starting concentration of methanol:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{CH3OH}]_0 &= \frac{0.700\ \text{mol}}{2.00\ \text{L}} \\
&= 0.350\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

There are no products, so $Q_c$ = 0, which is less than $K_c$. The reaction **runs toward products**.

Every value is in M. The rows are labeled I, C and E, for Initial, Change and Equilibrium:

| | CH₃OH | CH₃OCH₃ | H₂O |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.350 | 0 | 0 |
| C | −2x | +x | +x |
| E | 0.350 − 2x | x | x |

Methanol's coefficient is 2, so it falls by $2x$ while each product rises by $x$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate $x$.

<Answer>

Put the equilibrium row into the $K_c$ expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_c &= \frac{[\ce{CH3OCH3}][\ce{H2O}]}{[\ce{CH3OH}]^2} \\
&= \frac{x^2}{(0.350 - 2x)^2} = 9.00
\end{aligned}
$$

The top and the bottom are both squares, so take the square root of both sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x}{0.350 - 2x} &= \sqrt{9.00} \\
&= 3.00
\end{aligned}
$$

Use the positive root. The negative one, $-3.00$, gives $x$ = 0.210, which would make $[\ce{CH3OH}]$ negative ($0.350 - 0.420$).

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 3.00(0.350 - 2x) \\
x &= 1.050 - 6.00x \\
7.00x &= 1.050 \\
x &= \mathbf{0.150}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the three concentrations at equilibrium, and show that they give $K_c$ back.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{CH3OCH3}] &= [\ce{H2O}] \\
&= \mathbf{0.150}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{CH3OH}] &= 0.350 - 2(0.150) \\
&= \mathbf{0.050}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{(0.150)(0.150)}{(0.050)^2} \\
&= \frac{0.0225}{0.0025} = 9.00
\end{aligned}
$$

The answers give $K_c$ back. Six sevenths of the methanol has reacted.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the direction, toward products, because $Q_c$ = 0 (no products) is less than $K_c$; 1 point for the table with 0.350 M methanol at the start, −2x for methanol and +x for each product. A change row of −x for methanol earns 0 points for the table.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the expression in $x$ with the square root of both sides taken (3.00 on the right); 1 point for $x$ = 0.150. Expanding and solving the full quadratic instead earns both points. A change row of −x for methanol, carried through, gives $x$ = 0.2625 and earns the first point only.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for all three concentrations (0.150 M, 0.150 M and 0.050 M methanol); 1 point for putting them into the $K_c$ expression and getting 9.00. Giving 0.150 M as the methanol concentration loses the first point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Bromine atoms in a hot vessel" topic="7.7" points={6}>

At high temperatures a small share of bromine molecules split into atoms:

$$
\ce{Br2(g) <=> 2Br(g)}
$$

At a certain high temperature $K_c = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}$. A sealed 3.00 L quartz vessel holds 0.150 mol of $\ce{Br2}$ and nothing else, and is heated to that temperature. (The values are made up for practice.)

<Part label="a">

Set up a table of initial, change and equilibrium concentrations, and write $K_c$ in terms of $x$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Br2}]_0 &= \frac{0.150\ \text{mol}}{3.00\ \text{L}} \\
&= 0.0500\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

There are no bromine atoms at the start, so $Q_c$ = 0 and the reaction runs forward.

Every value is in M. The rows are labeled I, C and E, for Initial, Change and Equilibrium:

| | Br₂ | Br |
|---|---|---|
| I | 0.0500 | 0 |
| C | −x | +2x |
| E | 0.0500 − x | 2x |

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_c &= \frac{[\ce{Br}]^2}{[\ce{Br2}]} \\
&= \frac{(2x)^2}{0.0500 - x} \\
&= 1.2 \times 10^{-5}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$K_c$ is very small, so use the small-x approximation to calculate $x$. Say where $x$ is left out.

<Answer>

$x$ is left out only where it is subtracted from 0.0500, so $0.0500 - x$ becomes 0.0500. The $(2x)^2$ on top stands alone and stays. It is $4x^2$.

$$
\frac{4x^2}{0.0500} = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2 &= \frac{(1.2 \times 10^{-5})(0.0500)}{4} \\
&= 1.5 \times 10^{-7} \\
x &= \mathbf{3.87 \times 10^{-4}}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Carry out the 5 percent check, and state what it shows.

<Answer>

What was left out is $x$, and it was left out of the starting concentration of $\ce{Br2}$, 0.0500 M:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{3.87 \times 10^{-4}}{0.0500} \times 100\% \\
&= \mathbf{0.77\%}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is **under 5 percent**, so leaving $x$ out was justified.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate $[\ce{Br}]$ and $[\ce{Br2}]$ at equilibrium, and show that they give $K_c$ back.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Br}] &= 2(3.87 \times 10^{-4}) \\
&= \mathbf{7.7 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Br2}] &= 0.0500 - 0.000387 \\
&= \mathbf{0.0496}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{(7.7 \times 10^{-4})^2}{0.0496} \\
&= 1.2 \times 10^{-5}
\end{aligned}
$$

The answers give $K_c$ back. Fewer than 1 in 100 of the bromine molecules have split.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the table: 0.0500 M $\ce{Br2}$ at the start, −x for $\ce{Br2}$ and +2x for Br; 1 point for $K_c$ with $(2x)^2$ on top and $0.0500 - x$ on the bottom. Writing $(2x)^2$ as $2x^2$ loses the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for $x = 3.87 \times 10^{-4}$ (or $3.9 \times 10^{-4}$), with $x$ left out only beside 0.0500. Solving the full quadratic instead gives $3.86 \times 10^{-4}$ and earns the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for dividing $x$ by 0.0500 and giving the percentage, 0.77 percent (0.78 percent from the rounded $x$); 1 point for saying it is under 5 percent, so the approximation is justified. Dividing $2x$ by 0.0500 (1.5 percent) earns the second point only. A student who solved the quadratic in part (b) earns both points by dividing that $x$ by 0.0500.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for $[\ce{Br}] = 7.7 \times 10^{-4}$ M and $[\ce{Br2}]$ = 0.0496 M, with the check giving $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$. Giving $x$ as $[\ce{Br}]$ earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Equilibrium partial pressures of HOCl" topic="7.7" points={5}>

Dichlorine monoxide, $\ce{Cl2O}$, reacts with water vapor to give hypochlorous acid, HOCl:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H2O(g) + Cl2O(g) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{2HOCl(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

At a certain temperature $K_p$ = 0.160. A rigid flask is filled with water vapor at a partial pressure of 0.0240 atm and $\ce{Cl2O}$ at 0.0240 atm, with no HOCl, and held at that temperature. (The values are made up for practice.)

<Part label="a">

State which way the reaction runs, and set up a table of initial, change and equilibrium partial pressures.

<Answer>

There is no HOCl, so $Q_p$ = 0, which is less than $K_p$. The reaction **runs toward products**.

Every value is in atm. The rows are labeled I, C and E, for Initial, Change and Equilibrium:

| | H₂O | Cl₂O | HOCl |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.0240 | 0.0240 | 0 |
| C | −x | −x | +2x |
| E | 0.0240 − x | 0.0240 − x | 2x |

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the partial pressure of each gas at equilibrium.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_p &= \frac{(P_{\ce{HOCl}})^2}{(P_{\ce{H2O}})(P_{\ce{Cl2O}})} \\
&= \frac{(2x)^2}{(0.0240 - x)^2} \\
&= 0.160
\end{aligned}
$$

The two reactants started equal and fall by the same amount, so the bottom is a perfect square like the top. Take the square root of both sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2x}{0.0240 - x} &= \sqrt{0.160} \\
&= 0.400
\end{aligned}
$$

Use the positive root. The negative one gives a negative $x$, which would make the pressure of HOCl negative.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x &= 0.400(0.0240 - x) \\
2x &= 0.00960 - 0.400x \\
2.400x &= 0.00960 \\
x &= 0.00400
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\ce{HOCl}} &= 2(0.00400) \\
&= \mathbf{0.00800}\ \text{atm}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\ce{H2O}} &= P_{\ce{Cl2O}} \\
&= 0.0240 - 0.00400 \\
&= \mathbf{0.0200}\ \text{atm}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{(0.00800)^2}{(0.0200)(0.0200)} \\
&= \frac{6.40 \times 10^{-5}}{4.00 \times 10^{-4}} = 0.160
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the total pressure in the flask at equilibrium, and explain why it equals the total pressure at the start.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{total}} &= 0.0200 + 0.0200 \\
&\quad + 0.00800 \\
&= \mathbf{0.0480}\ \text{atm}
\end{aligned}
$$

At the start it was the same:

$$
0.0240 + 0.0240 = 0.0480\ \text{atm}
$$

Each time one $\ce{H2O}$ and one $\ce{Cl2O}$ react, two HOCl form: two molecules of gas become two. The total amount of gas does not change, so in a rigid flask at a fixed temperature the total pressure does not change either.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for the direction, toward products because $Q_p$ = 0, with the table in atm: −x for each reactant and +2x for HOCl.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for the expression in $x$ with the square root of both sides taken (0.400 on the right); 1 point for $x$ = 0.00400; 1 point for the three pressures (0.0200 atm, 0.0200 atm and 0.00800 atm HOCl). Expanding and solving the full quadratic instead earns the first two points. Giving $x$ as the pressure of HOCl loses the third point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for 0.0480 atm with the reason: two molecules of gas on each side of the equation, so the total amount of gas, and with it the total pressure, does not change. Showing that the $x$ terms cancel in the sum of the three pressures also earns the point. A total with no reason earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="A mixture that starts with product" topic="7.7" points={6}>

For the made-up reaction

$$
\ce{X(s) + Y(g) <=> Z(g)}
$$

$K_c$ = 1.50 at a certain temperature. A sealed vessel at that temperature holds some solid X, which stays present throughout, with $[\ce{Y}]$ = 0.100 M and $[\ce{Z}]$ = 0.400 M at the start.

<Part label="a">

Write the expression for $Q_c$, calculate it for the starting mixture, and state which way the reaction runs.

<Answer>

X is a solid, so it is left out:

$$
Q_c = \frac{[\ce{Z}]}{[\ce{Y}]}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_c &= \frac{0.400}{0.100} \\
&= \mathbf{4.00}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is greater than 1.50: there is too much Z for equilibrium. The reaction **runs toward reactants**, using up Z and forming Y and X, until $Q_c$ has fallen to 1.50.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Set up a table of initial, change and equilibrium concentrations, and calculate $x$.

<Answer>

The solid has no concentration in the table. The reaction runs toward reactants, so Y rises by $x$ and Z falls by $x$.

Every value is in M. The rows are labeled I, C and E, for Initial, Change and Equilibrium:

| | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|
| I | 0.100 | 0.400 |
| C | +x | −x |
| E | 0.100 + x | 0.400 − x |

$$
K_c = \frac{0.400 - x}{0.100 + x} = 1.50
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.400 - x &= 1.50(0.100 + x) \\
0.400 - x &= 0.150 + 1.50x \\
0.250 &= 2.50x \\
x &= \mathbf{0.100}
\end{aligned}
$$

If you skipped the direction and wrote the table for a forward reaction, you would get $x = -0.100$. The minus sign says the reaction runs the other way.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate $[\ce{Y}]$ and $[\ce{Z}]$ at equilibrium, and show that they give $K_c$ back.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Y}] &= 0.100 + 0.100 \\
&= \mathbf{0.200}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Z}] &= 0.400 - 0.100 \\
&= \mathbf{0.300}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check:

$$
\frac{0.300}{0.200} = 1.50
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

State whether the mass of solid X increases, decreases or stays the same as the mixture reaches equilibrium. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

The mass of X **increases**. The reaction runs toward reactants, and every Z that reacts forms one X along with one Y. X is left out of $K$ because its concentration is fixed, not because its amount cannot change.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for $Q_c = [\ce{Z}]/[\ce{Y}]$ with no X and the value 4.00; 1 point for the direction, toward reactants, because $Q_c$ is greater than $K_c$. An expression that includes X loses the first point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the table with +x for Y and −x for Z; 1 point for $x$ = 0.100. A table written for the forward direction that gives $x = -0.100$, with the minus sign read as the reverse direction, earns both points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for $[\ce{Y}]$ = 0.200 M and $[\ce{Z}]$ = 0.300 M, with the check giving 1.50.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for "increases", because the reaction runs toward reactants and forms X. "Stays the same because a solid is left out of $K$" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="When the shortcut fails" topic="7.7" points={7}>

For the made-up reaction

$$
\ce{A(g) + B(g) <=> AB(g)}
$$

$K_c$ = 0.500 at a certain temperature. A vessel is filled with 0.450 M A and 0.300 M B, with no AB, and held at that temperature.

<Part label="a">

Set up a table of initial, change and equilibrium concentrations, and write $K_c$ in terms of $x$.

<Answer>

There is no AB, so $Q_c$ = 0 and the reaction runs forward.

Every value is in M. The rows are labeled I, C and E, for Initial, Change and Equilibrium:

| | A | B | AB |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.450 | 0.300 | 0 |
| C | −x | −x | +x |
| E | 0.450 − x | 0.300 − x | x |

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_c &= \frac{[\ce{AB}]}{[\ce{A}][\ce{B}]} \\
&= \frac{x}{(0.450 - x)(0.300 - x)} \\
&= 0.500
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student leaves $x$ out beside both starting concentrations. Calculate the $x$ this gives, carry out the 5 percent check, and state what it shows.

<Answer>

With $0.450 - x$ taken as 0.450 and $0.300 - x$ as 0.300:

$$
\frac{x}{(0.450)(0.300)} = 0.500
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= (0.500)(0.450)(0.300) \\
&= 0.0675
\end{aligned}
$$

$x$ was left out of two starting concentrations, so check it against both:

$$
\frac{0.0675}{0.300} \times 100\% = 22.5\%
$$

$$
\frac{0.0675}{0.450} \times 100\% = 15.0\%
$$

Both are **well over 5 percent**. The approximation has failed, and 0.0675 is not good enough. Too large a share of the reactants reacts for $x$ to be left out, and only the check could tell you so.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Solve for $x$ with the quadratic formula. Give both roots, and explain which one you keep.

<Answer>

Clear the fraction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 0.500(0.450 - x) \\
&\quad \times (0.300 - x)
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply out the brackets:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.450 - x)(0.300 - x) \\
&= 0.135 - 0.750x + x^2
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 0.0675 - 0.375x \\
&\quad + 0.500x^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Collect every term on one side and multiply through by 2:

$$
x^2 - 2.75x + 0.135 = 0
$$

In the quadratic formula, $a$ = 1, $b = -2.75$ and $c$ = 0.135. The quantity under the square root, $b^2 - 4ac$, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(2.75)^2 - 4(0.135) \\
&= 7.5625 - 0.540 \\
&= 7.0225
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \frac{2.75 \pm \sqrt{7.0225}}{2} \\
&= \frac{2.75 \pm 2.65}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two roots:

$$
x = \frac{2.75 + 2.65}{2} = 2.70
$$

$$
x = \frac{2.75 - 2.65}{2} = 0.0500
$$

Both roots are positive, so the sign does not decide. **Reject 2.70**: it is larger than what was there of either reactant. It would make both concentrations negative, which is impossible:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{A}] &= 0.450 - 2.70 \\
&= -2.25\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

and $[\ce{B}]$ would be $-2.40$ M. **Keep $x$ = 0.0500.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the three concentrations at equilibrium, and show that they give $K_c$ back.

<Answer>

$$
[\ce{AB}] = \mathbf{0.0500}\ \text{M}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{A}] &= 0.450 - 0.0500 \\
&= \mathbf{0.400}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{B}] &= 0.300 - 0.0500 \\
&= \mathbf{0.250}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check:

$$
\begin{aligned}

<Question number={7} title="Hydrogen and deuterium trade atoms" topic="7.8" points={4}>

Two sealed flasks are held at the same temperature, each with a catalyst that lets the molecules trade atoms. Flask 1 has a volume of 1.00 L and has been left until nothing in it changes. Flask 2 has a volume of 2.00 L and has just been filled with a mixture of all three gases. |---|---|---|---|
| 1 (1.00 L) | 4 | 5 | 8 |
| 2 (2.00 L) | 3 | 3 | 8 |

<Part label="a">

Explain why the counts for flask 1 can be put straight into the expression for $K_c$, without first being turned into concentrations. Then calculate $K_c$ at this temperature.

<Answer>

$$
K_c = \frac{[\ce{HD}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{D2}]}
$$

Each concentration is the count times 0.0100 mol, divided by the volume of the flask. $$
\begin{aligned}
K_c &= \frac{8^2}{(4)(5)} = \frac{64}{20} \\
&= \mathbf{3.20}
\end{aligned}
$$

The concentrations give the same value. In flask 1 they are 0.0400 M $\ce{H2}$, 0.0500 M $\ce{D2}$ and 0.0800 M $\ce{HD}$:

\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

\end{aligned}

## Part G: Le Châtelier's principle and Q

Six questions on what happens when a system at equilibrium is disturbed. You will predict the direction of each shift, say which changes alter $K$, explain shifts with $Q$, calculate $Q$ just after a disturbance, and choose conditions for the best yield.

<Question number={10} title="Four changes to a hot gas mixture" topic="7.9, 7.10" points={6}>

Hydrogen turns nitrogen monoxide into nitrogen and steam:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2NO(g) + 2H2(g) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{N2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The forward reaction is exothermic. A mixture of the four gases is at equilibrium in a cylinder with a piston, at a fixed high temperature. Each change below is made to a fresh sample of this equilibrium mixture, and the temperature is held constant throughout.

<Part label="a">

With the piston locked, so that the volume cannot change, more $\ce{H2}$ is pumped in. State the direction in which the equilibrium shifts. Then compare $[\ce{H2}]$ at the new equilibrium with $[\ce{H2}]$ just before the $\ce{H2}$ was added and just after.

<Answer>

The equilibrium shifts **toward products**. $\ce{H2}$ is a reactant, and the shift uses up some of what was added. In terms of $Q$: $[\ce{H2}]$ is in the bottom of the fraction, so adding it makes $Q$ smaller than $K$, and the reaction runs toward products until $Q$ equals $K$ again.

At the new equilibrium, $[\ce{H2}]$ is **lower than just after the addition but higher than just before it**. The shift uses up part of the added $\ce{H2}$, not all of it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Instead, the piston is pushed in, so that the gases take up a smaller volume. State the direction in which the equilibrium shifts, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

The equilibrium shifts **toward products**. A smaller volume shifts an equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas. Here there are 2 + 2 = 4 moles of gas on the left and 1 + 2 = 3 on the right.

With $Q$ instead:

$$
Q_c = \frac{[\ce{N2}][\ce{H2O}]^2}{[\ce{NO}]^2[\ce{H2}]^2}
$$

The squeeze multiplies every concentration by the same factor, $f$, which is greater than 1. The top of the fraction picks up $f$ three times and the bottom four times:

$$
\frac{f^{1+2}}{f^{2+2}} = \frac{1}{f}
$$

So $Q$ falls below $K$, and the reaction runs toward products.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Instead, with the piston locked, argon is pumped in. Argon does not react with any of the gases. The total pressure rises. State whether the equilibrium shifts, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

**No shift.** The volume has not changed, and neither has the number of moles of each reacting gas. So the concentration and the partial pressure of each of the four gases are what they were, $Q$ still equals $K$, and nothing shifts. The rise in total pressure comes from the argon alone.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Instead, a catalyst for the reaction is added. State whether the amount of $\ce{N2}$ in the cylinder changes, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

**It does not change.** A catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reaction by the same factor. The two rates were equal, and they stay equal, so there is no shift. A catalyst gets a mixture to equilibrium sooner; this mixture is already there.

</Answer>

</Part>

$$

The two solids are left out. Halving the volume doubles both concentrations:

$$
\frac{2[\ce{CO2}]}{2[\ce{CO}]} = \frac{[\ce{CO2}]}{[\ce{CO}]}
$$

The factors cancel, so **$Q$ is unchanged and still equals $K$: no shift**. Counting gives the same answer: there is 1 mole of gas on each side of the equation. (The solids are not counted.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Instead, with the piston locked, the cylinder is cooled. State the direction in which the equilibrium shifts and what happens to the value of $K$.

<Answer>

The forward reaction is exothermic. Cooling shifts an equilibrium in the exothermic direction, so this one shifts **toward products**: more iron and carbon dioxide. For an exothermic reaction $K$ falls as the temperature rises, so on cooling **$K$ increases**.

</Answer>

</Part>

Counting the solids too (2 on each side) reaches "no shift" for a wrong reason and earns 0 points for the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for a shift toward products, because cooling favors the exothermic direction, or because $K$ rises while $Q_c$ is unchanged, so $Q$ is less than $K$; 1 point for $K$ increasing.</RubricLine>

<Question number={14} title="Expansion and heating compared" topic="7.9, 7.10" points={5}>

Ammonia burns in oxygen to give nitrogen and steam:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{4NH3(g) + 3O2(g) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{2N2(g) + 6H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}

The forward reaction is exothermic. A mixture of the four gases is at equilibrium in a cylinder with a piston, at a certain temperature. Each change below is made to a fresh sample of this equilibrium.

<Part label="a">

The piston is pulled out until the volume is doubled, at constant temperature. Calculate the factor by which $Q_c$ is multiplied at that moment, and state the direction in which the reaction then runs.

<Answer>

$$
Q_c = \frac{[\ce{N2}]^2[\ce{H2O}]^6}{[\ce{NH3}]^4[\ce{O2}]^3}
$$

Doubling the volume halves every concentration. The exponents decide what that does to $Q$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{top:}\quad & \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2+6} = \frac{1}{256} \\[0.8em]
\text{bottom:}\quad & \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{4+3} = \frac{1}{128} \\[0.8em]
Q\text{:}\quad & \frac{1}{256} \div \frac{1}{128} = \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

$Q_c$ is multiplied by $\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}$. Now $Q$ is less than $K$, so the reaction runs **toward products**. That is the side with more moles of gas, 8 against 7, as Le Châtelier's principle predicts for a larger volume.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Instead, with the piston locked, the mixture is heated. Just after the temperature rises, before anything has reacted, state whether $Q_c$ and $K$ have each changed. Then state the direction in which the reaction runs.

<Answer>

The forward reaction is exothermic, so $K$ falls as the temperature rises. **$K$ is smaller.**

$Q$ is now greater than the new $K$, so the reaction runs **toward reactants**. That agrees with Le Châtelier's principle: heating shifts an equilibrium in the endothermic direction, which here is the reverse reaction.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student says: "Both changes upset the equilibrium, so both changed the value of $K$." Evaluate the claim.

<Answer>

The claim is **wrong for the expansion**. At a fixed temperature $K$ keeps its value. The expansion changed $Q$, and the mixture then reacted until its concentrations fit the same $K$ again. Only the heating changed $K$.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Part H: Solubility and the common-ion effect

Six questions on salts that dissolve only slightly. You will write dissolving equations and $K_{sp}$ expressions, go from $K_{sp}$ to a molar solubility and back, compare two salts, and work out how an ion already in the water lowers a salt's solubility but not its $K_{sp}$. Every $K_{sp}$ given for a real salt is for 25 °C.

<Question number={16} title="Copper(I) iodide in water" topic="7.11" points={5}>

Copper(I) iodide, $\ce{CuI}$, is a solid that dissolves only very slightly in water. Its molar mass is 190.45 g/mol.

<Part label="a">

Write the dissolving equation for copper(I) iodide, with state symbols, and write its $K_{sp}$ expression.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CuI(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Cu^+(aq) + I^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_{sp} = [\ce{Cu^+}][\ce{I^-}]
$$

The solid is left out of the expression, because the concentration of a pure solid does not depend on how much of it there is.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

At 25 °C, $K_{sp}$ for copper(I) iodide is $1.27 \times 10^{-12}$. Calculate its molar solubility, and then the volume of saturated solution that holds 1.00 mg of dissolved $\ce{CuI}$.

<Answer>

Each mole of $\ce{CuI}$ that dissolves gives one mole of $\ce{Cu^+}$ and one mole of $\ce{I^-}$, so $[\ce{Cu^+}] = [\ce{I^-}] = s$, the molar solubility.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{sp} &= s^2 \\
s &= \sqrt{1.27 \times 10^{-12}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.13 \times 10^{-6}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

First find the moles of $\ce{CuI}$ in 1.00 mg:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{g}}{190.45\ \text{g/mol}} \\
&= 5.25 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each liter of saturated solution holds $s$ mol, so divide:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{5.25 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{mol}}{1.127 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}} \\
&= \mathbf{4.66}\ \text{L}
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the rounded $s$, $1.13 \times 10^{-6}$ M, gives 4.65 L.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The saturated solution stands with some solid $\ce{CuI}$ on the bottom. Describe what is happening to ions at the surface of the solid, and explain why the amount of dissolved $\ce{CuI}$ stays the same.

<Answer>

Both processes keep going. $\ce{Cu^+}$ and $\ce{I^-}$ ions leave the solid and go into the water (dissolving), and dissolved ions meet the solid and join it again (precipitation).

The two happen at the **same rate**, so there is no net change in the amount dissolved. The saturated solution is a dynamic equilibrium, not a solution in which dissolving has stopped.

</Answer>

</Part>

<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the dissolving equation with $\ce{CuI(s)}$ on the left and $\ce{Cu^+(aq)}$ and $\ce{I^-(aq)}$ on the right; 1 point for $K_{sp} = [\ce{Cu^+}][\ce{I^-}]$ with nothing on the bottom. An expression with $[\ce{CuI}]$ in it earns 0 for the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the molar solubility, $s = \sqrt{K_{sp}} = 1.13 \times 10^{-6}$ M; 1 point for the volume, 4.65 to 4.66 L, from the moles in 1.00 mg divided by $s$. Taking $s$ to be $K_{sp}$ itself, without the square root, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>

\end{aligned}
$$

Leave $2s$ out of the sum:

$$
(0.0250)^2(s) = 6.25 \times 10^{-11}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \frac{6.25 \times 10^{-11}}{6.25 \times 10^{-4}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.00 \times 10^{-7}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The 0.0250 M is not doubled. The 2 in the equation belongs to the $\ce{M^+}$ that comes from $\ce{M2X}$ (the $2s$), and the exponent 2 applies to the whole $\ce{M^+}$ concentration.

Check: what was left out is $2s$, $2.00 \times 10^{-7}$ M, from the 0.0250 M of $\ce{M^+}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{2.00 \times 10^{-7}}{0.0250} \\
&\quad \times 100\% = 0.0008\%
\end{aligned}
$$

That is far under 5 percent, so the approximation holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={21} title="The wrong water in a procedure" topic="7.12" points={4}>

A salt with the made-up formula $\ce{MZ}$ dissolves slightly in water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MZ(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{M^+(aq) + Z^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A procedure for finding its $K_{sp}$ says: stir excess solid $\ce{MZ}$ with distilled water at a fixed temperature until no more dissolves; filter off the solid; measure $[\ce{M^+}]$ in the clear liquid; calculate $K_{sp}$ as $[\ce{M^+}]^2$.

Student 1 follows the procedure and measures $[\ce{M^+}] = 2.40 \times 10^{-5}$ M. Student 2 works at the same temperature but, by mistake, uses $1.00 \times 10^{-3}$ M $\ce{NaZ}$ in place of distilled water. $\ce{NaZ}$ is soluble, like every sodium salt. Student 2 does everything else the same way, including calculating $K_{sp}$ as $[\ce{M^+}]^2$.

<Part label="a">

Calculate $K_{sp}$ from student 1's result.

<Answer>

In distilled water every $\ce{Z^-}$ ion comes from the salt, so $[\ce{Z^-}] = [\ce{M^+}]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{sp} &= [\ce{M^+}][\ce{Z^-}] \\
&= (2.40 \times 10^{-5})^2 \\
&= \mathbf{5.76 \times 10^{-10}}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Using your value of $K_{sp}$, calculate the $[\ce{M^+}]$ that student 2 would find, checking any approximation you make. Then calculate the $K_{sp}$ value that student 2 would report.

<Answer>

In student 2's liquid, $[\ce{Z^-}]$ starts at $1.00 \times 10^{-3}$ M and $[\ce{M^+}]$ at 0. If $s$ mol/L of $\ce{MZ}$ dissolves, then at equilibrium $[\ce{M^+}] = s$ and $[\ce{Z^-}] = 1.00 \times 10^{-3} + s$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&s(1.00 \times 10^{-3} + s) \\
&= 5.76 \times 10^{-10}
\end{aligned}
$$

Leave $s$ out of the sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \frac{5.76 \times 10^{-10}}{1.00 \times 10^{-3}} \\
&= \mathbf{5.76 \times 10^{-7}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: what was left out is $s$, $5.76 \times 10^{-7}$ M, from the $1.00 \times 10^{-3}$ M of $\ce{Z^-}$.

$$
\frac{5.76 \times 10^{-7}}{1.00 \times 10^{-3}} \times 100\% = 0.058\%
$$

That is far under 5 percent. So student 2 would find $[\ce{M^+}] = 5.76 \times 10^{-7}$ M, and would report

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{sp} &= (5.76 \times 10^{-7})^2 \\
&= \mathbf{3.32 \times 10^{-13}}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

State whether student 2's reported value is too high or too low, and explain why, referring to the true value of $K_{sp}$.

<Answer>

**Too low**, by far: $3.32 \times 10^{-13}$ is less than a thousandth of $5.76 \times 10^{-10}$.

The $\ce{Z^-}$ from the $\ce{NaZ}$ is a common ion, so much less $\ce{MZ}$ dissolves and $[\ce{M^+}]$ is small. $K_{sp}$ itself has not changed: in student 2's liquid

$$
\begin{aligned}
&[\ce{M^+}][\ce{Z^-}] \\
&= (5.76 \times 10^{-7}) \\
&\quad \times (1.00 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= 5.76 \times 10^{-10}
\end{aligned}
$$

Calculating $K_{sp}$ as $[\ce{M^+}]^2$ assumes that $[\ce{Z^-}]$ equals $[\ce{M^+}]$, which is true only in distilled water. Here it leaves out nearly all of the $\ce{Z^-}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

Saying that the $\ce{NaZ}$ lowered $K_{sp}$ earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>
