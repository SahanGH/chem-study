There are twelve ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions of the same kind: same skill, new numbers, no help. Work alone, and work all four before you check any of them.

**The one idea this whole unit turns on.** Kinetics is about *how fast*. Thermodynamics is about *how far*. They are independent. A reaction can be enormously favorable and still take centuries; diamond turning to graphite is the standard example. Never answer a rate question with an argument about stability.

**The habit that decides this unit.** Reaction orders are **experimental**. They come from data. A mechanism's slow step can predict them. The coefficients of the overall equation never can. Reading orders off a balanced equation is the single most common way to lose points here.

**One scope note.** The AP course framework excludes **Arrhenius equation calculations**: you will not be asked to extract an activation energy from two rate constants or from a plot of $\ln k$ against $1/T$. Reading an energy profile and reasoning about temperature and catalysts qualitatively both remain fully examinable.

## Ladder 1: Expressing reaction rate

Rate is a change in concentration per unit time. Divide each species' rate by its coefficient and every species gives the *same* number: one rate for the reaction.

<Worked title="Relating species">

In this reaction, oxygen appears at 0.0125 M/s:

$$
\ce{2N2O5 -> 4NO2 + O2}
$$

Find the rates for the other two.

**Scale by the coefficients.** Relative to $\ce{O2}$ (coefficient 1):

$\ce{NO2}$ has coefficient 4, so it forms at

$$
4 \times 0.0125 = \mathbf{0.0500}\ \text{M/s}
$$

$\ce{N2O5}$ has coefficient 2, so it is *consumed* at

$$
2 \times 0.0125 = \mathbf{0.0250}\ \text{M/s}
$$

As a rate of change, that is negative, because it is disappearing:

$$
\Delta[\ce{N2O5}]/\Delta t = -0.0250\ \text{M/s}
$$

**The single reaction rate:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta[\ce{N2O5}]}{\Delta t} &= \tfrac{1}{4}\frac{\Delta[\ce{NO2}]}{\Delta t} \\
&= \frac{\Delta[\ce{O2}]}{\Delta t} = 0.0125\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Rate law from initial rates

Compare two experiments in which *one* concentration changes and the others are held constant. The factor the rate changes by tells you the order.

<Worked title="Reading a rate table">

For $\ce{A + 2B -> C}$:

| Experiment | $[\ce{A}]_0$ (M) | $[\ce{B}]_0$ (M) | Rate (M/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.050 | 0.10 | $1.2 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 2 | 0.100 | 0.10 | $4.8 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 3 | 0.050 | 0.20 | $2.4 \times 10^{-3}$ |

**Order in A:** compare experiments 1 and 2, where $[\ce{B}]$ is constant. $[\ce{A}]$ doubles and the rate goes up by

$$
4.8/1.2 = 4
$$

Since $2^n = 4$, $n = \mathbf{2}$.

**Order in B:** compare experiments 1 and 3, where $[\ce{A}]$ is constant. $[\ce{B}]$ doubles and the rate doubles, so $n = \mathbf{1}$.

$$
\text{rate} = k[\ce{A}]^2[\ce{B}]
$$

The overall order:

$$
2 + 1 = \mathbf{3}
$$

**Note what the equation would have suggested:** a coefficient of 2 on B, hence perhaps second order in B. The data say first. Orders are experimental.

</Worked>

## Ladder 3: The rate constant and its units

$k$ is found by substituting one experiment back into the rate law. Its *units* are whatever makes the equation dimensionally consistent, and they depend on the overall order.

<Worked title="Value and units together">

From experiment 1 in the table above, find $k$ with units.

$$
\begin{aligned}
k &= \frac{\text{rate}}{[\ce{A}]^2[\ce{B}]} \\
&= \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-3}}{(0.050)^2(0.10)} \\
&= \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-3}}{2.5 \times 10^{-4}} = \mathbf{4.8}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Units:** rate is M/s and $[\ce{A}]^2[\ce{B}]$ is $\text{M}^3$, so $k$ carries per molar squared per second, that is, $\text{M}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

**Check with another experiment:**

$$
4.8(0.100)^2(0.10) = 4.8 \times 10^{-3}
$$

That matches experiment 2. ✓ A rate constant that fails this check means the orders are wrong.

**The pattern:** overall order 0 gives M/s; order 1 gives $\text{s}^{-1}$; order 2 gives $\text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$; order 3 gives $\text{M}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

</Worked>

## Ladder 4: Which plot is straight?

Given concentration-versus-time data, the order is identified by which plot comes out linear.

<Worked title="The three tests">

State which plot is linear for each order, and what the slope gives.

**Zero order:** $[\ce{A}]$ against $t$ is linear. The slope is $-k$. The concentration falls by equal *amounts* in equal times.

**First order:** $\ln[\ce{A}]$ against $t$ is linear. The slope is $-k$. The concentration falls by equal *fractions* in equal times, which is what a constant half-life means.

**Second order:** $1/[\ce{A}]$ against $t$ is linear. The slope is $+k$, positive because the reciprocal grows.

**How to use it:** plot all three from the same data. Exactly one comes out straight, and that identifies the order. The slope then gives $k$ directly. Note the sign: negative for zero and first order, positive for second.

</Worked>

## Ladder 5: Half-life

For a first-order reaction, $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$. It is constant, whatever the concentration. Every half-life removes half of whatever remains.

<Worked title="Half-life and successive halvings">

A first-order reaction has $k = 0.0300\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and starts at 0.800 M. Find the half-life and the concentration after three half-lives.

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{0.0300} = \mathbf{23.1}\ \text{s}
$$

**Three half-lives:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.800 \to 0.400 \\
&\to 0.200 \to \mathbf{0.100}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

That takes

$$
3 \times 23.1 = 69.3\ \text{s}
$$

**Why the half-life does not drift.** As the concentration falls, the rate falls in exact proportion. That is what first order means. Half of a smaller amount takes exactly as long to disappear as half of a larger one did.

**Contrast:** for a reaction that is second order in A ($\text{rate} = k[\ce{A}]^2$), each half-life is twice as long as the one before, because halving $[\ce{A}]$ cuts the rate to a quarter.

</Worked>

## Ladder 6: Elementary reactions

An elementary step describes an actual molecular event. For an elementary step *only*, the coefficients may be used as the orders.

<Worked title="When coefficients are allowed">

Write the rate law for this elementary step:

$$
\ce{2NO2 -> NO3 + NO}
$$

Then explain why the same cannot be done for this overall equation:

$$
\ce{2NO2 + F2 -> 2NO2F}
$$

**Elementary step:** the event is two $\ce{NO2}$ molecules colliding, so

$$
\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO2}]^2
$$

This is legitimate *because* the step is elementary: the equation describes the collision itself.

**Overall equation:** it is a summary of the net change, not a description of any single event. It says nothing about how many molecules actually meet in the slow step. Writing this from it would be a guess:

$$
\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO2}]^2[\ce{F2}]
$$

Experiment shows the true law is $k[\ce{NO2}][\ce{F2}]$.

**Molecularity:** one molecule is unimolecular, two bimolecular. Termolecular steps, three particles meeting at once, are very rare, which is why proposed mechanisms almost never contain them.

</Worked>

## Ladder 7: The collision model

A collision produces reaction only if it carries at least the activation energy *and* the molecules are correctly oriented. Everything that changes a rate changes how often particles collide, or what fraction of those collisions meets both requirements.

<Worked title="Why a small warming does so much">

A rise of 10 °C can roughly double a rate, though it raises the average speed only about 2 percent. Explain.

**What actually matters** is the *fraction* of collisions carrying at least $E_a$, and that fraction lies far out in the high-energy tail of the distribution of molecular energies.

**Warming shifts and broadens the whole distribution.** Near the average that is a small change. Out in the tail, where the threshold sits, the population rises steeply, easily doubling.

**Collision frequency rises too, but only slightly:** about 2 percent, in line with the speed. Essentially all of the rate increase comes from the energy distribution, not from more collisions.

**The answer that earns half credit:** "molecules move faster and collide more often". True, but it is the smaller effect; the credit is for the fraction above $E_a$.

</Worked>

**More energetic ones, by far.** Collision frequency rises only about 2 percent with a modest warming, while the fraction of collisions exceeding $E_a$ can double, because that fraction sits in the steep tail of the energy distribution.

## Ladder 8: Reading an energy profile

The vertical axis is potential energy. The peak is the transition state; the climb from reactants to peak is $E_a$; the difference between the two ends is $\Delta H$.

<Worked title="Everything on one diagram">

A profile rises from reactants at 50 kJ to a peak at 170 kJ, then falls to products at 20 kJ. Find $E_a$ forward, $E_a$ reverse and $\Delta H$, and say whether it is exothermic.

**Forward $E_a$:** reactants up to the peak.

$$
170 - 50 = \mathbf{120}\ \text{kJ}
$$

**Reverse $E_a$:** products up to the same peak.

$$
170 - 20 = \mathbf{150}\ \text{kJ}
$$

**$\Delta H$:** products minus reactants.

$$
20 - 50 = \mathbf{-30}\ \text{kJ}
$$

Negative, so **exothermic**. The products sit lower than the reactants, which is the visual signature.

**The relationship worth remembering:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= E_{a,\text{forward}} - E_{a,\text{reverse}} \\
&= 120 - 150 = -30\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same value as before. ✓

**A multi-step profile** has one peak per step, with shallow minima between them where intermediates sit. The slowest step, the rate-limiting step, is the one with the largest climb, each measured from where that step starts: the reactants for step 1, the valley just before its peak for a later step.

</Worked>

## Ladder 9: Mechanisms and the rate-limiting step

A mechanism can be right only if its steps add to the overall equation *and* the rate law it predicts matches the observed one. When the slow step comes first, that prediction is simply the slow step's rate law.

<Worked title="Testing a mechanism">

At low temperature, this reaction has the observed rate law $k[\ce{NO2}]^2$:

$$
\ce{NO2 + CO -> NO + CO2}
$$

Test this mechanism for it:

| Step | Equation |
|---|---|
| Step 1 (slow) | $\ce{NO2 + NO2 -> NO3 + NO}$ |
| Step 2 (fast) | $\ce{NO3 + CO -> NO2 + CO2}$ |

**Do the steps add?** Summing:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2NO2 + NO3 + CO} \\
&\qquad \ce{-> NO3 + NO + NO2 + CO2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Cancel $\ce{NO3}$ from both sides and one $\ce{NO2}$:

$$
\ce{NO2 + CO -> NO + CO2}
$$

That is the overall equation. ✓

**Does the slow step give the observed law?** Step 1 is the slow step and comes first, so its rate law is the reaction's. Two $\ce{NO2}$ molecules collide in it, so $\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO2}]^2$. ✓

**The striking prediction:** $\ce{CO}$ does not appear in the rate law at all, even though it is a reactant. It enters only in the *fast* step, after the bottleneck, so adding more of it does not speed the reaction up. That is exactly what the experiment finds, and it is strong evidence for this mechanism.

</Worked>

<Answer>

**Step 1**, the slow step. The overall reaction cannot proceed faster than its slowest stage, so that step is the bottleneck.

<Answer>

The steps sum to the overall equation; the predicted rate law matches the observed one.

(1) The steps must **sum to the overall balanced equation**, with intermediates canceling. (2) The **rate law the mechanism predicts must match the experimentally observed rate law**. When the slow step comes first, that is the slow step's rate law. Failing either rules the mechanism out. Passing both does not prove it correct, only that it is consistent.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 10: Intermediates versus catalysts

Both appear inside a mechanism and neither appears in the overall equation, but they are opposites. An intermediate is *made then used*; a catalyst is *used then remade*.

<Worked title="Telling them apart">

In this mechanism, identify the catalyst and the intermediate:

| Step | Equation |
|---|---|
| Step 1 | $\ce{X + A -> XA}$ |
| Step 2 | $\ce{XA + B -> C + X}$ |

**$\ce{X}$ is the catalyst.** It is *consumed* in step 1 and *regenerated* in step 2: present at the start, present at the end, unchanged overall.

**$\ce{XA}$ is the intermediate.** It is *produced* in step 1 and *consumed* in step 2: absent at the start, absent at the end.

**The test is the order of events.** Consumed first, then produced: catalyst. Produced first, then consumed: intermediate.

**Overall:** $\ce{A + B -> C}$, with $\ce{X}$ appearing nowhere, which is why a catalyst is never written into the equation, only above the arrow.

</Worked>

## Ladder 11: How a catalyst works

A catalyst provides an *alternative pathway* with a lower activation energy. It does not push the old reaction harder. It opens a new route.

<Worked title="What changes and what does not">

State precisely what a catalyst changes and what it leaves alone.

**Changes: the activation energy.** The new pathway has a lower barrier, so at any given temperature a larger fraction of collisions carries enough energy. The rate rises, often by orders of magnitude.

**Does *not* change $\Delta H$.** Reactants and products sit at exactly the same energies as before. Only the height of the hill between them is reduced. The two ends are untouched.

**Does *not* change the equilibrium position or $K$.** The forward and reverse barriers are lowered by the *same* amount, so both rates rise in the same proportion. Equilibrium arrives sooner, in exactly the same place.

**Two answers that earn zero:** "it lowers the energy of the reactants" and "it shifts the equilibrium toward products". Both describe things a catalyst cannot do.

</Worked>

<Question number={11} label="Your turn" topic="5.11">

<Part label="a">

What does a catalyst do to the activation energy?

<Answer>

Lowers it, via a new pathway.

**Lowers** it, by providing an alternative pathway, not by altering the original one.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

What does it do to $\Delta H$?

<Answer>

Nothing.

**Nothing.** $\Delta H$ depends only on the energies of reactants and products, which are unchanged.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

What does it do to the yield at equilibrium?

<Answer>

Nothing.

**Nothing.** The equilibrium position and $K$ are unaffected; only the time taken to reach equilibrium is shorter.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain why a catalyst speeds the reverse reaction too.

<Answer>

The same lowered barrier is crossed either way.

Because both directions pass over the *same* transition state. A pathway with a lower barrier is a lower barrier whichever way it is crossed, so the reverse activation energy falls by the same amount and the reverse rate rises in the same proportion. That is precisely why the equilibrium position cannot shift.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Ladder 12: Kinetics versus thermodynamics

Two independent questions: *does it want to happen* (thermodynamics) and *will it happen quickly* (kinetics). The answers need not agree.

<Worked title="Favorable but immeasurably slow">

Diamond converting to graphite has $\Delta G^\circ = -2.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$, which is thermodynamically favorable at room temperature. Explain why diamonds do not visibly change.

**Thermodynamics says it should happen.** A negative $\Delta G^\circ$ means graphite is the more stable form: the conversion is thermodynamically favored. ($\Delta G^\circ$, met in Unit 9, says whether a reaction is thermodynamically favored.)

**Kinetics says it will not, on any human timescale.** Rearranging diamond's covalent network requires breaking very strong $\ce{C-C}$ bonds, an enormous activation energy. At room temperature essentially no atom has that much energy, so the rate is indistinguishable from zero.

**The general statement:** $\Delta G$ tells you about the *destination*; $E_a$ tells you about the *journey*. A large negative $\Delta G$ with a large $E_a$ describes a reaction that is strongly favorable and completely stuck, which is why fuels can sit safely in a tank in air.

</Worked>

<Question number={12} label="Your turn" topic="5.5, 5.6">

<Part label="a">

Gasoline and oxygen do not react at room temperature although the reaction is highly favorable. Why?

<Answer>

High activation energy.

The **activation energy is high**, so at room temperature almost no collisions carry enough energy. The mixture is under **kinetic control**: favorable but stuck.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

What does a spark supply?

<Answer>

Energy to surmount $E_a$.

Enough **energy** to get a small number of molecules over the activation barrier. Once started, the reaction is exothermic and supplies the energy to keep itself going.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Does a large equilibrium constant mean a fast reaction?

<Answer>

No.

**No.** $K$ measures how *far* a reaction proceeds, not how *fast*. The two are independent: a reaction with an enormous $K$ can be immeasurably slow if $E_a$ is large.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Name two ways to speed a reaction without changing $\Delta H$.

<Answer>

Raise the temperature; add a catalyst.

**Raise the temperature** (increases the fraction of collisions above $E_a$) and **add a catalyst** (lowers $E_a$ via a new pathway). Increasing concentration or surface area also works. None of them alters $\Delta H$.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
