## Part A: Rates and rate laws from data

Ten questions on how fast a reaction goes. Questions 1 to 3 relate the rates of the substances in a reaction and read rates from measurements of concentration over time. Questions 4 to 10 find rate laws, rate constants and their units from initial rates, and use a rate law to predict a rate.

<Question number={1} title="Rates from the balanced equation" topic="5.1" points={5}>

The first step in making nitric acid burns ammonia over a platinum catalyst:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{4NH3(g) + 5O2(g)} \\
&\qquad \ce{-> 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

In one experiment, $\ce{NO}$ forms at $2.40 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the rate at which $\ce{O2}$ is used up and the rate at which $\ce{H2O}$ forms.

<Answer>

The coefficients set how the rates compare. Five $\ce{O2}$ are used up for every four $\ce{NO}$ that form, so $\ce{O2}$ is used up at

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\tfrac{5}{4} \times (2.40 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= \mathbf{3.00 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

Six $\ce{H2O}$ form for every four $\ce{NO}$, so $\ce{H2O}$ forms at

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\tfrac{6}{4} \times (2.40 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= \mathbf{3.60 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the rate of the reaction.

<Answer>

Divide any one substance's rate by its coefficient. Using $\ce{NO}$, with coefficient 4:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= \tfrac{1}{4} \times 2.40 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s} \\
&= \mathbf{6.00 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

Any other substance gives the same number. For $\ce{O2}$, with coefficient 5:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{3.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}}{5} \\
&= 6.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

State the value of $\Delta[\ce{NH3}]/\Delta t$, with its sign, and explain the sign.

<Answer>

Four $\ce{NH3}$ are used up for every four $\ce{NO}$ that form, so $\ce{NH3}$ is used up at the same $2.40 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$. Its concentration falls, so its change is negative:

$$
\frac{\Delta[\ce{NH3}]}{\Delta t} = \mathbf{-2.40 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
$$

The rate at which $\ce{NH3}$ disappears is reported as a positive number, $2.40 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$. The minus sign belongs to the change in concentration, and it says the concentration is falling.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for $3.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$ for $\ce{O2}$; 1 point for $3.60 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$ for $\ce{H2O}$. A rate found with the ratio upside down (4/5 or 4/6) earns 0 points for that rate.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for dividing a substance's rate by its coefficient; 1 point for $6.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for $-2.40 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$ with the sign explained by the falling concentration of $\ce{NH3}$.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Average rate over an interval" topic="5.1" points={5}>

The reaction below runs in solution at constant temperature. It starts with $[\ce{A}]_0 = 0.500\ \text{M}$ and no B.

$$
\ce{A(aq) -> 2B(aq)}
$$

The concentration of B is measured as the reaction runs.

| Time (s) | $[\ce{B}]$ (M) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.000 |
| 50 | 0.181 |
| 100 | 0.330 |
| 150 | 0.451 |
| 200 | 0.551 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the average rate at which B forms from 0 s to 100 s.

<Answer>

An average rate is the change in concentration divided by the time the change took:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= \frac{(0.330 - 0.000)\ \text{M}}{(100 - 0)\ \text{s}} \\
&= \mathbf{3.30 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the average rate at which B forms from 100 s to 200 s.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= \frac{(0.551 - 0.330)\ \text{M}}{(200 - 100)\ \text{s}} \\
&= \mathbf{2.21 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain, in terms of particles, why the rate in part (b) is smaller than the rate in part (a).

<Answer>

As the reaction runs, A is used up. In the second interval there are fewer particles of A in each liter of solution, so fewer of them react each second, and B forms more slowly.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Determine $[\ce{A}]$ at 100 s.

<Answer>

Two B form for every A used up. By 100 s, 0.330 M of B has formed, so half as much A, 0.165 M, has been used up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{A}] &= 0.500 - \tfrac{1}{2}(0.330) \\
&= \mathbf{0.335}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the change in $[\ce{B}]$ divided by the time; 1 point for $3.30 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for $2.21 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for linking the smaller rate to the lower concentration of A, with less A left to react. Saying only that the reaction slows down earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for 0.335 M. Subtracting all of $[\ce{B}]$ (0.170 M) earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Reading a rate from concentration data" topic="5.1" points={5}>

Gas X decomposes in a sealed flask at constant temperature:

$$
\ce{2X(g) -> 2Y(g) + Z(g)}
$$

The table gives the concentration of X over time. On a graph, these points lie on a curve that falls steeply at first and then flattens.

| Time (s) | $[\ce{X}]$ (M) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.800 |
| 20 | 0.655 |
| 40 | 0.536 |
| 60 | 0.439 |
| 80 | 0.359 |
| 100 | 0.294 |
| 120 | 0.241 |

<Part label="a">

Estimate the rate at which X is used up at 40 s, using the readings at 20 s and 60 s. Explain why these two readings give a good estimate.

<Answer>

Take the average rate over the interval from 20 s to 60 s, with the sign dropped because X is used up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= -\frac{(0.439 - 0.655)\ \text{M}}{(60 - 20)\ \text{s}} \\
&= \frac{0.216\ \text{M}}{40\ \text{s}} \\
&= \mathbf{5.40 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

The interval is short and has 40 s at its middle, so the straight line between the two readings has nearly the same slope as the curve at 40 s.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

On a graph of these data, a student draws the tangent to the curve at 40 s. The tangent passes through the points (0 s, 0.751 M) and (100 s, 0.215 M). Calculate the instantaneous rate at which X is used up at 40 s.

<Answer>

The instantaneous rate is the slope of the tangent, with the sign dropped:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{slope} &= \frac{(0.215 - 0.751)\ \text{M}}{(100 - 0)\ \text{s}} \\
&= -5.36 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

X is used up at $\mathbf{5.36 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}$ at 40 s. The estimate in part (a) is close to it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

State whether X is used up faster or more slowly at 100 s than at 40 s. Justify your answer with the data.

<Answer>

**More slowly.** Around 100 s, between the readings at 80 s and 120 s, $[\ce{X}]$ falls by much less in the same 40 s:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &\approx \frac{(0.359 - 0.241)\ \text{M}}{40\ \text{s}} \\
&= 2.95 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is about half the rate at 40 s. Less X is left, so less of it reacts each second, and the curve is flatter.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the rate at which Z forms at 40 s, using your answer to part (b).

<Answer>

One Z forms for every two X used up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5.36 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s} \\
&= \mathbf{2.68 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for $5.40 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$ from the readings at 20 s and 60 s; 1 point for explaining that a short interval with 40 s at its middle has nearly the slope of the curve at 40 s. A negative rate earns at most 1 point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for $5.36 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$ from the slope of the tangent.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for "more slowly" with a comparison from the table, such as a smaller drop in $[\ce{X}]$ over an equal interval.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for $2.68 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$, or half of the rate the student found in part (a) or (b).</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="A tripled and a halved concentration" topic="5.2" points={6}>

The initial rate of this reaction was measured three times at the same temperature:

$$
\ce{D(aq) + 2E(aq) -> products}
$$

| Experiment | $[\ce{D}]_0$ (M) | $[\ce{E}]_0$ (M) | Initial rate (M/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.0150 | 0.0400 | $1.80 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 2 | 0.0450 | 0.0400 | $1.62 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 3 | 0.0450 | 0.0200 | $8.10 \times 10^{-5}$ |

<Part label="a">

Determine the order of the reaction in D. Name the experiments you compare.

<Answer>

Compare experiments 1 and 2. $[\ce{E}]$ is the same in both, 0.0400 M, and $[\ce{D}]$ triples, from 0.0150 M to 0.0450 M. The rate changes by this factor:

$$
\frac{1.62 \times 10^{-4}}{1.80 \times 10^{-5}} = 9.00
$$

Tripling $[\ce{D}]$ multiplies the rate by $3^m$:

$$
3^m = 9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad m = \mathbf{2}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Determine the order of the reaction in E. Name the experiments you compare.

<Answer>

Compare experiments 2 and 3. $[\ce{D}]$ is the same in both, 0.0450 M, and $[\ce{E}]$ is halved, from 0.0400 M to 0.0200 M. The rate changes by this factor:

$$
\frac{8.10 \times 10^{-5}}{1.62 \times 10^{-4}} = 0.500
$$

Halving $[\ce{E}]$ halves the rate:

$$
\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n = \tfrac{1}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = \mathbf{1}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Write the rate law and state the overall order.

<Answer>

$$
\text{rate} = k[\ce{D}]^2[\ce{E}]
$$

The overall order is $2 + 1 = \mathbf{3}$.

The orders are not the coefficients. D has a coefficient of 1 and an order of 2; E has a coefficient of 2 and an order of 1. Only the data could show this.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for comparing experiments 1 and 2, with $[\ce{E}]$ held constant, and the factor of 9; 1 point for order 2.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for comparing experiments 2 and 3, with $[\ce{D}]$ held constant, and the factor of one half; 1 point for order 1.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for a rate law that matches the orders found in parts (a) and (b); 1 point for overall order 3. A rate law with the coefficients as exponents ($k[\ce{D}][\ce{E}]^2$) earns neither point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Three reactants, one with no effect" topic="5.2" points={8}>

Four experiments were run at the same temperature for this reaction in solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2A(aq) + B(aq) + C(aq)} \\
&\qquad \ce{-> products}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first table gives the starting concentrations in each experiment. The second gives the initial rates.

| Experiment | $[\ce{A}]_0$ (M) | $[\ce{B}]_0$ (M) | $[\ce{C}]_0$ (M) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.100 | 0.100 | 0.0200 |
| 2 | 0.200 | 0.100 | 0.0200 |
| 3 | 0.100 | 0.300 | 0.0200 |
| 4 | 0.200 | 0.100 | 0.0400 |

| Experiment | Initial rate (M/s) |
|---|---|
| 1 | $5.00 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 2 | $1.00 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 3 | $5.00 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 4 | $4.00 \times 10^{-4}$ |

<Part label="a">

Determine the order of the reaction in each reactant. For each one, name the experiments you compare and what was held constant.

<Answer>

**Order in A.** Compare experiments 1 and 2: $[\ce{B}]$ and $[\ce{C}]$ are held constant and $[\ce{A}]$ doubles. The rate changes by this factor:

$$
\frac{1.00 \times 10^{-4}}{5.00 \times 10^{-5}} = 2.00
$$

The rate doubles, so the order in A is **1**.

**Order in B.** Compare experiments 1 and 3: $[\ce{A}]$ and $[\ce{C}]$ are held constant and $[\ce{B}]$ triples, from 0.100 M to 0.300 M. The rate stays at $5.00 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M/s}$:

$$
3^n = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = \mathbf{0}
$$

The rate does not depend on $[\ce{B}]$ at all.

**Order in C.** Compare experiments 2 and 4: $[\ce{A}]$ and $[\ce{B}]$ are held constant and $[\ce{C}]$ doubles. The rate changes by this factor:

$$
\frac{4.00 \times 10^{-4}}{1.00 \times 10^{-4}} = 4.00
$$

Since $2^p = 4$, the order in C is **2**.

Experiments 1 and 4 differ in both $[\ce{A}]$ and $[\ce{C}]$, so they show the order in C only once the order in A is known.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Write the rate law and state the overall order.

<Answer>

$$
\text{rate} = k[\ce{A}][\ce{C}]^2
$$

B does not appear, because $[\ce{B}]^0 = 1$. The overall order is $1 + 0 + 2 = \mathbf{3}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the rate constant, with units.

<Answer>

Use experiment 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k &= \frac{\text{rate}}{[\ce{A}][\ce{C}]^2} \\
&= \frac{5.00 \times 10^{-5}}{(0.100)(0.0200)^2} \\
&= \frac{5.00 \times 10^{-5}}{4.00 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.25}\ \text{M}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Units: the rate is in M/s and $[\ce{A}][\ce{C}]^2$ is in $\text{M}^3$, so $k$ is in $\text{M}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

Check with experiment 4:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(1.25)(0.200)(0.0400)^2 \\
&= 4.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \checkmark
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the initial rate at the same temperature when $[\ce{A}]_0 = 0.150\ \text{M}$, $[\ce{B}]_0 = 0.250\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{C}]_0 = 0.0300\ \text{M}$.

<Answer>

$[\ce{B}]$ does not enter the rate law, so only A and C matter:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&k[\ce{A}][\ce{C}]^2 \\
&= (1.25)(0.150)(0.0300)^2 \\
&= \mathbf{1.69 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for each order (1 in A, 0 in B, 2 in C), each with a comparison in which the other two concentrations are held constant. For the order in C, experiments 1 and 4 with the effect of doubling $[\ce{A}]$ taken out also earn the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for a rate law that matches the orders found in part (a) ($k[\ce{A}][\ce{C}]^2$, or with $[\ce{B}]^0$ written), with overall order 3.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for 1.25; 1 point for $\text{M}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. A bare number earns 1 point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for substituting into the rate law with the value of $k$; 1 point for $1.69 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s}$. A calculation that includes $[\ce{B}]$ earns at most 1 point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="The units of the rate constant" topic="5.2" points={4}>

Concentrations are in M (mol/L) and time is in seconds, so every rate is in M/s. Parts (a) to (c) give rate laws for reactions that each have two reactants, A and B. For each one, state the overall order and determine the units of $k$.

<Part label="a">

$\text{rate} = k[\ce{A}]^0[\ce{B}]^0$

<Answer>

Any concentration to the power 0 is 1, so the rate law is just $\text{rate} = k$. The overall order is $0 + 0 = \mathbf{0}$. Here $k$ equals the rate, so it has the units of a rate: **M/s**, also written $\text{M}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\text{rate} = k[\ce{A}][\ce{B}]^0$

<Answer>

The overall order is $1 + 0 = \mathbf{1}$, and the concentration term is in M. Divide the units of the rate by the units of the concentration term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{units of } k &= \frac{\text{M/s}}{\text{M}} \\
&= \mathbf{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$\text{rate} = k[\ce{B}]^2$

<Answer>

A does not appear, so the reaction is zero order in A. The overall order is $0 + 2 = \mathbf{2}$, and the concentration term is in $\text{M}^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{units of } k &= \frac{\text{M/s}}{\text{M}^2} \\
&= \mathbf{M}^{-1}\,\mathbf{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A different reaction has three reactants, X, Y and Z. Its rate constant has units of $\text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. Determine the overall order of the reaction.

<Answer>

For an overall order $n$, the concentration term is in $\text{M}^n$, so

$$
\frac{\text{M/s}}{\text{M}^n} = \text{M}^{1-n}\,\text{s}^{-1}
$$

Match the power of M:

$$
1 - n = -1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = \mathbf{2}
$$

The reaction is second order overall, although it has three reactants. The orders add to 2, so if they are whole numbers, the rate does not depend on at least one of X, Y and Z: for example $\text{rate} = k[\ce{X}][\ce{Y}]$, zero order in Z.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for overall order 0 with units of M/s.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for overall order 1 with units of $\text{s}^{-1}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for overall order 2 with units of $\text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for second order, with the units of rate divided by $\text{M}^n$ or the pattern of units shown. Answering 3 because there are three reactants earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="Predicting the rate after dilution" topic="5.2" points={4}>

The reaction below takes place in water. Its rate law is $\text{rate} = k[\ce{G}][\ce{L}]$, with $k = 0.0350\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at 25 °C.

$$
\ce{G(aq) + 2L(aq) -> products}
$$

<Part label="a">

Calculate the initial rate at 25 °C when $[\ce{G}]_0 = 0.120\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{L}]_0 = 0.300\ \text{M}$.

<Answer>

Substitute into the rate law:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0350)(0.120)(0.300) \\
&= \mathbf{1.26 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

Units: $\text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ times M times M leaves M/s.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A second solution is made from the same amounts of G and L, dissolved to make twice the volume of solution, also at 25 °C. Determine its initial rate, and state whether $k$ is different.

<Answer>

The same amount in twice the volume halves each concentration: $[\ce{G}]_0 = 0.0600\ \text{M}$ and $[\ce{L}]_0 = 0.150\ \text{M}$. The temperature is the same, so $k$ is **unchanged**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0350)(0.0600)(0.150) \\
&= \mathbf{3.15 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is one quarter of the rate in part (a). The reaction is first order in each reactant, so halving both concentrations halves the rate twice.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for substituting into the rate law; 1 point for $1.26 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M/s}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for halving both concentrations with the same $k$, because the temperature is the same; 1 point for $3.15 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s}$. Saying that $k$ changes earns 0 points for the first point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="Order from a factor of 1.5" topic="5.2" points={5}>

A gas Q decomposes at constant temperature:

$$
\ce{2Q(g) -> products}
$$

| Experiment | $[\ce{Q}]_0$ (M) | Initial rate (M/s) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.0400 | $3.20 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| 2 | 0.0600 | $7.20 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| 3 | 0.0100 | $2.00 \times 10^{-7}$ |

<Part label="a">

Use experiments 1 and 2 to determine the order of the reaction in Q. Show your reasoning.

<Answer>

Compare experiments 1 and 2. The concentration is multiplied by this factor:

$$
\frac{0.0600}{0.0400} = 1.50
$$

The rate is multiplied by this factor:

$$
\frac{7.20 \times 10^{-6}}{3.20 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.25
$$

The order $m$ must satisfy $1.50^m = 2.25$. Since $1.50^1 = 1.50$ and $1.50^2 = 2.25$, the order is $m = \mathbf{2}$.

Experiments 1 and 3 agree. The concentration is divided by 4 and the rate by 16, which is $4^2$:

$$
\frac{3.20 \times 10^{-6}}{2.00 \times 10^{-7}} = 16.0
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the rate constant, with units.

<Answer>

Use experiment 1 in $\text{rate} = k[\ce{Q}]^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k &= \frac{3.20 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.0400)^2} \\
&= \frac{3.20 \times 10^{-6}}{1.60 \times 10^{-3}} \\
&= \mathbf{2.00 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Units: M/s divided by $\text{M}^2$ leaves $\text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the initial concentration of Q that gives an initial rate of $5.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M/s}$ at this temperature.

<Answer>

Solve $\text{rate} = k[\ce{Q}]^2$ for the concentration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Q}]^2 &= \frac{5.00 \times 10^{-6}}{2.00 \times 10^{-3}} \\
&= 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Q}] &= \sqrt{2.50 \times 10^{-3}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.0500}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

This rate lies between the rates of experiments 1 and 2, and so does this concentration.

</Answer>

</Part>

| 105 to 245 | 4.166 | 0.02976 |
