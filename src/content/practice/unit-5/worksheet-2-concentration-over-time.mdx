Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One habit runs through this set: say *which* plot is a straight line, not just "the graph".

You will need these relationships. Each order has an integrated rate law and a half-life.

Zero order:

$$
[\ce{A}] = -kt + [\ce{A}]_0
$$

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{[\ce{A}]_0}{2k}
$$

First order:

$$
\ln[\ce{A}] = -kt + \ln[\ce{A}]_0
$$

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}
$$

Second order:

$$
\frac{1}{[\ce{A}]} = kt + \frac{1}{[\ce{A}]_0}
$$

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[\ce{A}]_0}
$$

The half-life formulas for zero order and second order are not on the AP equation sheet. The parts that need them are marked "Beyond the exam".

<Question number={2} topic="5.3">

A first-order reaction has $k = 0.0250\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $[\ce{A}]_0 = 0.500\ \text{M}$.

<Part label="a">

Find $[\ce{A}]$ after 60.0 s.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln[\ce{A}] &= -(0.0250)(60.0) \\
&\quad + \ln(0.500) \\
&= -1.50 - 0.693 \\
&= -2.193
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
[\ce{A}] = e^{-2.193} = \mathbf{0.112}\ \text{M}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Find the half-life.

<Answer>

27.7 s

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

After two half-lives, what fraction remains, and does your answer to part a look consistent?

<Answer>

One quarter, so 0.125 M at 55.4 s. The value at 60 s is slightly lower, 0.112 M, which is exactly what it should be.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="5.3">

A second-order reaction has $k = 0.15\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ and $[\ce{A}]_0 = 0.20\ \text{M}$.

<Part label="a">

Find $[\ce{A}]$ after 100 s.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{[\ce{A}]} &= (0.15)(100) + \frac{1}{0.20} \\
&= 15 + 5.0 = 20
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
[\ce{A}] = \mathbf{0.050}\ \text{M}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b" beyond>

Find the half-life.

<Answer>

33 s

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c" beyond>

After the first half-life, will the second take the same time, less, or more? Justify your answer.

<Answer>

More. For second order $t_{1/2} = 1/(k[\ce{A}]_0)$ depends on the starting concentration, and once half has reacted the remaining concentration is lower, so the next half-life is twice as long.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} topic="5.3">

A zero-order reaction has $k = 0.020\ \text{M/s}$ and $[\ce{A}]_0 = 1.00\ \text{M}$.

<Part label="a" beyond>

Find $t_{1/2}$.

<Answer>

25 s

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Find $[\ce{A}]$ after 30 s.

<Answer>

0.40 M

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

At what time does the reactant run out completely? What does that tell you about zero-order behavior?

<Answer>

At 50 s, since the concentration falls linearly. Zero-order kinetics cannot continue past that point. A real reaction stops being zero order once the reactant becomes scarce, because the mechanism causing the saturation (a full catalyst surface, say) can no longer be maintained.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="5.3">

A student writes "the graph was linear, so the reaction is first order." Explain why this earns no credit, and write the sentence that would.

<Answer>

All three orders produce *some* linear plot, so the statement does not distinguish them. The answer must name the quantity plotted: "the plot of $\ln[\ce{A}]$ against time is linear, therefore the reaction is first order in A."

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="5.3">

Why is a constant half-life such strong evidence of first-order kinetics?

<Answer>

Of the three expressions, only $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$ contains no $[\ce{A}]_0$. For zero order the half-life shortens as the reaction proceeds and for second order it lengthens, so a half-life that does not change with concentration is a signature of first order.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={8} topic="5.3">

A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8.0 days. A sample starts at 40.0 mg.

<Part label="a">

The mass remaining after 24 days: <Blank />

<Answer>

5.0 mg

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

How did you get it without using the integrated rate law?

<Answer>

24 days is exactly three half-lives, so the mass halves three times:

$$
40.0 \to 20.0 \to 10.0 \to 5.0\ \text{mg}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Radioactive decay is always first order. What does that imply about the time to go from 40.0 to 20.0 mg versus 20.0 to 10.0 mg?

<Answer>

the same: 8.0 days each

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
