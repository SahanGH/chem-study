Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One habit matters throughout: before you write an ionic equation, decide which acids and bases are strong and which are weak. Where an equation depends on it, the question tells you which is which.

One solubility rule is expected from memory: treat every sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salt as soluble. Any other solubility you need is given in the question.

State the kind of reaction first, then write the equation. For every species you keep whole, be ready to say why.

## Questions

<Question number={1} topic="4.7">

Classify each reaction as precipitation, acid–base or oxidation–reduction (redox). Ask all three questions every time, one for each kind, because a reaction can be more than one kind, and it can be none.

<Part label="a">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2 K(s) + Br2(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2 KBr(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Answer>

Redox (oxidation–reduction).

**Did dissolved ions join into a solid?** No. A solid forms, but from two elements, not from ions in a solution.

**Did a proton pass from one species to another?** No. There is no hydrogen in the equation.

**Did any oxidation number change?** Yes. Potassium goes from 0 to $+1$ and bromine goes from 0 to $-1$, so electrons pass from the potassium to the bromine.

This reaction is one kind only.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HNO3(aq) + LiOH(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{LiNO3(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Answer>

Acid–base.

**Did dissolved ions join into a solid?** No. No solid forms.

**Did a proton pass from one species to another?** Yes. The nitric acid ends as the nitrate ion, so it lost a proton. The hydroxide ion ends as water, so it gained one. Nitric acid is a strong acid, so in the solution that proton was already $\ce{H+(aq)}$, as in question 3(b): what happens in the beaker is $\ce{H+}$ passing to $\ce{OH-}$.

**Did any oxidation number change?** No. Hydrogen is $+1$, nitrogen $+5$, oxygen $-2$ and lithium $+1$ on both sides.

This reaction is one kind only.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{K2CrO4(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbCrO4(s) + 2 KNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Answer>

Precipitation.

**Did dissolved ions join into a solid?** Yes. Lead(II) ions and chromate ions join into solid lead(II) chromate.

**Did a proton pass from one species to another?** No. There is no hydrogen in the equation.

**Did any oxidation number change?** No. Lead is $+2$, chromium $+6$, nitrogen $+5$, potassium $+1$ and oxygen $-2$ on both sides.

This reaction is one kind only.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} topic="4.2">

Write the complete ionic and net ionic equations for this reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ba(NO3)2(aq) + K2SO4(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{BaSO4(s) + 2 KNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Complete ionic equation: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + 2 NO3^-(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{2 K+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{BaSO4(s) + 2 K+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{2 NO3^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Spectator ions: <Blank />

<Answer>

$\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Net ionic equation: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{BaSO4(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="4.2">

Write net ionic equations for these pairs of solutions.

<Part label="a">

$$
\ce{Na2S(aq) + Zn(NO3)2(aq)}
$$

Zinc sulfide is insoluble.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn^2+(aq) + S^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{ZnS(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Precipitation. $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ are spectator ions: sodium nitrate is soluble, by the rule.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$$
\ce{HBr(aq) + NaOH(aq)}
$$

Hydrobromic acid is a strong acid, and sodium hydroxide is a strong base.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H+(aq) + OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Acid–base. $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Br-}$ are spectator ions, because sodium bromide is soluble, by the rule, and water is written whole.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$$
\ce{HC2H3O2(aq) + KOH(aq)}
$$

Acetic acid is a weak acid, and potassium hydroxide is a strong base.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HC2H3O2(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C2H3O2^-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Acid–base. Acetic acid is weak, so it is written whole. $\ce{K+}$ is the only spectator ion.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

$$
\ce{NH3(aq) + HCl(aq)}
$$

Ammonia is a weak base, and hydrochloric acid is a strong acid.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{NH3(aq) + H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{NH4+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Acid–base. Ammonia is a weak base, so it is written whole. $\ce{Cl-}$ is the only spectator ion.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} topic="4.2">

Explain why parts (b) and (c) of question 3 have different net ionic equations, even though both are "an acid reacting with a strong base."

<Answer>

Hydrobromic acid is a strong acid. In water essentially all of its molecules break up into ions, so the species that reacts is $\ce{H+}$, and $\ce{Br-}$ is a spectator ion. Acetic acid is weak. Nearly all of its molecules are still whole, so the whole molecule is written, and it passes its proton directly to the hydroxide ion. The acetate ion is formed, so it does not cancel.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="4.7">

Predict the products, write the balanced molecular equation, and state whether a precipitate forms.

<Part label="a">

$$
\ce{AgNO3(aq) + K2CrO4(aq)}
$$

Silver chromate is insoluble.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2 AgNO3(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{K2CrO4(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Ag2CrO4(s) + 2 KNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Yes, a precipitate forms: silver chromate is insoluble. Potassium nitrate is soluble, by the rule.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$$
\ce{NaCl(aq) + Mg(NO3)2(aq)}
$$

Magnesium chloride is soluble.

<Answer>

No reaction. Trading partners would give $\ce{NaNO3}$ and $\ce{MgCl2}$. Sodium nitrate is soluble, by the rule for sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts, and the question says that magnesium chloride is soluble, so no precipitate forms. None of the four ions has a proton to give, and no oxidation number changes. All four ions stay dissolved and unchanged, so there is no equation to write.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="4.8">

Classify and explain.

<Part label="a">

Is $\ce{NH3}$ a Brønsted–Lowry base? Explain.

<Answer>

Yes. A Brønsted–Lowry base is a species that takes a proton, and ammonia takes one and becomes $\ce{NH4+}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b" beyond>

By an older definition, a base is a substance that gives hydroxide ions in water (an Arrhenius base). Is $\ce{NH3}$ an Arrhenius base? Explain the difference between the two definitions.

<Answer>

It depends on how strictly the older definition is read. Ammonia has no $\ce{OH-}$ of its own to release, so read strictly it is not an Arrhenius base. Its solution does hold more hydroxide ions than pure water, because a little of the ammonia takes a proton from water and leaves $\ce{OH-}$ behind, so a looser reading counts it.

The difference: the older definition looks for hydroxide ions in water, and the Brønsted–Lowry definition looks only at which species takes the proton, so it needs no OH in the formula.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

List the six common strong acids.

<Answer>

$\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$, $\ce{HI}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$ (its first proton) and $\ce{HClO4}$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="4.2">

Many different pairs of solutions give this net ionic equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Write molecular equations for two of them.

<Answer>

For example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{AgNO3(aq) + KCl(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s) + KNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Any pair is right if one solution supplies $\ce{Ag+}$, the other supplies $\ce{Cl-}$, and the two ions left over stay dissolved and unchanged, as spectator ions. Silver nitrate with ammonium chloride works, and so does silver nitrate with hydrochloric acid, a strong acid.

</Answer>

</Question>
