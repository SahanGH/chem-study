## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide" topic="4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.5" points={10}>

A student investigates the reaction between aqueous lead(II) nitrate and aqueous potassium iodide. She mixes 50.0 mL of 0.100 M $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ with 50.0 mL of 0.100 M $\ce{KI}$. Both solutions are clear and colorless. She observes a bright yellow solid.

<Part label="a">

Write the balanced molecular equation, including states.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2 KI(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbI2(s) + 2 KNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Write the net ionic equation and identify the spectator ions.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2 I^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbI2(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The spectator ions are $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Determine the limiting reactant, showing the comparison that justifies your answer.

<Answer>

Turn both amounts into moles, with each volume in liters.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n(\ce{Pb^2+}) \\
&= (0.0500\ \text{L}) \\
&\quad \times (0.100\ \text{mol/L}) \\
&= 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The potassium iodide solution has the same volume and the same molarity, so

$$
n(\ce{I-}) = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
$$

The two amounts are equal, so their sizes alone settle nothing. Set them against the equation. The ratio required is 1 $\ce{Pb^2+}$ : 2 $\ce{I-}$.

Suppose all the $\ce{I-}$ reacts. It uses half as many moles of $\ce{Pb^2+}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\tfrac12(5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}) \\
&= 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\ \ce{Pb^2+}
\end{aligned}
$$

There is $5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$ of $\ce{Pb^2+}$, twice as much as that. So iodide runs out first, while some $\ce{Pb^2+}$ is left: $\ce{I-}$, from the potassium iodide, is the limiting reactant, and $\ce{Pb^2+}$ is in excess.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the mass of $\ce{PbI2}$ (molar mass 461.0 g/mol) produced.

<Answer>

The limiting reactant sets the amount of product: one mole of the solid for every two moles of iodide.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n(\ce{PbI2}) \\
&= \tfrac12(5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}) \\
&= 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= (2.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}) \\
&\quad \times (461.0\ \text{g/mol}) \\
&= 1.15\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

On paper, make a particle drawing of the mixture *after* the reaction is complete. Show at least six particles, label your symbols, and leave out water.

<Answer>

Water is left out, every symbol is labeled, and each $\ce{NO3-}$ is drawn as one particle. A correct drawing shows three things.

- **The precipitate as a cluster.** A cluster of $\ce{PbI2}$ solid: $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{I-}$ ions packed together in one place, two $\ce{I-}$ for every $\ce{Pb^2+}$. Every ion touches ions of the opposite charge, and no two cations or two anions touch.
- **The ions that did not react, still apart.** The spectator ions, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$, are separated in the solution: each is drawn dissolved, on its own, and there are as many of each as before the reaction.
- **The reactant in excess.** Iodide was limiting, so no $\ce{I-}$ is left dissolved. The leftover $\ce{Pb^2+}$ ions, half the original amount, are still dissolved, each on its own.

Here is one set of counts that works. The two solutions hold equal moles of lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide, so start from equal numbers of formula units, say 4 of each. That is 4 $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and 8 $\ce{NO3-}$ from one solution, and 4 $\ce{K+}$ and 4 $\ce{I-}$ from the other. The 4 $\ce{I-}$ take 2 $\ce{Pb^2+}$. The drawing then shows:

- a cluster of 2 $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and 4 $\ce{I-}$;
- still dissolved and apart, 2 $\ce{Pb^2+}$, 8 $\ce{NO3-}$ and 4 $\ce{K+}$, which is 14 ions.

Every ion that was there before mixing is in the drawing, and the charge is still zero. The dissolved ions carry $+4$ on the $\ce{Pb^2+}$, $+4$ on the $\ce{K+}$ and $-8$ on the $\ce{NO3-}$. The cluster carries $+4$ and $-4$.

Your drawing may start from any other even number of each formula unit; the smallest that works has 10 ions. A drawing that matches the amounts in this question always has this: no $\ce{I-}$ dissolved, and for each $\ce{Pb^2+}$ in the cluster, 2 $\ce{I-}$ in the cluster with it and, still dissolved, 1 $\ce{Pb^2+}$, 4 $\ce{NO3-}$ and 2 $\ce{K+}$. The rubric's two points ask for less: the cluster with the spectator ions apart, and the leftover $\ce{Pb^2+}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="f">

The student claims the yellow color alone proves a chemical reaction occurred. Evaluate this claim.

<Answer>

The claim goes too far. A new color is possible evidence of a chemical change, not proof. In general a color can appear with nothing new made, as when colored solutions are mixed. That does not fit here, because both solutions were colorless, but one observation alone is still not proof. The stronger evidence is that a solid formed when two clear solutions were mixed, with nothing cooled and nothing evaporated, so it is hard to explain without a new substance. Even that is strong evidence, not proof.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="A redox reaction" topic="4.7">

Potassium permanganate reacts with oxalic acid in a solution made acidic with sulfuric acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2 KMnO4 + 5 H2C2O4 +} \\
&\quad \ce{3 H2SO4 ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2 MnSO4 + 10 CO2 +} \\
&\quad \ce{K2SO4 + 8 H2O}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Oxidation number of Mn on each side: <Blank />

<Answer>

In $\ce{KMnO4}$, K is $+1$ and O is $-2$, and the sum is zero:

$$
1 + x + 4(-2) = 0
$$

so Mn is $+7$. $\ce{MnSO4}$ holds $\ce{Mn^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$, and a one-atom ion has its charge as its oxidation number, so Mn is $+2$.

$$
+7 \to +2
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Oxidation number of C in $\ce{H2C2O4}$ and in $\ce{CO2}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

In $\ce{H2C2O4}$, H is $+1$ and O is $-2$, and the sum is zero:

$$
2(+1) + 2x + 4(-2) = 0
$$

so each C is $+3$. In $\ce{CO2}$,

$$
x + 2(-2) = 0
$$

so C is $+4$.

$$
+3 \to +4
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c" beyond>

The species that is reduced is called the oxidizing agent, and the species that is oxidized is called the reducing agent. Oxidizing agent and reducing agent: <Blank />

<Answer>

$\ce{KMnO4}$ is the oxidizing agent; $\ce{H2C2O4}$ is the reducing agent.

The oxidation number of Mn falls, so $\ce{KMnO4}$ is reduced. The oxidation number of C rises, so $\ce{H2C2O4}$ is oxidized.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
