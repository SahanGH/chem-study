Every particle drawing must conserve atoms and charge. Label your particles or give a key, so that a reader can tell what each of your circles represents.

## Questions

<Question number={1} topic="4.4">

Classify each as a physical or a chemical change, and give the reason at the level of the particles.

<Part label="a">

Water boiling: <Blank />

<Answer>

Physical: the $\ce{H2O}$ molecules separate from one another, and the bonds inside each molecule are untouched.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Copper turning green outdoors: <Blank />

<Answer>

Chemical: bonds form. Copper atoms become ions in a new green compound, made with substances from the air and rain.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Sugar dissolving in tea: <Blank />

<Answer>

Physical: the sugar molecules disperse intact among the water molecules. No bond inside a molecule is broken.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Baking soda fizzing in vinegar: <Blank />

<Answer>

Chemical: bonds are broken and formed, and $\ce{CO2}$ molecules are made that were in neither starting substance.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} topic="4.4">

Dissolving $\ce{NaCl}$ is often classified as a physical change, yet ionic bonds are broken. Reconcile these two statements.

<Answer>

What the particles do is not in doubt. In the crystal, sodium ions and chloride ions are held together by ionic bonds. In the water, those bonds are broken: the ions are separated, and each one ends up surrounded by water molecules that are attracted to it, which is an ion–dipole attraction. What to call the change can be argued both ways.

**It looks physical.** No new substance forms. The same $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are present, and evaporating the water recovers the original solid. On this argument, a chemical change requires new substances, not merely separated ones.

**It looks chemical.** Ionic bonds are chemical bonds, and dissolving broke them. A process that breaks chemical bonds is typically classified as chemical.

Both arguments can be defended, and both start from the same facts about the particles. Either classification is accepted with its reason.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="4.1">

A student mixes two clear solutions. The mixture becomes warm and slightly cloudy.

<Part label="a">

Name two pieces of evidence suggesting a chemical reaction.

<Answer>

The temperature rise, which shows that heat is given off, and the cloudiness, which suggests that a precipitate is forming. Each is possible evidence of a chemical reaction, not proof. The cloudiness is the stronger of the two: a solid that appears when two clear solutions are mixed is hard to explain without a new substance.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Give one alternative explanation for each observation that does *not* require a chemical reaction.

<Answer>

The warmth: mixing alone can give off heat. Diluting a concentrated solution with more water can warm it, with no new substance made.

The cloudiness: it could be one of the dissolved solids coming back out of its solution unchanged, because it dissolves less well in the mixture, or a liquid that does not mix with water dispersing through it as droplets, not a new compound.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

What single additional test would most strengthen the claim?

<Answer>

Filter off the solid and identify it, to see whether it is a compound that was in neither original solution.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} topic="4.3">

Picture a particle drawing of a mixture before a reaction. Filled circles are $\ce{A}$ atoms, and open circles joined in pairs are $\ce{B2}$ molecules. The box holds 6 atoms of $\ce{A}$ and 3 molecules of $\ce{B2}$.

<Part label="a">

The reaction is

$$
\ce{2 A + B2 -> 2 AB}
$$

How many $\ce{AB}$ units form?

<Answer>

6

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Which reactant is limiting?

<Answer>

Neither: the two reactants are present in the exact ratio of the coefficients, so they run out together.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

On paper, draw the "after" box.

<Answer>

The drawing shows 6 $\ce{AB}$ units, each one filled circle bonded to one open circle, and nothing left over. Atom check: 6 atoms of $\ce{A}$ and 6 atoms of $\ce{B}$, before and after.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="4.3">

On paper, make a particle drawing of what is present when $\ce{K2SO4(s)}$ dissolves in water. Show 3 formula units' worth, and label your symbols. Leave out the water molecules, and say on the drawing that you have done so.

<Answer>

The drawing shows 6 separate $\ce{K+}$ ions and 3 separate $\ce{SO4^2-}$ ions, randomly distributed. Each sulfate ion is one labeled particle. The ions are never drawn as bonded $\ce{K2SO4}$ units. Caption: "water omitted for clarity."

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="4.3">

A student draws the "after" box for this reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The student's drawing shows scattered $\ce{Ag+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions plus bonded $\ce{NaNO3}$ pairs. Identify both errors.

<Answer>

$\ce{AgCl}$ is insoluble and must be drawn as a solid, a cluster of ions packed together, not as free ions. $\ce{NaNO3}$ is soluble and must be drawn as separate $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ ions, not as bonded pairs. The student has the two products the wrong way round.

</Answer>

</Question>
