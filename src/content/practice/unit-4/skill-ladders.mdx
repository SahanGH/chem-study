There are twelve ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions on the same skill, with no help. Work alone, and work all four before you check any of them.

**The habit that decides this unit.** A calculation that goes from an amount of one substance to an amount of another runs the same three-step road: **to moles**, **through the balanced equation**, **back out**. Grams never talk to grams directly. If you find yourself comparing the masses of two substances, or the volumes of two solutions, you have skipped the middle step.

**One scope note.** The names "oxidizing agent" and "reducing agent", solubility rules beyond the one for sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts, and Lewis acids and bases are not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for them.

## Ladder 1: Balancing equations

Change **coefficients** only. Changing a subscript changes the substance, so it is never allowed.

<Worked title="A combustion">

Balance this equation:

$$
\ce{C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}
$$

**Carbon first** (it appears in one place on each side): 3 carbons on the left, so 3 $\ce{CO2}$.

**Hydrogen next:** 8 hydrogens on the left, so 4 $\ce{H2O}$.

**Oxygen last.** It is in two products, so leave it until the rest is fixed. The right side now has

$$
3(2) + 4(1) = 10
$$

oxygens, so 5 $\ce{O2}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{3CO2(g) + 4H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Check every element:** C 3 and 3, H 8 and 8, O 10 and 10. ✓

**Why oxygen last:** balancing the element that appears in the most places first forces you to redo it every time something else changes.

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Net ionic equations

Write what actually changes. A dissolved ionic compound, a strong acid and a strong base are already separated into ions in the solution; an ion that appears unchanged on both sides is a spectator ion and cancels.

<Worked title="Molecular to net ionic">

Write the net ionic equation for this reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Split what is written as ions.** $\ce{AgNO3}$, $\ce{NaCl}$ and $\ce{NaNO3}$ are all soluble and ionic, so in the solution all are separated into ions. $\ce{AgCl}$ is a *solid* and stays written as one unit.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ag^+(aq) + NO3^-(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{Na^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s) + Na^+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{NO3^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Cancel the spectator ions:** $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ appear unchanged on both sides.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The rule for what stays together:** only a dissolved ionic compound, a strong acid in water and a strong base in water are written as separate ions. Everything else is written as a whole unit: solids, liquids, gases, weak acids, weak bases, and dissolved molecular substances that are not acids or bases.

</Worked>

## Ladder 3: Physical and chemical change

A change is typically called chemical when it makes a new substance: chemical bonds are broken or formed, and the identity changes. It is typically called physical when it only rearranges what is already there: only the attractions between particles change.

<Worked title="The hard case">

Classify: (i) ice melting, (ii) iron rusting, (iii) $\ce{NaCl}$ dissolving.

**(i) Physical.** The $\ce{H2O}$ molecules are unchanged; only the arrangement and the intermolecular forces between them change.

**(ii) Chemical.** Iron, oxygen and water form rust, which is mostly iron(III) oxide: a new substance with new bonds.

**(iii) Either, with its reason.** This is the one that causes arguments. What the particles do is not in doubt: the ionic lattice is pulled apart and each ion is surrounded by water molecules, so strong new ion–dipole attractions do form.

*The argument that it is physical.* No new *substance* is made: the ions in solution are the same $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ that were in the crystal, and ion–dipole attractions are attractions between particles, not bonds. Evaporating the water returns the original solid unchanged.

*The argument that it is chemical.* The ionic bonds of the crystal are chemical bonds, and dissolving breaks them.

Either classification is accepted with its reason. The label alone, with no reason, explains nothing.

</Worked>

## Ladder 4: Mole-to-mole stoichiometry

The coefficients of a balanced equation are a *mole* ratio, never a mass ratio. That ratio is the bridge from the moles of one substance to the moles of another.

<Worked title="The three-step road">

How many grams of $\ce{NH3}$ form from 28.0 g of $\ce{N2}$, with hydrogen in excess?

$$
\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}
$$

**To moles:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&28.0/28.02 \\
&= 0.9993\ \text{mol}\ \ce{N2}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Through the equation:** the ratio $\ce{N2}$ : $\ce{NH3}$ is 1 : 2, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.9993 \times 2 \\
&= 1.999\ \text{mol}\ \ce{NH3}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Back out:**

$$
1.999 \times 17.03 = \mathbf{34.0}\ \text{g}
$$

An extra digit is carried through the steps, and the answer is rounded at the end.

**The check that catches the commonest error:** mass is conserved overall, but it is *not* transferred one-for-one between species. 28.0 g of $\ce{N2}$ does not give 28.0 g of $\ce{NH3}$; the extra mass comes from the hydrogen.

</Worked>

## Ladder 5: Limiting reactant

Convert both reactants to moles, then test them *against the equation's ratio*. Comparing raw moles, or masses, is the standard way to get this wrong.

<Worked title="Testing against the ratio">

A mixture of 28.0 g $\ce{N2}$ and 4.00 g $\ce{H2}$ reacts by this equation:

$$
\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}
$$

Which is limiting, and what mass of $\ce{NH3}$ forms?

**To moles:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{N2}) &= 28.0/28.02 \\
&= 0.999\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{H2}) &= 4.00/2.016 \\
&= 1.98\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

An extra digit of the hydrogen amount, 1.984 mol, is carried into the steps below.

**Test against the ratio.** Consuming all the $\ce{N2}$ would require

$$
3 \times 0.999 = 3.00\ \text{mol}
$$

of $\ce{H2}$, but only 1.98 mol is available. So **hydrogen is limiting**.

Note that hydrogen is limiting despite there being *about twice as many moles* of it. The ratio, not the raw count, decides.

**Yield from the limiting reactant only:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{NH3}) &= \tfrac{2}{3}(1.984) \\
&= 1.32\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= 1.32 \times 17.03 \\
&= \mathbf{22.5}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Excess left over:** the $\ce{N2}$ used is

$$
1.984/3 = 0.661\ \text{mol}
$$

so what remains is

$$
0.999 - 0.661 = 0.338\ \text{mol}
$$

which is 9.47 g.

</Worked>

## Ladder 6: Percent yield

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{percent yield} \\
&= \frac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}} \times 100
\end{aligned}
$$

The theoretical yield always comes from the **limiting** reactant.

<Worked title="Yield from the previous ladder">

The ammonia synthesis of the previous ladder has a theoretical yield of 22.5 g. A run produces 18.0 g. Find the percent yield and give two reasons it is below 100%.

$$
\frac{18.0}{22.5} \times 100 = \mathbf{80.0\%}
$$

**Reasons:** the reaction may not have gone to completion, since ammonia synthesis does not consume all its reactants (topic 7.1 says why), and product is lost during separation and purification. In other reactions, side reactions can also use up reactants.

**What a yield above 100% means:** not a better reaction, but an error. Almost always the product was still wet, or contained impurities.

</Worked>

## Ladder 7: Solution stoichiometry

Volume and concentration give moles: $n = MV$, with the volume in liters. From there it is the same three-step road as always. Where two solutions are mixed in this ladder, the volumes are taken to add.

<Worked title="Two solutions, a precipitate">

A 30.0 mL sample of 0.200 M $\ce{AgNO3}$ is mixed with 25.0 mL of 0.150 M $\ce{NaCl}$. What mass of $\ce{AgCl}$ forms? Silver chloride is insoluble.

**To moles:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{Ag+}) &= (0.0300)(0.200) \\
&= 0.00600\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{Cl-}) &= (0.0250)(0.150) \\
&= 0.00375\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Test the ratio:** the net ionic equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{AgCl(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

It is 1 : 1, so the smaller amount limits: **chloride**.

**Back out:** $n(\ce{AgCl})$ is 0.00375 mol, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= 0.00375 \times 143.32 \\
&= \mathbf{0.537}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

**If asked for a leftover concentration:** the excess $\ce{Ag+}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.00600 - 0.00375 \\
&= 0.00225\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

in the *combined* 55.0 mL, giving 0.0409 M. The silver ions are now spread through all of the mixture, so here the total volume is the one to divide by. This is not a titration question. In a titration, the analyte's concentration is its moles divided by the volume of the sample.

</Worked>

## Ladder 8: Titration

At the equivalence point the titrant added has exactly used up the analyte, so the moles of acid and base brought together are in exactly the ratio the equation demands. A titration calculation starts from that one sentence.

<Worked title="Finding an unknown concentration">

A 25.00 mL sample of $\ce{HCl}$ requires 22.40 mL of 0.150 M $\ce{NaOH}$ to reach the equivalence point. Find $[\ce{HCl}]$.

**Moles of base:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.02240)(0.150) \\
&= 0.00336\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Through the ratio:** the equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

It is 1 : 1, so $n(\ce{HCl})$ is 0.00336 mol.

**Back out:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{HCl}] &= 0.00336/0.02500 \\
&= \mathbf{0.134}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Watch for a ratio that is not 1 : 1.** With $\ce{H2SO4}$, one mole of acid supplies two of $\ce{H+}$, so it takes *twice* as much base, and forgetting that halves or doubles your answer.

</Worked>

They are equal.

Every number after it is too large, and so is the analyte's calculated concentration. It does not matter whether the titrant is the base, as in the worked example, or the acid, as in part (a).

## Ladder 9: Recognizing reaction types

Three families are the ones to recognize on the AP exam: precipitation (two solutions, an insoluble solid), acid–base (proton transfer), and redox (electron transfer, shown by changing oxidation numbers). A reaction can be more than one kind, and it can be none, so ask about all three.

<Worked title="Classify and justify">

Classify each reaction. Each takes place in water. Lead(II) iodide is insoluble, and copper(II) sulfate and zinc sulfate are soluble. Nitric acid is a strong acid, and sodium hydroxide is a strong base.

**(i)**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbI2 + 2KNO3}
\end{aligned}
$$

**(ii)**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HNO3 + NaOH ->} \\
&\quad \ce{NaNO3 + H2O}
\end{aligned}
$$

**(iii)**

$$
\ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}
$$

**(i) Precipitation.** Two aqueous solutions meet and an insoluble solid, $\ce{PbI2}$, forms.

**(ii) Acid–base.** A proton moves from the acid to the hydroxide ion; the net ionic equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**(iii) Redox.** Zinc goes from 0 to $+2$ (oxidized, loses electrons); copper goes from $+2$ to 0 (reduced, gains them). Changing oxidation numbers is the signature.

**The quick test for redox:** if any element's oxidation number changes, it is redox. If none changes, it is not.

</Worked>

<Question number={9} label="Your turn" topic="4.7">

<Part label="a">

## Ladder 10: Acid–base reactions

Brønsted–Lowry: an acid donates a proton, a base accepts one. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one $\ce{H+}$.

<Worked title="Pairs and strength">

Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in this equation, and explain why $\ce{NH3}$ is called a weak base.

$$
\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH^-}
$$

**Pairs.** $\ce{NH3}$ accepts a proton to become $\ce{NH4+}$: that is one pair, base and its conjugate acid. $\ce{H2O}$ donates a proton to become $\ce{OH-}$: that is the other, acid and its conjugate base.

**Weak, not strong.** When the reaction has settled, most of the ammonia is still $\ce{NH3}$. Only a small fraction has accepted a proton, so the solution contains far fewer $\ce{OH-}$ ions than a strong base of the same concentration would give. That is why the equation has a double arrow: the reaction settles with most of the $\ce{NH3}$ still there.

**Strength is about extent, not concentration.** A concentrated solution of a weak base is entirely possible.

</Worked>

## Ladder 11: Oxidation numbers

Six rules, in order of priority. Work down the list from the top. Once the rules used so far settle an atom's number, it stays.

- An atom of an element on its own (Zn, $\ce{O2}$) is 0.
- A one-atom ion is its charge.
- The oxidation numbers of all the atoms add up to the charge of the species: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for a polyatomic ion.
- Fluorine in a compound is $-1$.
- Hydrogen is $+1$ when it is bonded to a nonmetal, and $-1$ when it is bonded directly to a metal, as in a metal hydride.
- Oxygen is $-2$, except in peroxides, where it is $-1$.

<Worked title="Three species">

Find the oxidation number of the central atom in $\ce{MnO4-}$, $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ and $\ce{H2SO4}$.

**$\ce{MnO4-}$:** four oxygens at $-2$ give $-8$; the total must be $-1$.

$$
x + (-8) = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+7}
$$

**$\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$:** seven oxygens give $-14$; the total is $-2$; and there are **two** chromiums.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x + (-14) &= -2 \\
2x &= +12 \\
x &= \mathbf{+6}
\end{aligned}
$$

**$\ce{H2SO4}$:** two hydrogens give $+2$, four oxygens $-8$, total 0.

$$
2 + x - 8 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+6}
$$

**The slip to avoid:** forgetting to divide by the number of central atoms. In dichromate the answer is $+6$, not $+12$.

</Worked>

## Ladder 12: Balancing redox in acid

Split the reaction into two half-reactions and balance each: every element except oxygen and hydrogen first, then oxygen with $\ce{H2O}$, then hydrogen with $\ce{H+}$, and last the charge with electrons. The number of electrons lost must equal the number gained, so multiply the half-reactions until they match, then add. Check the *charge* as well as the atoms.

<Worked title="Permanganate and iron">

Balance this equation for a reaction in acidic solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MnO4^- + Fe^2+ + H+ ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mn^2+ + Fe^3+ + H2O}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The iron half-reaction.** There is one iron on each side, so the atoms are balanced. The left side is $+2$ and the right side is $+3$, so one electron goes on the right.

$$
\ce{Fe^2+(aq) -> Fe^3+(aq) + e^-}
$$

**The manganese half-reaction.** Start with every element except oxygen and hydrogen: there is one manganese on each side, so it is balanced.

**Then balance O with water and H with $\ce{H+}$.** Four oxygens on the left need 4 $\ce{H2O}$ on the right, which needs 8 $\ce{H+}$ on the left.

**Last, the charge, with electrons.** The left side is

$$
(-1) + 8(+1) = +7
$$

and the right side is $+2$, so 5 electrons go on the left.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MnO4^-(aq) + 8H^+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{5e^- ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mn^2+(aq) + 4H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Electron bookkeeping.** The oxidation numbers agree: Mn goes from $+7$ to $+2$, a gain of **5**, and Fe goes from $+2$ to $+3$, a loss of **1**. To make these equal, multiply the iron half-reaction by 5.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{5Fe^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{5Fe^3+(aq) + 5e^-}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Add, and cancel the electrons.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MnO4^-(aq) + 5Fe^2+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{8H^+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mn^2+(aq) + 5Fe^3+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{4H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Check the atoms.** Mn 1 and 1, Fe 5 and 5, O 4 and 4, H 8 and 8.

**Charge check: do this every time.** Left:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(-1) + 5(+2) + 8(+1) \\
&= +17
\end{aligned}
$$

Right:

$$
(+2) + 5(+3) = +17
$$

✓

If the charges do not match, the equation is wrong even if every atom balances.

</Worked>

How many electrons does each Cr gain going from $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ to $\ce{Cr^3+}$?

Cr goes from $+6$ to $+3$, a gain of **3** each; with two chromiums the ion takes **6** electrons.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2-(aq) +} \\

&\quad \ce{2Cr^3+(aq) + 7H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}

Each $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ion becomes $\ce{Fe^3+}$. How many $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ions are needed for each $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ ion in part (a)?

\ce{Fe^2+(aq) -> Fe^3+(aq) + e^-}

<Part label="c">

In this reaction, which species is oxidized?

$$
\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}
$$

<Answer>

$\ce{Zn}$

**$\ce{Zn}$**: it goes from 0 to $+2$, losing electrons. Its half-reaction is the oxidation, with the electrons on the right:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

The copper(II) ion gains them, in the reduction:

$$
\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Cu(s)}
$$

The electrons lost equal the electrons gained, so the two half-reactions add as they stand, the electrons cancel, and the sum is the equation of the question.

<Part label="d">

Why is a charge check necessary even when all atoms balance?

<Answer>

Electrons must balance too.

Because balancing atoms alone does not guarantee that electrons have been accounted for. A redox equation transfers charge, and if the totals differ between the two sides, electrons have been created or destroyed somewhere, which is impossible. The charge check catches what the atom count cannot.
