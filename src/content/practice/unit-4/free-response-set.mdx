This set covers all of Unit 4: topics 4.1 to 4.9. Unit 4 is worth 7 to 9 percent of the AP exam.

There are 5 questions worth 32 points. Give yourself about 50 minutes. You may use a calculator. Write out each answer in full before you open the worked answer and the scoring rubric.

The names "oxidizing agent" and "reducing agent", solubility rules beyond the one for sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts, and Lewis acids and bases are not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for them.

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Precipitation and solution stoichiometry" topic="4.2, 4.5, 4.7" points={10}>

A 40.0 mL sample of 0.150 M $\ce{Pb(NO3)2}$ is mixed with 50.0 mL of 0.200 M $\ce{KI}$. A bright yellow precipitate of $\ce{PbI2}$ (molar mass 461.0 g/mol) forms. Assume that the two volumes add and that the precipitation is complete.

<Part label="a">

Write the balanced net ionic equation.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbI2(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Determine the limiting reactant.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{Pb^2+}) &= 0.0400\ \text{L} \\
&\quad \times 0.150\ \text{mol/L} \\
&= 0.00600\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{I-}) &= 0.0500\ \text{L} \\
&\quad \times 0.200\ \text{mol/L} \\
&= 0.0100\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The net ionic equation needs **two** iodide ions for each lead(II) ion, so consuming all the $\ce{Pb^2+}$ would require

$$
2(0.00600) = 0.0120\ \text{mol}
$$

of iodide. That is more than the 0.0100 mol available.

**Iodide is limiting.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the mass of $\ce{PbI2}$ formed.

<Answer>

Iodide is limiting and the ratio is $2\ \ce{I-} : 1\ \ce{PbI2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{PbI2}) &= \frac{0.0100\ \text{mol}}{2} \\
&= 0.00500\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= 0.00500\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad \times 461.0\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= 2.305\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

To three significant figures, the mass is $\mathbf{2.31}$ g.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the concentration of lead(II) ion remaining in solution.

<Answer>

The lead(II) ion consumed is 0.00500 mol, so the amount that remains is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.00600 - 0.00500 \\
&= 0.00100\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The total volume is

$$
40.0 + 50.0 = 90.0\ \text{mL}
$$

The lead(II) ions that remain are now spread through all of the mixture, so their moles are divided by the combined volume:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Pb^2+}] &= \frac{0.00100\ \text{mol}}{0.0900\ \text{L}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.0111}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is not a titration question. In a titration, the analyte's concentration is its moles divided by the volume of the sample alone.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

Identify the spectator ions and explain what makes them spectators.

<Answer>

$\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$. Both are present, dissolved and unchanged, before and after the reaction, so they take no part in the chemical change and cancel from the equation.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>2 points for the net ionic equation with the 2 on iodide and the solid state shown; 1 point if it is written in full molecular form.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for both mole quantities; 1 point for applying the $2:1$ ratio rather than comparing moles directly; 1 point for iodide (or $\ce{KI}$). Comparing 0.00600 with 0.0100 and answering $\ce{Pb^2+}$ earns 1 point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for dividing by 2 for the ratio; 1 point for 2.31 g. The unrounded mass is 2.305 g, so 2.30 g earns the point as well.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for 0.00100 mol remaining; 1 point for dividing by the *combined* 90.0 mL.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="e" points={1}>Both spectator ions named, with a reason.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Redox and a dichromate titration" topic="4.6, 4.7, 4.9" points={10}>

Acidified dichromate oxidizes iron(II) in solution. The unbalanced equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2-(aq) + Fe^2+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cr^3+(aq) + Fe^3+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Assign the oxidation number of chromium on each side and state whether it is oxidized or reduced.

<Answer>

In $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$, oxygen is $-2$ and the oxidation numbers add up to the charge of the ion:

$$
2x + 7(-2) = -2
$$

So $x = \mathbf{+6}$. In $\ce{Cr^3+}$ the oxidation number is the charge of the ion, $\mathbf{+3}$.

The oxidation number falls, so chromium is **reduced**. The species that is reduced is the reactant that holds it, the dichromate ion.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Balance the equation in acidic solution.

<Answer>

Split the reaction into two half-reactions. Balance each one in atoms and then in charge, match the electrons, and add.

**The oxidation.** Each iron(II) ion loses 1 electron.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Fe^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e^-}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The reduction, starting with chromium.** Dichromate becomes chromium(III). There are 2 chromium atoms on the left, so 2 $\ce{Cr^3+}$ go on the right.

$$
\ce{Cr2O7^2- -> 2Cr^3+}
$$

**Then oxygen, with water.** The left side has 7 oxygen atoms and the right side has none, so add 7 $\ce{H2O}$ to the right.

$$
\ce{Cr2O7^2- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}
$$

**Then hydrogen, with hydrogen ions.** The right side now has 14 hydrogen atoms and the left side has none, so add 14 $\ce{H+}$ to the left.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Last, the charge, with electrons.** The left side is

$$
(-2) + 14(+1) = +12
$$

and the right side is

$$
2(+3) = +6
$$

The left is the more positive side, so 6 electrons go there.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2-(aq) + 14H+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{6e^- ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2Cr^3+(aq) + 7H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The oxidation numbers of part (a) agree. Each chromium gains 3 electrons and there are two of them, so the dichromate half-reaction takes **6** electrons.

**Match the electrons.** Iron loses 1 each, so six $\ce{Fe^2+}$ are needed. Multiply the iron half-reaction by 6.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{6Fe^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{6Fe^3+(aq) + 6e^-}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Add, and cancel the electrons.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2-(aq) + 6Fe^2+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{14H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2Cr^3+(aq) + 6Fe^3+(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{7H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Check.** Chromium: 2 and 2. Oxygen: 7 and 7. Iron: 6 and 6. Hydrogen: 14 and 14. The charge on the left is

$$
-2 + 12 + 14 = +24
$$

and on the right it is

$$
+6 + 18 = +24
$$

Both sides match, and no electrons are left. ✓

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A 20.00 mL sample of the iron(II) solution required 24.50 mL of 0.0150 M $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ to reach the endpoint, which is taken as the equivalence point. Calculate $[\ce{Fe^2+}]$.

<Answer>

Each $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ supplies one $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}) \\
&= 0.02450\ \text{L} \\
&\quad \times 0.0150\ \text{mol/L} \\
&= 3.675 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The balanced ratio is $1 : 6$, one $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ to six $\ce{Fe^2+}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n(\ce{Fe^2+}) \\
&= 6 \times 3.675 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol} \\
&= 2.205 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by the volume of the sample:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&[\ce{Fe^2+}] \\
&= \frac{2.205 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}}{0.02000\ \text{L}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.110}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

State what would happen to the calculated $[\ce{Fe^2+}]$ if the endpoint was overshot (titrant was run in past it), and why.

<Answer>

It would come out **too high**. The volume recorded is larger than the true volume at the equivalence point. So more dichromate seems to have reacted, and the calculated amount of iron(II) is too large.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for both oxidation numbers; 1 point for "reduced". The name "oxidizing agent" is not required.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for 6 electrons on the dichromate side; 1 point for the factor of 6 on iron; 1 point for $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ balanced (14 and 7). Charges not balancing caps this at 2 points. Either method earns these points: half-reactions, or counting the electrons from the oxidation numbers. A fully balanced final equation earns all 3 points, whatever the method.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={3}>1 point for the moles of dichromate; 1 point for the $1:6$ ratio; 1 point for 0.110 M. Omitting the ratio gives 0.0184 M and earns 1 point. An answer of 0.111 M, from rounding the moles to three figures on the way, earns the point as well.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for "too high"; 1 point for the reason, that the recorded volume exceeds the true volume at the equivalence point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Physical versus chemical change" topic="4.1, 4.4" points={4}>

<Part label="a">

Classify each as physical or chemical, with a one-line reason: (i) ice melting; (ii) iron rusting; (iii) table salt, $\ce{NaCl}$, dissolving in water.

<Answer>

**(i) Ice melting is physical.** The molecules are unchanged. Only the arrangement and the intermolecular forces between them change.

**(ii) Iron rusting is chemical.** New substances with new bonds form.

**(iii) Salt dissolving can be argued either way, and either classification is accepted with its reason.** What the particles do is not in doubt. The ionic bonds of the crystal are broken and the ions separate. Each ion ends up surrounded by water molecules, which hold it by ion–dipole attractions. The $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions themselves are unchanged.

*The argument for physical:* no new substance is made. The $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions in the solution are the same ions that were in the crystal, and the salt comes back when the water is evaporated.

*The argument for chemical:* ionic bonds are chemical bonds, and dissolving breaks the ionic bonds of the crystal.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Dissolving a salt is sometimes argued to be chemical because the ionic bonds of the crystal are broken. State the strongest argument that it is physical.

<Answer>

No new chemical substance is produced: the ions that were in the crystal are the same ions in solution, and evaporating the water recovers the original solid unchanged.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for classifying ice melting as physical, with its reason.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for classifying iron rusting as chemical, with its reason.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for classifying salt dissolving, with its reason. Either classification earns the point, physical or chemical, with a reason at the level of the particles: which bonds or attractions changed, or that the ions are unchanged and the salt can be recovered. An answer that says it can be argued either way, with the reason for each side, earns the point too. A reason that says no bonds are broken does not earn the point: the ionic bonds of the crystal are broken.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>Recoverability, or no new substance formed.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Acid–base titration" topic="4.2, 4.6, 4.8" points={4}>

A 25.00 mL sample of a weak acid, $\ce{HA}$, which has one proton to give, is titrated with 0.100 M $\ce{NaOH}$, a strong base. The endpoint occurs when 28.75 mL of the $\ce{NaOH}$ solution has been added, and it is taken as the equivalence point.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the concentration of the acid.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{OH-}) &= 0.02875\ \text{L} \\
&\quad \times 0.100\ \text{mol/L} \\
&= 0.002875\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

This equals $n(\ce{HA})$ at the equivalence point, because the acid has one proton to give.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{HA}] &= \frac{0.002875\ \text{mol}}{0.02500\ \text{L}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.115}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Write the net ionic equation for the reaction, given that $\ce{HA}$ is a *weak* acid.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{A^-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

In water, only some of a weak acid's molecules give up their proton, for most weak acids a small fraction. So it must be written whole, as the intact molecule $\ce{HA}$, rather than as $\ce{H+}$. Only $\ce{Na+}$ is a spectator ion.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for moles of base equal to moles of acid at the equivalence point; 1 point for 0.115 M.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the correct equation; 1 point for $\ce{HA}$ kept intact because it is weak. Writing $\ce{H+ + OH^- -> H2O}$ earns 0 for the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Equations and particle drawings" topic="4.2, 4.3, 4.5, 4.7" points={4}>

<Part label="a">

Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, which gives carbon dioxide and water.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CO2(g) + 3H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A particle drawing of the reactants shows 4 ethanol molecules and 9 oxygen molecules. Determine how many molecules of each species the drawing after the reaction should show. Assume that the complete combustion of part (a) is the only reaction.

<Answer>

The ratio needed is $1 : 3$, so 4 ethanol molecules would require 12 oxygen molecules. Only 9 are present, so **oxygen is limiting**. It is enough for

$$
\frac{9}{3} = 3\ \text{ethanol molecules}
$$

The drawing after the reaction should show these molecules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
3 \times 2 &= \mathbf{6}\ \ce{CO2} \\
3 \times 3 &= \mathbf{9}\ \ce{H2O} \\
4 - 3 &= \mathbf{1}\ \text{ethanol}
\end{aligned}
$$

No oxygen molecules are left: all 9 reacted.

The ethanol molecule is the one that did not react. It must be drawn, since it is still there.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>Coefficients 1, 3, 2, 3.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for identifying oxygen as limiting; 1 point for 6 $\ce{CO2}$, 9 $\ce{H2O}$ *and* the leftover ethanol. Omitting the excess reactant is a common error and loses the point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>
