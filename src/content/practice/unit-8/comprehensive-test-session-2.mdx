## Part E: Molecular structure

Four questions on how the way a molecule is put together sets the strength of an acid. You will weigh two claims about oxygen atoms against measured values, match a set of p$K_a$ values to the structures they belong to, and decide when two rules for hydrogen compounds can rank a pair of acids and when only a measurement can.

<Question number={1} title="An extra oxygen atom in peracetic acid" topic="8.6" points={5}>

Peracetic acid is a sanitizer used in food plants. Its molecule is an acetic acid molecule with one more oxygen atom in it.

- **Acetic acid** is CH₃C(=O)–O–H. One carbon atom has a double bond to one oxygen atom and a single bond to a second oxygen atom, and that second oxygen atom holds the hydrogen.
- **Peracetic acid** is CH₃C(=O)–O–O–H. The same carbon atom has a double bond to one oxygen atom and a single bond to a second. The second oxygen atom is bonded to a third oxygen atom, and the third one holds the hydrogen.

In each acid the hydrogen atom on oxygen is the acidic one. Use these values, at 25 °C:

| Acid | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|
| acetic acid | 4.76 |
| peracetic acid | 8.20 |

<Part label="a">

Identify the stronger acid, and calculate how many times as large its $K_a$ is as the other acid's.

<Answer>

**Acetic acid** is the stronger acid: it has the lower p$K_a$, so it has the larger $K_a$.

A p$K_a$ is minus the logarithm of a $K_a$, so a difference between two p$K_a$ values is a ratio of the two $K_a$ values. The difference is

$$
8.20 - 4.76 = 3.44
$$

and the ratio is

$$
10^{3.44} = 2{,}754
$$

The $K_a$ of acetic acid is **about 2,800 times** that of peracetic acid.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Each acid gives up its acidic hydrogen as a proton. For each conjugate base, state which atom or atoms carry the negative charge, and whether a second Lewis diagram can be drawn that moves the charge to another atom.

<Answer>

**The acetate ion, CH₃C(=O)–O⁻.** The oxygen atom that lost the proton is bonded to the carbon atom that holds the C=O double bond. A second diagram can be drawn with the double bond to that oxygen atom and the negative charge on the other one. The real ion is the blend of the two diagrams, so **two oxygen atoms share the charge**, half each.

**The peracetate ion, CH₃C(=O)–O–O⁻.** The oxygen atom that lost the proton is the outer one. Its only bond is to the middle oxygen atom. It is not bonded to the carbon atom, so the C=O double bond cannot be redrawn to reach it, and no second diagram moves the charge onto the C=O oxygen atom. **One oxygen atom carries the whole charge.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student says: "One more oxygen atom makes an acid stronger." Use the two p$K_a$ values and your answer to part b to decide whether the claim holds for this pair.

<Answer>

The claim **does not hold** for this pair. Peracetic acid has three oxygen atoms and acetic acid has two, yet peracetic acid is the weaker acid, by a factor of about 2,800 in $K_a$ (p$K_a$ 8.20 against 4.76).

What matters is where the added atom sits and what it does to the ion left behind. In peracetic acid the added oxygen atom sits between the carbon atom and the O–H group. That moves the charge of the conjugate base off the –COO group, where two oxygen atoms would share it, and onto one outer oxygen atom. An ion with its charge on one atom is less stable than an ion that shares it, so it holds a proton more tightly, and its acid is weaker.

The added oxygen atom is electronegative, and it does pull some electron density toward itself. The measured values show that this pull is worth less than the sharing that was lost.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Identify the stronger base of the two conjugate bases, with a reason, and calculate $K_b$ of each at 25 °C, where $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$.

<Answer>

**The peracetate ion** is the stronger base. The weaker acid has the stronger conjugate base, and peracetic acid is the weaker acid. In terms of structure: its charge sits on one oxygen atom, so the ion has more to gain by bonding to a proton than acetate has.

For a conjugate pair, $K_b$ is $K_w$ divided by $K_a$. First find each $K_a$ from its p$K_a$.

Acetic acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &= 10^{-4.76} \\
&= 1.738 \times 10^{-5}
\end{aligned}
$$

So for the acetate ion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_b &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.738 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= \mathbf{5.8 \times 10^{-10}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Peracetic acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &= 10^{-8.20} \\
&= 6.310 \times 10^{-9}
\end{aligned}
$$

So for the peracetate ion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_b &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{6.310 \times 10^{-9}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.6 \times 10^{-6}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two values of $K_b$ differ by the same factor of about 2,800 as the two values of $K_a$, the other way around: the acid that is 2,800 times weaker leaves a base that is 2,800 times stronger.

</Answer>

</Part>

Acetic acid with no factor, or the difference 3.44 given as the factor, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for both ions: in acetate two oxygen atoms share the charge (two diagrams), and in peracetate the outer oxygen atom carries all of it, because that atom is not bonded to the carbon atom of the C=O group. Rejecting the claim from the p$K_a$ values alone, with nothing about the ion, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for the peracetate ion with a reason: it is the conjugate base of the weaker acid, or its charge is not shared; 1 point for both values of $K_b$, $5.8 \times 10^{-10}$ for acetate ($5.7 \times 10^{-10}$ to $5.9 \times 10^{-10}$ is accepted) and $1.6 \times 10^{-6}$ for peracetate, found as $K_w$ over $K_a$ or from 14.00 minus the p$K_a$. The two values assigned to the wrong ions earn 0 points for the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Where the chlorine atom sits" topic="8.6" points={4}>

Butanoic acid is CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH. Number its carbon atoms from the –COOH end: carbon 1 is the –COOH carbon, and carbon 4 is the CH₃ carbon. Three other acids are made by replacing one hydrogen atom on carbon 2, 3 or 4 with a chlorine atom:

- 2-chlorobutanoic acid, CH₃CH₂CHClCOOH
- 3-chlorobutanoic acid, CH₃CHClCH₂COOH
- 4-chlorobutanoic acid, ClCH₂CH₂CH₂COOH

The electronegativities are 2.2 for hydrogen, 2.6 for carbon and 3.2 for chlorine.

The p$K_a$ values of the four acids at 25 °C, in order of size and not yet matched to the acids, are:

$$
2.86 \quad 4.05 \quad 4.52 \quad 4.82
$$

<Part label="a">

Match each p$K_a$ to its acid, and justify the match from the structures.

<Answer>

| Acid | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|
| butanoic acid | **4.82** |
| 4-chlorobutanoic acid | **4.52** |
| 3-chlorobutanoic acid | **4.05** |
| 2-chlorobutanoic acid | **2.86** |

All four acids have the same –COOH group, and all four leave a carboxylate ion that shares its charge between two oxygen atoms. What differs is the chlorine atom.

- Chlorine (3.2) is more electronegative than the hydrogen (2.2) it replaces. It pulls electron density toward itself through the bonds of the chain. That spreads a little of the negative charge of the –COO⁻ group over more of the ion, and the ion is more stable. So each of the three acids with a chlorine atom is stronger than butanoic acid, and the highest p$K_a$, 4.82, belongs to butanoic acid.
- The pull is passed along from bond to bond, and it fades with distance. The chlorine atom on carbon 2 is nearest to the –COO⁻ group and does the most, so 2-chlorobutanoic acid has the lowest p$K_a$, 2.86. On carbon 3 it does less (4.05), and on carbon 4 least of all (4.52).

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the factor by which a chlorine atom raises the $K_a$ of butanoic acid when it is on carbon 2, and when it is on carbon 4.

<Answer>

A difference between two p$K_a$ values is a power of ten in $K_a$.

On carbon 2:

$$
4.82 - 2.86 = 1.96
$$

$$
10^{1.96} = \mathbf{91}
$$

On carbon 4:

$$
4.82 - 4.52 = 0.30
$$

$$
10^{0.30} = \mathbf{2.0}
$$

A chlorine atom next to the –COOH group makes the acid about 91 times as strong. Two carbon atoms farther along, it only doubles the $K_a$. (On carbon 3 the difference is 0.77, a factor of 5.9.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Two more acids:

- 2,2-dichlorobutanoic acid, CH₃CH₂CCl₂COOH, has two chlorine atoms on carbon 2.
- 5-chloropentanoic acid, ClCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂COOH, has one chlorine atom on carbon 5 of a chain that is one carbon atom longer. Pentanoic acid itself, with no chlorine, has a p$K_a$ within a few hundredths of butanoic acid's.

Predict a range for the p$K_a$ of each from the four values you matched in part a, and give your reasons. No exact value is expected.

<Answer>

**2,2-Dichlorobutanoic acid: below 2.86.** It has the chlorine atom of 2-chlorobutanoic acid in the same place, and a second one beside it. Two chlorine atoms pull more electron density from the –COO⁻ group than one does, so its conjugate base is more stable still and the acid is stronger than 2-chlorobutanoic acid.

**5-Chloropentanoic acid: between 4.52 and about 4.8.** Its chlorine atom still pulls, so the acid is a little stronger than the acid with no chlorine, whose p$K_a$ is close to 4.82. But the chlorine atom is one carbon atom farther from the –COOH group than in 4-chlorobutanoic acid, so it does less than the 0.30 units it does there, and the p$K_a$ is above 4.52.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for all four matches (butanoic acid 4.82, then 4.52, 4.05 and 2.86 for chlorine on carbon 4, 3 and 2); 1 point for the reason, which needs both halves: the chlorine atom pulls electron density and makes the conjugate base more stable, and the effect is smaller the farther the chlorine atom is from the –COOH group. The matches with no reason earn the first point only.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for both factors, about 91 on carbon 2 (90 to 93 is accepted) and about 2 on carbon 4, each from ten raised to a difference of two p$K_a$ values or from a quotient of two $K_a$ values. The differences 1.96 and 0.30 given as the factors earn 0 points. Factors that follow correctly from a wrong match in part a earn the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for both ranges, each with its reason: below 2.86, because two chlorine atoms on carbon 2 pull more than one; and above 4.52 but no higher than the p$K_a$ of the acid with no chlorine (any range that lies between 4.5 and 4.9 is accepted), because the chlorine atom is farther away than on carbon 4 and still pulls a little. One correct range with its reason, or two ranges with no reasons, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={3} title="Two rules on a diagonal" topic="8.6" points={4}>

Two rules compare acids in which the acidic hydrogen is bonded to different atoms:

- **Across a period,** the atoms are close in size, and the more electronegative atom gives the stronger acid.
- **Down a group,** the larger atom, with its longer and weaker bond to hydrogen, gives the stronger acid.

Here are six elements that form such acids. Of any two of these six atoms, the one in the later period is the larger.

| Element | Group | Period | Electronegativity |
|---|---|---|---|
| F | 17 | 2 | 4.0 |
| S | 16 | 3 | 2.6 |
| Cl | 17 | 3 | 3.2 |
| Se | 16 | 4 | 2.6 |
| Br | 17 | 4 | 3.0 |
| Te | 16 | 5 | 2.1 |

<Part label="a">

Sort these five pairs of acids into three kinds. For each pair, state whether (1) the two atoms share a period or a group, so that one rule applies; (2) they share neither, but size and electronegativity favor the same acid; or (3) they share neither, and size favors one acid while electronegativity favors the other. Name the stronger acid of each pair of kind 1 or kind 2.

- $\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{HBr}$
- $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{HCl}$
- $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{HBr}$
- $\ce{H2Se}$ and $\ce{HCl}$
- $\ce{H2Te}$ and $\ce{HF}$

<Answer>

**$\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{HBr}$: kind 1.** Chlorine and bromine are both in group 17, so the rule for a group applies. Bromine is the larger atom (period 4 against period 3), so **$\ce{HBr}$** is the stronger acid. Both ionize essentially completely in water, so the difference does not show in the pH of a solution.

**$\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{HCl}$: kind 1.** Sulfur and chlorine are both in period 3, so the rule for a period applies. Chlorine is the more electronegative (3.2 against 2.6), so **$\ce{HCl}$** is the stronger acid.

**$\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{HBr}$: kind 2.** Sulfur (group 16, period 3) and bromine (group 17, period 4) share neither a period nor a group. But bromine is the larger atom and also the more electronegative one (3.0 against 2.6). Both effects favor **$\ce{HBr}$**.

**$\ce{H2Se}$ and $\ce{HCl}$: kind 3.** Selenium (period 4) is the larger atom, which favors $\ce{H2Se}$. Chlorine is the more electronegative (3.2 against 2.6), which favors $\ce{HCl}$. The rules cannot decide.

**$\ce{H2Te}$ and $\ce{HF}$: kind 3.** Tellurium (period 5) is much the larger atom, which favors $\ce{H2Te}$. Fluorine is much the more electronegative (4.0 against 2.1), which favors $\ce{HF}$. The rules cannot decide.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Measurements settle the pairs of kind 3. Use these values, at 25 °C:

- $\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{HBr}$ are strong acids.
- The p$K_a$ values of the other four are 7.0 for $\ce{H2S}$, 3.9 for $\ce{H2Se}$, 3.17 for $\ce{HF}$ and 2.6 for $\ce{H2Te}$.

For each pair of kind 3, identify the stronger acid and state which effect, size or electronegativity, turned out to count for more. Then state what the two results together tell you about ranking two such acids whose atoms share neither a period nor a group.

<Answer>

**$\ce{H2Se}$ and $\ce{HCl}$.** $\ce{HCl}$ is a strong acid, and $\ce{H2Se}$, with a p$K_a$ of 3.9, is a weak one. **$\ce{HCl}$ is the stronger**, and it is the acid of the more electronegative atom. Here **electronegativity** counted for more than size.

**$\ce{H2Te}$ and $\ce{HF}$.** The lower p$K_a$ belongs to the stronger acid, and 2.6 is lower than 3.17. **$\ce{H2Te}$ is the stronger**, and it is the acid of the larger atom. Here **size** counted for more than electronegativity. The difference is

$$
3.17 - 2.6 = 0.57
$$

units of p$K_a$, so the $K_a$ of $\ce{H2Te}$ is about 4 times that of $\ce{HF}$:

$$
10^{0.57} = 3.7
$$

**Together.** In one pair the more electronegative atom gave the stronger acid, and in the other the larger atom did. So when size and electronegativity favor different acids, neither effect can be counted on to win, and the two rules cannot rank the pair. A measured value, such as a p$K_a$, has to decide. The rules are safe within one period, within one group, or where both effects favor the same acid.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the three pairs that can be decided, each with its basis: $\ce{HBr}$ over $\ce{HCl}$ (one group, larger atom), $\ce{HCl}$ over $\ce{H2S}$ (one period, more electronegative atom), and $\ce{HBr}$ over $\ce{H2S}$ (bromine is both larger and more electronegative); 1 point for the two pairs of kind 3, with what favors each acid: $\ce{H2Se}$ against $\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{H2Te}$ against $\ce{HF}$. Two of the first three correct with their bases, and nothing else, earn 0 points for the first point. A stronger acid named for a pair of kind 3 in this part does not lose the second point if the opposing effects are stated. For $\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{HBr}$, the rule for a group must be applied: "both are strong acids in water, and the rule makes $\ce{HBr}$ the stronger" is accepted, and "both are strong, so neither is stronger" is not.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for both results from the data: $\ce{HCl}$ is stronger than $\ce{H2Se}$, so electronegativity counted for more, and $\ce{H2Te}$ is stronger than $\ce{HF}$, so size counted for more (no factor is needed); 1 point for the conclusion that the winner differs from pair to pair, so the rules alone cannot rank such a pair and a measured value is needed. "Electronegativity is more important" or "size is more important" as a general conclusion earns 0 points for the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Counting oxygen atoms two ways" topic="8.6" points={5}>

Five oxyacids are written below so that every O–H group shows. In each, every oxygen atom is bonded to the central atom, and an oxygen atom outside an O–H group is bonded to the central atom alone. Use these values for the first proton each acid gives up, at 25 °C:

| Acid | Written as | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|---|
| telluric acid | Te(OH)₆ | 7.68 |
| periodic acid | (HO)₅IO | 3.29 |
| phosphoric acid | (HO)₃PO | 2.15 |
| iodic acid | (HO)IO₂ | 0.77 |
| sulfuric acid | (HO)₂SO₂ | strong acid |

<Part label="a">

For each acid, count the oxygen atoms in the molecule, and count the oxygen atoms that hold no hydrogen.

<Answer>

| Acid | Oxygen atoms | With no hydrogen |
|---|---|---|
| Te(OH)₆ | 6 | 0 |
| (HO)₅IO | 6 | 1 |
| (HO)₃PO | 4 | 1 |
| (HO)IO₂ | 3 | 2 |
| (HO)₂SO₂ | 4 | 2 |

Each O–H group has one oxygen atom that holds a hydrogen. The oxygen atoms written after the central atom hold none. For periodic acid, for example, there are 5 + 1 = 6 oxygen atoms, and 1 of them holds no hydrogen.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student says: "The number of oxygen atoms decides how strong an oxyacid is: the more oxygen atoms, the stronger the acid." Test the claim against the table.

<Answer>

The claim **fails**. Any one of these comparisons shows it:

- Telluric acid has the most oxygen atoms, six, and it is the **weakest** of the five (p$K_a$ 7.68).
- Telluric acid and periodic acid have six oxygen atoms each. If the count decided, equal counts would give equal strengths, but their p$K_a$ values are 7.68 and 3.29.
- Iodic acid has the fewest, three, and it is stronger than both acids with six (0.77 against 3.29 and 7.68).
- Phosphoric acid and sulfuric acid have four each. One is a weak acid and the other is a strong acid.

The two acids with six oxygen atoms differ by

$$
7.68 - 3.29 = 4.39
$$

units of p$K_a$, a factor in $K_a$ of

$$
10^{4.39} = 24{,}547
$$

or about 25,000.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Two other counts are put forward in place of the total:

- **Count X:** the oxygen atoms that hold no hydrogen. The more of them, the stronger the acid.
- **Count Y:** the O–H groups. The fewer of them, the stronger the acid.

The claim in part b said that the count decides the strength, so two acids with one count and different strengths counted against it. Counts X and Y claim less: each only ranks two acids whose counts differ, and says nothing about two acids that share a count. Test each count against every pair of the five acids. Identify the count that no pair contradicts, and name the pair of acids that contradicts the other count.

<Answer>

Count X is the second count of part a. Count Y is the total less count X:

| Acid | X | Y |
|---|---|---|
| Te(OH)₆ | 0 | 6 |
| (HO)₅IO | 1 | 5 |
| (HO)₃PO | 1 | 3 |
| (HO)IO₂ | 2 | 1 |
| (HO)₂SO₂ | 2 | 2 |

**Count Y is contradicted by iodic acid and sulfuric acid.** Iodic acid has one O–H group and sulfuric acid has two, so count Y makes iodic acid the stronger. But iodic acid is a weak acid (p$K_a$ 0.77), and sulfuric acid is a strong acid. Count Y gets the other nine pairs right, which is why it has to be tested on all ten.

**Count X, the count of oxygen atoms that hold no hydrogen,** is contradicted by no pair:

| With no hydrogen | Acids | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | telluric | 7.68 |
| 1 | periodic, phosphoric | 3.29, 2.15 |
| 2 | iodic, sulfuric | 0.77, strong |

Every acid with a higher count is stronger than every acid with a lower count. The count does not separate two acids that share it: periodic acid from phosphoric acid, or iodic acid from sulfuric acid.

An oxygen atom that keeps its hydrogen adds to the total count without adding an atom that can share the charge, which is why the total fails. It is also why count Y fails: sulfuric acid has one more O–H group than iodic acid, and it is not the weaker for it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Predict where two more acids fall among the five, from their structures: arsenic acid, (HO)₃AsO, and perchloric acid, (HO)ClO₃. Justify each prediction with a count.

<Answer>

**Arsenic acid, (HO)₃AsO:** one oxygen atom holds no hydrogen. That puts it with periodic acid and phosphoric acid: **a weak acid with a p$K_a$ of roughly 2 to 3.5**, stronger than telluric acid and weaker than iodic acid. (Its measured p$K_a$ is about 2.2.) Its total of four oxygen atoms would not tell you this, since sulfuric acid also has four.

**Perchloric acid, (HO)ClO₃:** three oxygen atoms hold no hydrogen, more than in any acid of the table. It should be at least as strong as the acids with two, so **a strong acid**. When its proton has left, four oxygen atoms share one negative charge. It is in fact one of the strong acids you are expected to know.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Part F: pH, pK_a and indicators

Four questions on what the pH of a solution does to a weak acid or a weak base dissolved in it. You will follow two groups on one molecule, find a p$K_a$ from the strength of a color, decide which indicator test is worth doing, and find the range of pH that keeps two acids in different forms.

<Question number={5} title="Two groups on one molecule" topic="8.7" points={5}>

Alanine is one of the amino acids that proteins are built from. In a strongly acidic solution its formula is H₃N⁺–CH(CH₃)–COOH: one carbon atom bonded to an –NH₃⁺ group, a –COOH group, a CH₃ group and a hydrogen atom. Two of its groups can give up a proton, and each has its own p$K_a$. The hydrogen atoms bonded to carbon stay on.

Take these values.

| Group | Acid form | Base form | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | –COO⁻ | 2.34 |
| Amine | –NH₃⁺ | –NH₂ | 9.69 |

A small amount of alanine is dissolved in each of three buffers, at pH 1.00, pH 6.00 and pH 11.00. The buffer sets the pH.

<Part label="a">

For each of the three buffers, identify the form of each group that has the higher concentration, and determine the net charge of an alanine particle that has those two forms.

<Answer>

Compare the pH with the p$K_a$ of each group, one group at a time. Below its p$K_a$ a group is mostly in its acid form. Above it, the group is mostly in its base form.

- **pH 1.00** is below 2.34 and below 9.69. Both groups are in the acid form: –COOH, with no charge, and –NH₃⁺. The net charge is $\mathbf{+1}$.

## Part G: Buffers and the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

Five questions on what a buffer holds, what it does with added acid and added base, and how its pH follows the ratio of its two members. Three of them use triethanolamine, a weak base, and each gives the one value it needs.

<Question number={9} title="Four bottles with no labels" topic="8.8" points={6}>

| Bottle | As found | After HCl | After NaOH |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 7.00 | 2.00 | 12.00 |

| R | 10.53 | 9.04 | 12.00 |
| S | 4.23 | 2.00 | 6.48 |

<Answer>

$$
\frac{1.0\ \text{mmol}}{100.0\ \text{mL}} = 0.010\ \text{M}
$$

With HCl that is $[\ce{H3O+}]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(0.010) \\
&= 2.00
\end{aligned}
$$

With NaOH it is $[\ce{OH-}]$, so the pOH is 2.00, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.00 \\
&= 12.00
\end{aligned}
$$

**Q is the solution of both.** It holds equal amounts of B and $\ce{HB+}$, and equal amounts of the two members of a pair give a pH equal to the p$K_a$ of the acid form, 7.76. It is also the only bottle whose pH moved by less than 0.1 with each addition.

**R is 0.20 M triethanolamine.** Its pH as found, 10.53, is the most basic of the four by far, and the most basic of the four liquids is the weak base with none of its conjugate acid beside it.

</Answer>

\end{aligned}

|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 1.0 | 20 | 20 |
| Change | −1.0 | −1.0 | +1.0 |
| After | 0 | 19 | 21 |

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{OH-(aq) + HB+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{B(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every value is in mmol.

| | OH⁻ | HB⁺ | B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 1.0 | 20 | 20 |

| After | 0 | 19 | 21 |

| Liquid | B | HB⁺ |
|---|---|---|
| With HCl | 19 | 21 |
| With NaOH | 21 | 19 |
| Beaker | 40 | 40 |

$$

$$

## Part H: Buffer capacity, and pH and solubility

Six questions on how much a buffer can take and on how the pH changes what dissolves. <Question number={14} title="Two lactate buffers and a limit" topic="8.10" points={6}>

A candy maker holds the sourness of a fruit candy steady with a lactate buffer: lactic acid, HC₃H₅O₃, with its conjugate base, the lactate ion, C₃H₅O₃⁻. Two buffers are on trial. Each holds 120 mmol of the two forms in all, in 250 mL of solution at 25 °C.

| Buffer | Lactic acid (mmol) | Lactate ion (mmol) |
|---|---|---|
| X | 60 | 60 |
| Y | 96 | 24 |

Use this value: the p$K_a$ of lactic acid is 3.86. Write HA for lactic acid and A⁻ for the lactate ion.

A buffer does not wait for its last mole before its pH moves sharply. In this question, take a buffer to have **reached its limit** when one of its forms has fallen to a tenth of the other.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the pH of each buffer.

<Answer>

Both forms are in the same solution, so the ratio of their concentrations is the ratio of their moles.

Buffer X holds equal amounts. The ratio is 1 and its logarithm is 0, so the pH is the p$K_a$: **3.86**.

Buffer Y has a ratio of base form to acid form of 24 ÷ 96 = 0.25.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 3.86 + \log(0.25) \\
&= 3.86 - 0.60 \\
&= \mathbf{3.26}
\end{aligned}
$$

Y holds more acid form than base form, so its pH is below the p$K_a$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For buffer X, calculate the amount of HCl, in mmol, that takes the buffer to its limit. Then calculate the amount of NaOH that does.

<Answer>

**HCl.** It is a strong acid, so its H₃O⁺ reacts to completion with the base form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C3H5O3-(aq)} \\
&{}+ \ce{H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\ce{HC3H5O3(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each millimole of HCl turns one millimole of A⁻ into HA. Call the amount of HCl added $n$. Here is the table of moles, with every value in mmol.

| | A⁻ | H₃O⁺ | HA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 60 | $n$ | 60 |
| Change | −$n$ | −$n$ | +$n$ |
| After | 60 − $n$ | 0 | 60 + $n$ |

A⁻ is the form that is running out. At the limit it is a tenth of HA:

$$
\begin{aligned}
60 - n &= 0.10(60 + n) \\
60 - n &= 6.0 + 0.10n \\
54 &= 1.10n \\
n &= 49.1
\end{aligned}
$$

The buffer takes **49 mmol** of HCl. Check: 10.9 mmol of A⁻ is then left beside 109.1 mmol of HA, a tenth.

**NaOH.** It is a strong base, so its OH⁻ reacts to completion with the acid form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HC3H5O3(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C3H5O3-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now HA is the form that is running out: after $n$ mmol of NaOH there is 60 − $n$ of HA and 60 + $n$ of A⁻. X starts with 60 mmol of each, so the equation is the one above with the two names changed over, and the buffer takes **49 mmol** of NaOH.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the same two amounts for buffer Y. Then state which buffer the candy maker should choose if only acid will get into it, if only base will, and if it could be either. Justify each choice with your amounts.

<Answer>

**HCl into Y.** A⁻ runs out, from 24 mmol, while HA grows from 96 mmol:

$$
\begin{aligned}
24 - n &= 0.10(96 + n) \\
24 - n &= 9.6 + 0.10n \\
14.4 &= 1.10n \\
n &= 13.1
\end{aligned}
$$

Y takes **13 mmol** of HCl.

**NaOH into Y.** HA runs out, from 96 mmol, while A⁻ grows from 24 mmol:

$$
\begin{aligned}
96 - n &= 0.10(24 + n) \\
96 - n &= 2.4 + 0.10n \\
93.6 &= 1.10n \\
n &= 85.1
\end{aligned}
$$

Y takes **85 mmol** of NaOH.

| To the limit | X | Y |
|---|---|---|
| HCl (mmol) | 49 | 13 |
| NaOH (mmol) | 49 | 85 |

- **Only acid: buffer X.** It takes 49 mmol of HCl, and Y takes 13 mmol. Added acid is met by the base form, and X holds 60 mmol of it against Y's 24 mmol.
- **Only base: buffer Y.** It takes 85 mmol of NaOH, and X takes 49 mmol. Added base is met by the acid form, and Y holds 96 mmol of it.
- **Either: buffer X.** A buffer that must guard both ways is only as good as its weaker side. X's weaker side is 49 mmol, and Y's is 13 mmol.

The two buffers hold the same 120 mmol in all. What differs is how that total is shared between the two forms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d" beyond>

A helper adds 10 mmol of HCl to buffer Y. Calculate the pH of the buffer afterward.

<Answer>

React first, in moles. The H₃O⁺ from the HCl reacts to completion with A⁻, by the first equation of part (b). Here is the table of moles, with every value in mmol.

| | A⁻ | H₃O⁺ | HA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 24 | 10 | 96 |
| Change | −10 | −10 | +10 |
| After | 14 | 0 | 106 |

The flask holds 14 mmol of A⁻ and 106 mmol of HA. Both forms are still there, so it is still a buffer, and the new ratio gives the pH. The ratio is 14 ÷ 106 = 0.132.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 3.86 + \log(0.132) \\
&= 3.86 - 0.88 \\
&= \mathbf{2.98}
\end{aligned}
$$

The pH has fallen from 3.26 to 2.98. That agrees with part (c): 10 mmol is less than the 13 mmol that takes Y to its limit, and 2.98 is still above 2.86, the pH one unit below the p$K_a$, where the ratio is 0.10.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={15} title="The record of a buffer giving out" topic="8.10" points={5}>

<Answer>

The buffer gave out **during the fifth hour**, between the readings at 4 h and 5 h.

|---|---|---|

<Part label="b">

Use the reading at 3 h to calculate the rate at which H₃O⁺ is released, in mmol per hour. Then state what the flask holds at 5 h and at 6 h, and show that the reading at 6 h agrees with your rate.

<Answer>

**The ratio at 3 h.** The flask was still a buffer then, so the reading gives the ratio of base form to acid form.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{[\ce{B}]}{[\ce{HB+}]} &= 7.47 - 8.07 \\
&= -0.60
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{[\ce{B}]}{[\ce{HB+}]} &= 10^{-0.60} \\
&= 0.25
\end{aligned}
$$

**The amount released by 3 h.** The H₃O⁺ reacts to completion with the base form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{B(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HB+(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each millimole of H₃O⁺ turns one millimole of B into HB⁺. After $n$ mmol, the flask holds 20.0 − $n$ of B and 20.0 + $n$ of HB⁺. Both are in the same liter, so the ratio of moles is the ratio of concentrations.

$$
\begin{aligned}
20.0 - n &= 0.25(20.0 + n) \\
20.0 - n &= 5.0 + 0.25n \\
15.0 &= 1.25n \\
n &= 12
\end{aligned}

| After | 0 | 4.0 | 40.0 |

\begin{aligned}

&= 0.0040\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(0.0040) \\
&= 2.40
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the reading at 6 h, so the rate of 4.0 mmol/h holds for the whole record.

</Answer>

</Part>

The temperature is the same in every beaker, so silver chromate has one $K_{sp}$, in the water and in the acid. Calculate that $K_{sp}$ from the beaker of water. Then calculate [CrO₄²⁻] in the beaker of acid, and decide how much of the chromate that dissolved there is still CrO₄²⁻: about half, about one part in 60, or a few parts in a million.

<Answer>

The solid is in equilibrium with its ions in both beakers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ag2CrO4(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{2Ag+(aq) + CrO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}]^2[\ce{CrO4^2-}]
$$

**In the water.** Each formula unit that dissolves gives two silver ions and one chromate ion, and in water the chromate stays as CrO₄²⁻. So [CrO₄²⁻] is half of [Ag⁺]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{CrO4^2-}] &= \frac{1.3 \times 10^{-4}}{2} \\
&= 6.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{sp} &= (1.3 \times 10^{-4})^2 \\
&\quad \times (6.5 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= \mathbf{1.1 \times 10^{-12}}
\end{aligned}
$$

**In the acid.** Solid is left, so this solution is saturated too, and the same product holds with [Ag⁺] = 0.0080 M:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{CrO4^2-}] &= \frac{K_{sp}}{[\ce{Ag+}]^2} \\
&= \frac{1.1 \times 10^{-12}}{(0.0080)^2} \\
&= \frac{1.1 \times 10^{-12}}{6.4 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.7 \times 10^{-8}}\ \textbf{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The decision.** The chromate that dissolved in the acid is half of the silver ion, whatever became of it:

$$
\frac{0.0080}{2} = 0.0040\ \text{M}
$$

Only $1.7 \times 10^{-8}$ M of that 0.0040 M is still CrO₄²⁻. That is about 4 parts in a million, so the answer is **a few parts in a million**. All the rest is HCrO₄⁻, made by the H₃O⁺ of the acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CrO4^2-(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HCrO4-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two columns of the table now fit together. The silver ion concentration is 62 times as high in the acid as in water, and the solution is still only saturated, because [CrO₄²⁻] is about 3,800 times lower there than the $6.5 \times 10^{-5}$ M of the beaker of water.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A classmate says: "Acid helps zinc carbonate dissolve because of the zinc ion. Zinc is a metal, and acids react with metals." Choose the two solids in the table that best test whether the metal ion or the anion decides, and use them to weigh the claim.

<Answer>

The two to choose are **silver chromate and silver thiocyanate**. They are the only two solids in the table that share a metal ion, so between them only the anion changes.

If the metal ion decided, the two silver solids would behave alike. They do not. Acid raises the silver ion concentration of silver chromate from $1.3 \times 10^{-4}$ M to 0.0080 M, and leaves that of silver thiocyanate at $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ M. The same metal ion gives two different results, so the metal ion is not what decides. The difference follows the anion: chromate is a base and thiocyanate is not.

So **the claim is not supported**. The table has no second zinc solid, so it cannot test zinc directly. But what it does show points away from the claim, and the anion explains zinc carbonate without any help from the metal ion: carbonate is a base, like chromate. A zinc ion in a solid is also not zinc metal, and it is not a base: it does not take a proton from H₃O⁺.

Zinc carbonate with silver chromate would be a weaker test. Those two differ in both ions at once, so they cannot show which ion matters.

</Answer>

<Question number={18} title="How acidic is acidic enough?" topic="8.7, 8.11" points={4}>

Three barium salts are each only slightly soluble in water. Use these values, at 25 °C, for the conjugate acid of each anion.

| Salt | Anion | Conjugate acid | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Ba(IO3)2}$ | IO₃⁻ | HIO₃ | 0.77 |
| $\ce{BaF2}$ | F⁻ | HF | 3.17 |
| $\ce{BaSO3}$ | SO₃²⁻ | HSO₃⁻ | 7.20 |

An excess of each salt is stirred into three buffers, at pH 9.00, pH 5.00 and pH 2.00. Each buffer holds its pH, and nothing else in the buffers reacts with the ions of the salts. Take each anion to gain one proton only, so the sulfite ion stops at HSO₃⁻ (the further step to H₂SO₃ and SO₂ at pH 2.00 is left out), and take the flasks to be sealed from the air.

Some of the anion that dissolves stays as the anion, and some is turned into its conjugate acid. More of a salt dissolves in one buffer than in another when that buffer leaves a smaller share of the dissolved anion as the anion itself. In this question, call the change **noticeable** when that share falls to less than nine-tenths of what it was in the other buffer.

<Part label="a">

For each salt, state whether its solubility rises noticeably from the buffer at pH 9.00 to the buffer at pH 5.00, and whether it rises noticeably from pH 5.00 to pH 2.00. Justify each answer by comparing the pH values with the p$K_a$.

<Answer>

| Salt | 9.00 to 5.00 | 5.00 to 2.00 |
|---|---|---|
| Barium iodate | no | no |
| Barium fluoride | no | **yes** |
| Barium sulfite | **yes** | **yes** |

Compare each pH with the p$K_a$. Above the p$K_a$ the base form, the anion, has the higher concentration. Below it the acid form has. Each pH unit away is a factor of ten.

**Barium iodate.** The three pH values are 8.23, 4.23 and 1.23 above the p$K_a$ of HIO₃. Even the lowest is more than one unit above, so in all three buffers there are more than ten iodate ions to each HIO₃ molecule. More than ten parts in eleven of the iodate stays as the anion in every buffer, so its share never falls below nine-tenths of what it was. No noticeable change either time.

**Barium fluoride.** pH 9.00 and pH 5.00 are 5.83 and 1.83 above the p$K_a$ of HF. Both are more than one unit above, so in both buffers nearly all the fluoride stays as F⁻: no noticeable change. pH 2.00 is 1.17 below the p$K_a$. Now there are more than ten HF molecules to each F⁻ ion. Most of the fluoride that dissolves is taken away, so the solubility rises.

**Barium sulfite.** pH 9.00 is 1.80 above the p$K_a$ of HSO₃⁻, so there nearly all the sulfite stays as SO₃²⁻. pH 5.00 is 2.20 below it: more than a hundred HSO₃⁻ ions to each SO₃²⁻ ion, so the solubility rises. pH 2.00 is 5.20 below. That is three more pH units, so the share left as SO₃²⁻ is a thousand times smaller again, and the solubility rises again.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For barium fluoride, calculate the ratio of F⁻ to HF in the buffer at pH 5.00 and in the buffer at pH 2.00. Use the two ratios, with $Q$ and $K_{sp}$, to explain your answers for barium fluoride in part (a).

<Answer>

The ratio of base form to acid form follows from the pH and the p$K_a$.

At pH 5.00:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{[\ce{F-}]}{[\ce{HF}]} &= 5.00 - 3.17 \\
&= 1.83
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{[\ce{F-}]}{[\ce{HF}]} &= 10^{1.83} \\
&= \mathbf{68}
\end{aligned}
$$

At pH 2.00:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{[\ce{F-}]}{[\ce{HF}]} &= 2.00 - 3.17 \\
&= -1.17
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{[\ce{F-}]}{[\ce{HF}]} &= 10^{-1.17} \\
&= \mathbf{0.068}
\end{aligned}
$$

The solid is in equilibrium with its ions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{BaF2(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Ba^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
Q = [\ce{Ba^2+}][\ce{F-}]^2
$$

**At pH 5.00** there are 68 F⁻ ions to each HF molecule. Of every 69 fluoride ions that dissolve, 68 stay as F⁻. Almost nothing is taken away, so $Q$ reaches $K_{sp}$ after about as much solid has dissolved as at pH 9.00.

**At pH 2.00** the ratio is 0.068, which is about 15 HF molecules to each F⁻ ion. Most of the F⁻ that dissolves is turned into HF. That holds [F⁻] down, so $Q$ stays below $K_{sp}$ until much more solid has dissolved. The solubility is higher, and $K_{sp}$ is the same as before.

</Answer>

A student writes: "An anion that is a weak base makes its salt more soluble whenever the solution is acidic." Correct the statement, and support your correction with one case from this question.

<Answer>

The statement is too wide. An acidic solution is one with a pH below 7, and that is not the pH that matters here.

**A corrected statement:** an anion that is a weak base makes its salt more soluble when the pH is low enough to turn a real share of the anion into its conjugate acid. That happens when the pH is near the p$K_a$ of the conjugate acid or below it, and not while the pH is more than about one unit above that p$K_a$.

**A case that supports it:** the buffer at pH 5.00 is acidic, and fluoride is a weak base, yet barium fluoride is essentially no more soluble there than at pH 9.00, because pH 5.00 is still 1.83 above the p$K_a$ of HF. Barium iodate is a second case: even pH 2.00 is 1.23 above the p$K_a$ of HIO₃, and its solubility hardly changes.

The comparison is with the p$K_a$, not with 7. The table shows it from the other side too. The p$K_a$ of HSO₃⁻ is 7.20, so for barium sulfite the rise has already begun in a solution that is not yet acidic: at pH 7.20, half of the dissolved sulfite is HSO₃⁻.

</Answer>

<Question number={19} title="A hydroxide, water and two basic buffers" topic="8.11" points={5}>

Manganese(II) hydroxide, Mn(OH)₂, is a pale solid that is very slightly soluble in water. Take its $K_{sp}$ as $2.0 \times 10^{-13}$ at 25 °C. Air slowly turns the solid brown, so every flask in this question is sealed.

<Part label="a">

An excess of the solid is stirred into pure water until no more dissolves. Calculate [OH⁻] in the saturated solution, and its pH.

<Answer>

The solid is in equilibrium with its ions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Mn(OH)2(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Mn^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $s$ be the concentration of Mn²⁺ at equilibrium, in M. Each formula unit that dissolves gives one Mn²⁺ ion and two OH⁻ ions, so [OH⁻] is $2s$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{sp} &= [\ce{Mn^2+}][\ce{OH-}]^2 \\
&= (s)(2s)^2 \\
&= 4s^3
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
s^3 &= \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-13}}{4} \\
&= 5.0 \times 10^{-14} \\
s &= 3.68 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $s$ is kept to three figures for the next step.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 2(3.68 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= 7.36 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

To two figures, [OH⁻] is $\mathbf{7.4 \times 10^{-5}}$ **M**. Water's own OH⁻, $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M, is tiny beside that. The solid is a base, so take the pOH first.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(7.36 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= 4.13
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 4.13 \\
&= \mathbf{9.87}
\end{aligned}
$$

Pure water with this solid lying in it is a basic solution.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

An excess of the solid is also stirred into a buffer at pH 9.00 and into a buffer at pH 11.00. Each buffer holds its pH, and nothing in the buffers reacts with Mn²⁺. State in which buffer more of the solid dissolves than in pure water, and in which less. Explain with $Q$ and $K_{sp}$.

<Answer>

**More dissolves in the buffer at pH 9.00. Less dissolves in the buffer at pH 11.00.**

Each buffer fixes [OH⁻]. At pH 9.00 the pOH is 5.00, and at pH 11.00 it is 3.00:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 10^{-5.00} \\
&= 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 10^{-3.00} \\
&= 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The saturated solution in water has [OH⁻] = $7.4 \times 10^{-5}$ M, from part (a). The buffer at pH 9.00 holds [OH⁻] below that, and the buffer at pH 11.00 holds it above.

Now test each buffer with $Q$. Suppose as much solid had dissolved as dissolves in water, so that [Mn²⁺] were $3.7 \times 10^{-5}$ M.

In the buffer at pH 9.00:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= [\ce{Mn^2+}][\ce{OH-}]^2 \\
&= (3.7 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&\quad \times (1.0 \times 10^{-5})^2 \\
&= 3.7 \times 10^{-15}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is below $K_{sp}$, $2.0 \times 10^{-13}$. The solution is not yet saturated, so more solid dissolves than in water.

In the buffer at pH 11.00:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= (3.7 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&\quad \times (1.0 \times 10^{-3})^2 \\
&= 3.7 \times 10^{-11}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is above $K_{sp}$. The solution cannot hold that much Mn²⁺ beside so much OH⁻, so less solid dissolves than in water. Here OH⁻ acts as a common ion.

$K_{sp}$ is the same in the water and in both buffers.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student says: "A hydroxide is always less soluble in a basic solution than in water." Use your answers to parts (a) and (b) to weigh the claim.

<Answer>

**The claim is false as it stands.** The buffer at pH 9.00 is a basic solution, and more manganese(II) hydroxide dissolves in it than in pure water.

The claim compares the buffer with pH 7, the pH of water before any solid goes in. That is the wrong comparison. Water with the solid in it does not stay at pH 7: its saturated solution is at pH 9.87. A buffer held at pH 9.00 is basic, but it is less basic than that, so it holds less OH⁻ than the solid's own saturated solution does.

**What is true:** this hydroxide is less soluble than in water in any solution whose pH is held above 9.87, such as the buffer at pH 11.00, and more soluble in any solution whose pH is held below 9.87. The line is the pH of the solid's own saturated solution, not 7.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A student says: "No buffer is needed. A solution of NaOH with [OH⁻] = $1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ M is at pH 9.00 too, so it will stay at pH 9.00 while the solid dissolves in it." Use the amounts from parts (a) and (b) to decide whether it would. Then name what the buffer has that this solution lacks, and write the equation for what it does, with HA for that species.

<Answer>

**It would not stay at pH 9.00.** Suppose it did, so that [OH⁻] stayed at $1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ M. Part (b) showed that at this [OH⁻], $Q$ is still below $K_{sp}$ when [Mn²⁺] has reached $3.7 \times 10^{-5}$ M, so the solid would still be dissolving. \frac{7.4 \times 10^{-5}}{1.0 \times 10^{-5}} = 7.4
$$

That is more than seven times the OH⁻ the solution began with, and nothing in a solution of NaOH removes it. Na⁺ does not react with OH⁻, and the H₃O⁺ in the solution is only $1.0 \times 10^{-9}$ M. $$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\

\end{aligned}
$$

Each OH⁻ ion that the solid gives is used up by an HA molecule. So in the buffer [OH⁻] stays near $1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ M, $Q$ stays below $K_{sp}$, and the solid goes on dissolving until [Mn²⁺] is high enough to bring $Q$ up to $K_{sp}$.

</Answer>
