Work each question on paper before you open its answer.

You will need these relationships. The first defines p$K_a$, and a lower p$K_a$ means a stronger acid:

$$
\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a
$$

The second holds for a conjugate pair:

$$
K_b = \frac{K_w}{K_a}
$$

Use these values:

| Acid or base | Constant |
|---|---|
| $\ce{HF}$ | $K_a = 7.2 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| acetic acid, $\ce{HC2H3O2}$ (also written $\ce{CH3COOH}$) | $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| $\ce{HCN}$ | $K_a = 6.2 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | $K_b = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |

Every solution here is at 25 °C unless a question says otherwise, so $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$.

For a salt solution, take the salt apart into its two ions. For each ion, ask whether it is the conjugate of something weak. If it is, it reacts with water. The anion of a strong acid and the cation of a strong base do not.

This site writes $\ce{H3O+}$. The exam's equation sheet writes $\ce{H+}$ for the same ion, and either is accepted.

<Question number={2} title="Structure and acid strength" topic="8.6">

Structure and acid strength.

<Part label="a">

Rank $\ce{HF}$, $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$, $\ce{HI}$ by increasing strength and give the controlling factor.

<Answer>

$$
\ce{HF} < \ce{HCl} < \ce{HBr} < \ce{HI}
$$

The H–X bond weakens down the group, so the proton is released more readily; bond strength outweighs electronegativity.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why $\ce{HF}$ is weakest despite fluorine being the most electronegative element.

<Answer>

The H–F bond is by far the strongest of the four, and holding the proton tightly matters more than pulling electron density away from it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Rank $\ce{HClO}$, $\ce{HClO2}$, $\ce{HClO3}$, $\ce{HClO4}$ and give both reasons.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HClO} < \ce{HClO2} < \\
&\ce{HClO3} < \ce{HClO4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Strength increases with the number of oxygen atoms. Each additional electronegative oxygen atom pulls electron density away from the O–H bond, which makes the bond more polar and the proton easier to remove, and spreads the negative charge of the conjugate base, stabilizing it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Rank $\ce{HOI}$, $\ce{HOBr}$, $\ce{HOCl}$.

<Answer>

$$
\ce{HOI} < \ce{HOBr} < \ce{HOCl}
$$

$\ce{HOCl}$ is the $\ce{HClO}$ of part c, written to show that its hydrogen is bonded to oxygen.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Sodium chloride and sodium cyanide" topic="8.4">

Explain, in conjugate-pair terms, why $\ce{NaCl}$ solutions are neutral but $\ce{NaCN}$ solutions are basic.

<Answer>

Both hold $\ce{Na+}$, the cation of the strong base $\ce{NaOH}$, and $\ce{Na+}$ does not react with water. $\ce{Cl-}$ is the conjugate base of the strong acid $\ce{HCl}$, so it is so weak a base that it essentially never takes a proton from water. $\ce{CN-}$ is the conjugate base of the very weak acid $\ce{HCN}$, so it takes protons from some of the water molecules and makes $\ce{OH-}$.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="The pH of a sodium acetate solution" topic="8.4">

Calculate the pH of 0.20 M $\ce{NaC2H3O2}$.

<Part label="a">

Write the equation for the reaction of the acetate ion with water.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C2H3O2-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{HC2H3O2(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate $K_b$.

<Answer>

$$
K_b = 5.6 \times 10^{-10}
$$

That is $5.556 \times 10^{-10}$ before rounding; carry it into part c.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the pH.

<Answer>

Leave $x$ out beside 0.20. For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \\
&\quad \sqrt{(5.556 \times 10^{-10})(0.20)} \\
&= 1.054 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M} \\
&= [\ce{OH-}]
\end{aligned}
$$

The check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{1.054 \times 10^{-5}}{0.20} \times 100\% \\
&\quad = 0.0053\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(1.054 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= 4.98
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 4.98 \\
&= \mathbf{9.02}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="The pH of a sodium fluoride solution" topic="8.4">

Calculate the pH of 0.30 M $\ce{NaF}$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_b &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{7.2 \times 10^{-4}} \\
&= 1.4 \times 10^{-11}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is $1.389 \times 10^{-11}$ before rounding; carry it. Leave $x$ out beside 0.30. For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \\
&\quad \sqrt{(1.389 \times 10^{-11})(0.30)} \\
&= 2.04 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{2.04 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30} \times 100\% \\
&\quad = 0.00068\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(2.04 \times 10^{-6}) \\
&= 5.69
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 5.69 \\
&= \mathbf{8.31}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="The common-ion effect" topic="8.4">

The common-ion effect. For 1.0 M $\ce{HF}$ alone, $[\ce{H3O+}] = 2.7 \times 10^{-2}$ M and the acid is 2.7% ionized.

<Part label="a">

Calculate $[\ce{H3O+}]$ in a solution 1.0 M in $\ce{HF}$ and 1.0 M in $\ce{NaF}$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}] &= \frac{K_a[\ce{HF}]}{[\ce{F-}]} \\
&= (7.2 \times 10^{-4})\left(\frac{1.0}{1.0}\right) \\
&= 7.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pH} = \mathbf{3.14}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Percent ionization now: <Blank />

<Answer>

0.072%. This is also the check for part a, where $x$ was left out beside 1.0 M: under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Use Le Châtelier's principle to explain why less of the acid ionizes.

<Answer>

$\ce{F-}$ is a product of the $\ce{HF}$ ionization equilibrium. Adding it shifts the equilibrium to the left, so less $\ce{HF}$ ionizes and $[\ce{H3O+}]$ falls, here by a factor of about 37.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="Buffer or not?" topic="8.8">

Buffer or not? Give a one-phrase reason.

<Part label="a">

$\ce{HC2H3O2}$ and $\ce{NaC2H3O2}$

<Answer>

A buffer: a weak acid and its conjugate base.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{NaCl}$

<Answer>

Not a buffer: $\ce{HCl}$ is strong, and $\ce{Cl-}$ is a spectator.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{NH4NO3}$

<Answer>

A buffer: a weak base and its conjugate acid.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

$\ce{NaOH}$ and $\ce{NaC2H3O2}$

<Answer>

Not a buffer: no weak conjugate pair is present.

</Answer>

</Part>
