## Part A: Water, pH, and strong acids and bases

Five questions on water's own equilibrium and on solutions of strong acids and strong bases. You will find a $K_w$ from a meter reading, test a log sheet for entries that cannot both be right, mix and dilute solutions to reach a wanted pH, and rank three bases in two ways.

<Question number={1} title="Pure water in a cold room" topic="8.1" points={5}>

A purifying unit stands in a cold room and delivers very pure water. A pH meter dipped into the water as it leaves the unit reads 7.08. The water is at the temperature of the room, and it holds nothing but water.

Use these values of $K_w$.

| Temperature | $K_w$ |
|---|---|
| 10 °C | $2.9 \times 10^{-15}$ |
| 20 °C | $6.8 \times 10^{-15}$ |
| 25 °C | $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ |
| 30 °C | $1.5 \times 10^{-14}$ |
| 40 °C | $2.9 \times 10^{-14}$ |

<Part label="a">

Calculate $[\ce{H3O+}]$ in the water, and calculate $K_w$ at the temperature of the room.

<Answer>

Undo the logarithm.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}] &= 10^{-7.08} \\
&= \mathbf{8.3 \times 10^{-8}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The water is pure. Every H₃O⁺ ion in it was made together with one OH⁻ ion, when one water molecule passed a proton to another. So $[\ce{OH-}]$ is $8.3 \times 10^{-8}$ M too.

$K_w$ is the product of the two concentrations. Keep the unrounded $8.32 \times 10^{-8}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_w &= [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] \\
&= (8.32 \times 10^{-8})^2 \\
&= \mathbf{6.9 \times 10^{-15}}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify the temperature in the table that the room is nearest. Then state whether the water is acidic, basic or neutral, and justify that with the two concentrations.

<Answer>

The room is nearest **20 °C**. The value found, $6.9 \times 10^{-15}$, lies between the rows for 10 °C and 25 °C, and it is almost the 20 °C row's $6.8 \times 10^{-15}$. The small gap comes from the meter, which reads to two decimal places: neutral water with a $K_w$ of $6.8 \times 10^{-15}$ has a pH of 7.08 too.

The water is **neutral**. Its $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and its $[\ce{OH-}]$ are equal, $8.3 \times 10^{-8}$ M each, and equal concentrations are what neutral means.

The reading of 7.08 does not make the water basic. A neutral solution has a pH of 7.00 only where $K_w$ is $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$, at 25 °C. In this room, 7.08 is the pH of neutral water.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A trace of nitric acid gets into a sample of this water. The temperature does not change, and $[\ce{H3O+}]$ rises to $3.0 \times 10^{-6}$ M. Using your $K_w$ from part a, calculate $[\ce{OH-}]$, the pH and the pOH of the sample. Then add the pH and the pOH, and explain why the sum is not 14.00.

<Answer>

The product of the two concentrations is still $K_w$ for this temperature. Keep the unrounded $6.92 \times 10^{-15}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= \frac{K_w}{[\ce{H3O+}]} \\
&= \frac{6.92 \times 10^{-15}}{3.0 \times 10^{-6}} \\
&= \mathbf{2.3 \times 10^{-9}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(3.0 \times 10^{-6}) \\
&= \mathbf{5.52}
\end{aligned}
$$

The pOH comes from $[\ce{OH-}]$, with the unrounded $2.31 \times 10^{-9}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(2.31 \times 10^{-9}) \\
&= \mathbf{8.64}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sum:

$$
5.52 + 8.64 = \mathbf{14.16}
$$

The pH and the pOH of a solution add to $\text{p}K_w$, the negative logarithm of $K_w$. That is 14.00 only where $K_w$ is $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$, at 25 °C. Here $K_w$ is smaller, so $\text{p}K_w$ is larger:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{p}K_w &= -\log(6.92 \times 10^{-15}) \\
&= 14.16
\end{aligned}
$$

The meter said the same thing before any acid was there. In the pure water the pH and the pOH were both 7.08, and twice 7.08 is 14.16.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for $[\ce{H3O+}]$ of $8.3 \times 10^{-8}$ M; 1 point for $K_w$ of $6.9 \times 10^{-15}$, found by squaring that concentration because the two ions are equal in pure water (or as 10 raised to the power −14.16, because the pOH equals the pH). A value copied from the table with no calculation does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for 20 °C together with neutral, justified by the equal concentrations of the two ions (or by the ions of pure water being made in pairs). "Neutral because the pH is close to 7" and "basic because the pH is above 7" earn 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for $[\ce{OH-}]$ of $2.3 \times 10^{-9}$ M, from the $K_w$ of part a divided by $3.0 \times 10^{-6}$ M (the 20 °C row's value gives the same two figures and earns the point; using $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ gives $3.3 \times 10^{-9}$ M and does not); 1 point for the pH, 5.52, the pOH, 8.64, and their sum, 14.16, each within 0.02, with the reason that the sum is $\text{p}K_w$ for this temperature and is 14.00 only at 25 °C. A pOH found as 14.00 minus the pH (8.48) does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="A log sheet that does not add up" topic="8.1" points={5}>

A technician at a plating shop tests the rinse water from four tanks. The log sheet says that every sample was at 25 °C. For each sample the sheet has two entries. (The entries are made up for this question.)

- Sample 1: a pH of 8.30, and $[\ce{OH-}]$ of $2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ M
- Sample 2: $[\ce{H3O+}]$ of $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ M, and a pOH of 9.60
- Sample 3: a pH of 6.80, and a pOH of 6.80
- Sample 4: $[\ce{H3O+}]$ of $2.5 \times 10^{-9}$ M, and $[\ce{OH-}]$ of $4.0 \times 10^{-6}$ M

Use these values: $K_w$ is $2.9 \times 10^{-15}$ at 10 °C, $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at 25 °C, $1.5 \times 10^{-14}$ at 30 °C and $2.9 \times 10^{-14}$ at 40 °C.

<Part label="a">

For each sample, test whether its two entries agree with each other at 25 °C. Identify the sample whose entries do not.

<Answer>

At 25 °C the two concentrations multiply to $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$, and the pH and the pOH add to 14.00. Either test will do. Use whichever is one step away from the entries.

Sample 1. The hydroxide entry gives a pOH:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(2.0 \times 10^{-6}) \\
&= 5.70
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
8.30 + 5.70 = 14.00
$$

Sample 2. The hydronium entry gives a pH:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(4.0 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= 4.40
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
4.40 + 9.60 = 14.00
$$

Sample 3. Both entries are ready to add:

$$
6.80 + 6.80 = 13.60
$$

Sample 4. Both entries are ready to multiply:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(2.5 \times 10^{-9})(4.0 \times 10^{-6}) \\
&= 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
\end{aligned}
$$

Samples 1, 2 and 4 agree with $K_w$ at 25 °C. **Sample 3 does not:** its pH and pOH add to 13.60, not 14.00.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Rank the four samples from the highest $[\ce{OH-}]$ to the lowest. Show the value for each, taken from the sample's hydroxide entry or its pOH entry.

<Answer>

Samples 1 and 4 have $[\ce{OH-}]$ on the sheet. Samples 2 and 3 have a pOH, and undoing the logarithm gives the concentration.

Sample 2:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 10^{-9.60} \\
&= 2.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Sample 3:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 10^{-6.80} \\
&= 1.6 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

| Sample | $[\ce{OH-}]$ in M |
|---|---|
| 4 | $4.0 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| 1 | $2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| 3 | $1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ |
| 2 | $2.5 \times 10^{-10}$ |

From highest to lowest: **sample 4, sample 1, sample 3, sample 2**.

None of these four values needed $K_w$. Each came from a hydroxide entry or from a pOH, so the ranking stands whatever the temperature of sample 3 was.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The technician tests sample 3 again and finds that both of its entries are right. So something else on the sheet is wrong for that sample. Calculate $K_w$ for sample 3. State whether the sample was warmer or colder than 25 °C, and whether it was acidic, basic or neutral. Justify each.

<Answer>

The pH and the pOH of any water solution add to $\text{p}K_w$. For sample 3 that is 13.60, so:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_w &= 10^{-13.60} \\
&= \mathbf{2.5 \times 10^{-14}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same value comes from the concentrations. A pH of 6.80 and a pOH of 6.80 each mean $1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ M, and the square of the unrounded $1.58 \times 10^{-7}$ is $2.5 \times 10^{-14}$.

The sample was **warmer than 25 °C**. Its $K_w$ is larger than $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$, and the values given show $K_w$ rising with temperature. It lies between the values for 30 °C and 40 °C, so the sample was between those two temperatures.

The sample was **neutral**. Its pH equals its pOH, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals $[\ce{OH-}]$. A pH of 6.80 would be acidic at 25 °C. In this warm sample it is the pH of neutral water.

So the wrong entry was the temperature. The two readings were right all along.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for a sound test shown for the samples that agree: the pH and the pOH adding to 14.00, or the two concentrations multiplying to $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ (all three of samples 1, 2 and 4 tested; either route may be used for each); 1 point for naming sample 3 with its sum, 13.60, or with its product, $2.5 \times 10^{-14}$. Naming sample 3 with its sum and testing no other sample earns the second point only.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for the order 4, 1, 3, 2 with the hydroxide concentrations of samples 3 and 2 shown ($1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ M and $2.5 \times 10^{-10}$ M). Ranking by pOH from lowest to highest, with the four pOH values shown (5.40, 5.70, 6.80, 9.60), also earns the point. The order with no values earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for $K_w$ of $2.5 \times 10^{-14}$, from the sum 13.60 or from the square of $1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ M (the rounded concentration gives $2.6 \times 10^{-14}$, which is accepted); 1 point for both warmer, because this $K_w$ is larger than the value at 25 °C, and neutral, because the pH equals the pOH or the two concentrations are equal. "Acidic because the pH is below 7" does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Two etching baths, mixed" topic="8.2" points={4}>

An etcher keeps two baths of nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$, a strong acid. Bath 1 has a pH of 1.00. Bath 2 has a pH of 3.00. Both are at 25 °C. When the two are mixed, take the volumes to add.

<Part label="a">

A helper mixes 50.0 mL of bath 1 with 50.0 mL of bath 2 and expects a pH of 2.00, halfway between the two. Calculate the pH of the mixture, and state what the helper averaged that cannot be averaged.

<Answer>

Nitric acid is strong, so each bath's $[\ce{H3O+}]$ comes straight from its pH: 0.10 M in bath 1 and 0.0010 M in bath 2.

Count the H₃O⁺ that each bath brings. A molarity times a volume in mL is an amount in mmol.

$$
0.10 \times 50.0 = 5.0\ \text{mmol}
$$

$$
0.0010 \times 50.0 = 0.050\ \text{mmol}
$$

Together that is 5.05 mmol, in a total volume of 100.0 mL:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}] &= \frac{5.05}{100.0} \\
&= 0.0505\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(0.0505) \\
&= \mathbf{1.30}
\end{aligned}
$$

The helper **averaged the two pH values**. A pH is an exponent, and exponents are not what mixes. Amounts of H₃O⁺ are. Mixing equal volumes averages the two concentrations, and 0.0505 M is the average of 0.10 M and 0.0010 M.

Bath 1 holds 100 times as much H₃O⁺ in each milliliter as bath 2. So the mixture is very nearly bath 1 diluted to twice its volume, and its pH is only 0.30 above bath 1's.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Determine the volume of bath 2 that must be mixed with 10.0 mL of bath 1 to give a pH of 2.00. Check your answer by calculating the pH of that mixture.

<Answer>

A pH of 2.00 means $[\ce{H3O+}]$ of 0.010 M. Let $V$ be the volume of bath 2, in mL.

The H₃O⁺ from the two baths, in mmol, is $1.0 + 0.0010V$: bath 1 brings 0.10 M times 10.0 mL, and bath 2 brings 0.0010 M times $V$. The total volume is $10.0 + V$ mL. The amount is the wanted concentration times that volume:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1.0 + 0.0010V \\
&= 0.010(10.0 + V) \\
&= 0.10 + 0.010V
\end{aligned}
$$

Collect the terms in $V$ on one side:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.90 &= 0.0090V \\
V &= \mathbf{100}\ \text{mL}
\end{aligned}
$$

It takes ten volumes of bath 2 for each volume of bath 1, not one for one.

Check. Bath 1 brings 1.0 mmol, and bath 2 brings 0.0010 M times 100 mL, which is 0.10 mmol. The total volume is 110 mL.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}] &= \frac{1.10}{110} \\
&= 0.0100\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(0.0100) \\
&= 2.00
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for a pH of 1.30 (1.29 to 1.31), from the total amount of H₃O⁺ over the total volume or from the average of the two concentrations; 1 point for saying that the pH values were averaged where the concentrations (or the amounts of H₃O⁺) should have been. Dividing the total amount by 50.0 mL does not earn the first point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for a setup that puts the H₃O⁺ from both baths over the total volume and sets it to 0.010 M; 1 point for 100 mL with a check that returns a pH of 2.00. A volume found by trial and shown by the check to give a pH of 2.00 earns both points. Leaving out the H₃O⁺ of bath 2 gives 90 mL: that earns the first point only if the total volume is used and the result is set to 0.010 M, and it does not earn the second.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Three strong bases, by mass and by molarity" topic="8.2" points={5}>

A technician makes three solutions at 25 °C. Each holds 1.00 g of one strong base, dissolved in water and made up to 1.00 L. All of each solid dissolves.

Use these molar masses.

| Base | Molar mass (g/mol) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{NaOH}$ | 40.00 |
| $\ce{KOH}$ | 56.11 |
| $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ | 171.35 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the pH of each of the three solutions, and rank them from the highest pH to the lowest.

<Answer>

Each solution has a volume of 1.00 L, so the molarity of each base is its number of moles in 1.00 g.

**Sodium hydroxide.**

$$
\frac{1.00}{40.00} = 0.0250\ \text{M}
$$

Each $\ce{NaOH}$ gives one OH⁻, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ is 0.0250 M.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(0.0250) \\
&= 1.60
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 1.60 \\
&= \mathbf{12.40}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Potassium hydroxide.**

$$
\frac{1.00}{56.11} = 0.0178\ \text{M}
$$

Each $\ce{KOH}$ gives one OH⁻, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ is 0.0178 M.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(0.0178) \\
&= 1.75
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 1.75 \\
&= \mathbf{12.25}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Barium hydroxide.**

$$
\frac{1.00}{171.35} = 0.00584\ \text{M}
$$

Each formula unit gives two OH⁻:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ba(OH)2(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Ba^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 2 \times 0.00584 \\
&= 0.0117\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(0.0117) \\
&= 1.93
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 1.93 \\
&= \mathbf{12.07}
\end{aligned}
$$

From highest pH to lowest: **sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, barium hydroxide**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The technician then makes three more solutions at 25 °C, each 0.0200 M in one of the same three bases. Calculate the pH of each, and rank them.

<Answer>

Sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide each give one OH⁻, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ is 0.0200 M in both.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(0.0200) \\
&= 1.70
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 1.70 \\
&= \mathbf{12.30}
\end{aligned}
$$

Barium hydroxide gives two:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 2 \times 0.0200 \\
&= 0.0400\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(0.0400) \\
&= 1.40
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 1.40 \\
&= \mathbf{12.60}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Barium hydroxide has the highest pH, 12.60. The other two are equal, at 12.30.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Barium hydroxide is last in part a and first in part b. Explain both ranks, using the formulas and the molar masses.

<Answer>

What sets the pH is how many moles of OH⁻ are in each liter. The two parts count out the bases in different ways.

**By mass (part a).** A gram of barium hydroxide holds the fewest moles of OH⁻ of the three. Its formula unit carries two OH⁻, but its molar mass is more than four times that of sodium hydroxide and more than three times that of potassium hydroxide, so a gram holds far fewer formula units. Per mole of OH⁻, it takes 40.00 g of $\ce{NaOH}$, 56.11 g of $\ce{KOH}$ and about 85.7 g of $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, which is half of 171.35 g. The second OH⁻ does not make up for the mass of the barium. So 1.00 g gives $[\ce{OH-}]$ of only 0.0117 M, against 0.0250 M and 0.0178 M.

**By molarity (part b).** Now each liter holds the same number of formula units, 0.0200 mol, and the molar masses play no part. Each formula unit of barium hydroxide gives two OH⁻ where the others give one. Its $[\ce{OH-}]$ is twice theirs, and its pH is higher by 0.30, the logarithm of 2.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the three hydroxide concentrations, 0.0250 M, 0.0178 M and 0.0117 M, with the factor of two for barium hydroxide and for no other; 1 point for the three pH values, 12.40, 12.25 and 12.07 (each within 0.02), in that order. Leaving out the factor of two gives 11.77 for barium hydroxide and loses the first point only. A pOH reported as a pH loses the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for 12.30, 12.30 and 12.60 (each within 0.02), with barium hydroxide highest and the other two equal.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for the rank by mass: a gram of barium hydroxide supplies the fewest moles of OH⁻, because its molar mass is more than twice either of the others', so that two OH⁻ in the formula do not make up for it (a comparison of grams per mole of OH⁻, or of moles of OH⁻ per gram, earns the point); 1 point for the rank by molarity: equal numbers of formula units in each liter, and two OH⁻ from each formula unit of barium hydroxide. "It has two OH" with nothing about equal numbers of formula units earns 0 points for the second point, and it never earns the first.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Water to reach a wanted pH" topic="8.2" points={4}>

Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid, and barium hydroxide, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, is a strong base. Every solution in this question is at 25 °C. When water is added to a solution, take the volumes to add.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the volume of water that must be added to 25.0 mL of hydrochloric acid with a pH of 1.60 to bring its pH to 2.30.

<Answer>

Find $[\ce{H3O+}]$ before and after.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}]_{\text{before}} &= 10^{-1.60} \\
&= 0.025\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}]_{\text{after}} &= 10^{-2.30} \\
&= 0.0050\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The concentration must fall to one-fifth:

$$
\frac{0.025}{0.0050} = 5.0
$$

Adding water does not change the amount of H₃O⁺ from the acid. So the same amount has to be spread through 5.0 times the volume.

$$
5.0 \times 25.0 = 125\ \text{mL}
$$

That is the final volume. The acid already fills 25.0 mL of it:

$$
125 - 25.0 = \mathbf{100}\ \text{mL}
$$

A shorter route to the factor: the pH rises by 0.70, and 10 raised to the power 0.70 is 5.0.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A solution of barium hydroxide has a pH of 12.90. Calculate the molarity of the barium hydroxide. Then calculate the volume of water that must be added to 20.0 mL of this solution to bring its pH to 12.20.

<Answer>

**The molarity.** For a base, go through the pOH.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= 14.00 - 12.90 \\
&= 1.10
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 10^{-1.10} \\
&= 0.0794\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.079 M to two figures. The third figure is carried into the next steps. Each $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ gave two of those OH⁻ ions, so there is half as much barium hydroxide:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{Ba(OH)2}] &= \frac{0.0794}{2} \\
&= \mathbf{0.040}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The water.** At a pH of 12.20:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= 14.00 - 12.20 \\
&= 1.80
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 10^{-1.80} \\
&= 0.0158\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.016 M to two figures. Divide the carried values:

$$
\frac{0.0794}{0.0158} = 5.0
$$

$[\ce{OH-}]$ must fall to one-fifth, so the volume must become 5.0 times as large.

$$
5.0 \times 20.0 = 100\ \text{mL}
$$

$$
100 - 20.0 = \mathbf{80}\ \text{mL}
$$

The factor of two is needed for the molarity and not for the water. Dilution divides $[\ce{OH-}]$ and the concentration of the barium hydroxide by the same 5.0.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student plans to keep adding water to the hydrochloric acid of part a until its pH is 7.50. Calculate the $[\ce{H3O+}]$ that a pH of 7.50 means, and use it to explain why no volume of water brings the acid to that pH.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}] &= 10^{-7.50} \\
&= 3.2 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is less H₃O⁺ than the water being added holds. Pure water at 25 °C has $[\ce{H3O+}]$ of $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M, about three times as much.

Water brings H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ in equal amounts, and the acid's H₃O⁺ comes on top of that. So however much water is added, the mixture holds more H₃O⁺ than OH⁻. Its $[\ce{H3O+}]$ stays above $1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M, and its pH stays below 7.00.

**No volume of water works.** Adding water takes the pH from 1.60 up toward 7.00 and never past it. A pH of 7.50 at 25 °C belongs to a basic solution, with more OH⁻ than H₃O⁺, and nothing in the plan adds a base.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Part B: Weak acids and weak bases

Five questions on acids and bases that ionize only in part. You will work from a pH back to a molarity or a constant, put a weak acid into a solution that already holds hydronium ions, name a base from one reading, and follow two unlabeled acids through a dilution.

<Question number={6} title="Two solutions with one pH" topic="8.3" points={6}>

Acrylic acid, $\ce{HC3H3O2}$, is a weak acid with one acidic proton. It is a liquid that mixes with water, and it is the starting material of many plastics and paints. Use this value, for 25 °C:

$$
K_a = 5.6 \times 10^{-5}
$$

A technician has two solutions at 25 °C. One is acrylic acid in water. The other is nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$, a strong acid, in water. A pH meter reads 2.80 in each of them.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the molarity of the nitric acid solution.

<Answer>

Nitric acid is a strong acid, so essentially every molecule has given its proton to water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HNO3(aq) + H2O(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Its molarity is therefore its hydronium concentration, and the pH gives that.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{H3O+}] &= 10^{-2.80} \\
&= 1.585 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

A pH with two decimal places gives two figures, so the nitric acid is $\mathbf{1.6 \times 10^{-3}}$ **M**. Carry $1.585 \times 10^{-3}$ M into the steps below.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the molarity of the acrylic acid solution, with no approximation, and the percent ionization of the acrylic acid in it.

<Answer>

Write HA for acrylic acid and A⁻ for its conjugate base, the acrylate ion, $\ce{C3H3O2-}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The meter reads the same pH, so this solution has the same $[\ce{H3O+}]$, $1.585 \times 10^{-3}$ M. Each molecule that ionizes makes one $\ce{H3O+}$ and one $\ce{A-}$, so $[\ce{A-}]$ is $1.585 \times 10^{-3}$ M as well. Call both $x$. The $K_a$ expression then has one unknown, the concentration of the acid that is still whole:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{HA}] &= \frac{x^2}{K_a} \\
&= \frac{(1.585 \times 10^{-3})^2}{5.6 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= 0.04486\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The molarity of the solution counts all of the acid that was dissolved, whole and ionized together:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.04486 + 0.001585 \\
&= 0.0464\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

To two figures the acrylic acid is $\mathbf{0.046}$ **M**. Nothing was left out, because $x$ was measured, so there is no approximation to check.

The percent ionization is the hydronium concentration over the molarity of the acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{1.585 \times 10^{-3}}{0.0464} \times 100\% \\
&\quad = \mathbf{3.4\%}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A 25.0 mL sample of each solution is titrated with 0.0500 M sodium hydroxide, $\ce{NaOH}$. Calculate the volume of the $\ce{NaOH}$ solution that brings each sample to its equivalence point.

<Answer>

Sodium hydroxide is a strong base, so each reaction goes to completion, 1 to 1. In the nitric acid sample the acid is all $\ce{H3O+}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the acrylic acid sample the hydroxide ion takes the proton straight off the acid's molecules, the whole ones as well as the ionized ones, until none is left (topic 8.4). The overall reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{A-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Nitric acid.** Millimoles are milliliters times molarity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(25.0)(1.585 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= 0.0396\ \text{mmol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The equivalence point needs 0.0396 mmol of $\ce{OH-}$, and each milliliter of the base brings 0.0500 mmol.

$$
\frac{0.0396}{0.0500} = \mathbf{0.79}\ \text{mL}
$$

**Acrylic acid.** Use the carried molarity, 0.0464 M.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(25.0)(0.0464) \\
&= 1.16\ \text{mmol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{1.16}{0.0500} = \mathbf{23.2}\ \text{mL}
$$

The rounded 0.046 M gives 23 mL, which is the same answer to two figures.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A student says: "The two solutions have the same pH, so equal volumes of them take the same amount of base." Use your results to decide whether the student is right.

<Answer>

The student is **not right**. The acrylic acid sample takes 23.2 mL of the base and the nitric acid sample takes 0.79 mL:

$$
\frac{23.2}{0.79} = 29
$$

That is about 29 times as much base for the same volume at the same pH.

The two measurements count different things. A pH counts only the $\ce{H3O+}$ ions in the solution at that moment, and the two solutions have the same number of those in each liter. A strong base reacts with all of the acid, whole molecules included. In the nitric acid every molecule is ionized, so the pH has counted all of the acid. In the acrylic acid only 3.4 percent of the molecules are ionized, so the pH has counted 3.4 molecules in every 100, and the base reacts with all 100. The ratio of all the acid to the ionized part is the ratio of the two volumes:

$$
\frac{0.0464}{1.585 \times 10^{-3}} = 29
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={11} title="A wanted mixture, and two flasks that overshoot it" topic="8.4" points={6}>

<Part label="a">

Calculate the ratio the mixture must have: the moles of benzoate ion, $\ce{C7H5O2-}$, divided by the moles of benzoic acid.

<Answer>

Write HA for benzoic acid and A⁻ for the benzoate ion. A mixture that holds both obeys the acid's $K_a$ expression:

$$
K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

Solve it for the ratio that is asked for:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} &= \frac{K_a}{[\ce{H3O+}]} \\
&= \frac{6.3 \times 10^{-5}}{2.1 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= \mathbf{3.0}
\end{aligned}
$$

HA and A⁻ share one flask, so the volume cancels and the ratio of the moles is also 3.0. The mixture needs **three benzoate ions for every benzoic acid molecule**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

<Question number={13} title="Four salt solutions, four readings" topic="8.4" points={5}>

- potassium bromide, $\ce{KBr}$
- sodium benzoate, $\ce{NaC7H5O2}$
- triethylammonium chloride, $\ce{(C2H5)3NHCl}$

| Bottle | pH |
|---|---|
| W | 8.75 |
| X | 3.84 |
| Y | 7.00 |
| Z | 5.72 |

Some facts about the acids and bases the ions come from:

- HCl and HBr are strong acids. NaOH and KOH are strong bases.

Identify the salt in bottle W and the salt in bottle Y. For each, name the ion that sets the pH, or explain why no ion does.

<Answer>

Split each salt into its ions and ask whether each ion is the conjugate of something weak.

**Bottle Y is potassium bromide.** K⁺ is the cation of the strong base KOH, and Br⁻ is the anion of the strong acid HBr. Neither gives a proton to water or takes one from it, so the solution is neutral, and 7.00 is the neutral reading at 25 °C.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C7H5O2-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{HC7H5O2(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Sodium benzoate is the only one of the four salts with an ion that takes protons from water, and 8.75 is the only reading above 7.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify the salt in bottle X and the salt in bottle Z. Justify the order of their two readings.

<Answer>

**Bottle X (3.84) is imidazolium chloride, and bottle Z (5.72) is triethylammonium chloride.**

Both salts hold Cl⁻, which does nothing, and a cation that is the conjugate acid of a weak base. Each cation gives protons to water, so both solutions are acidic. For the triethylammonium ion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{(C2H5)3NH+(aq)} \\
&{}+ \ce{H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\ce{(C2H5)3N(aq) + H3O+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

What separates them is the strength of the two cations as acids. The $K_a$ of a conjugate acid times the $K_b$ of its base is $K_w$, so the stronger base has the weaker conjugate acid. Triethylamine is by far the stronger base, so the triethylammonium ion is by far the weaker acid. At the same 0.20 M it makes less H₃O⁺, and its solution has the higher pH of the two: 5.72. </Answer>

<Part label="c">

Use the reading of the imidazolium chloride solution to calculate $K_a$ of the imidazolium ion, $\ce{C3H4N2H+}$. Then calculate $K_b$ of imidazole.

<Answer>

The ion's equilibrium with water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C3H4N2H+(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{C3H4N2(aq) + H3O+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reading gives $x$, which is $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and also $[\ce{C3H4N2}]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 10^{-3.84} \\
&= 1.445 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

To two figures that is $1.4 \times 10^{-4}$ M. The extra figures are carried into the next step. The imidazolium ion left is 0.20 − 0.00014, which is still 0.20 M. Nothing has to be left out or checked, because $x$ was measured.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &= \frac{x^2}{0.20} \\
&= \frac{(1.445 \times 10^{-4})^2}{0.20} \\
&= 1.04 \times 10^{-7}
\end{aligned}
$$

To two figures, $K_a$ of the imidazolium ion is $\mathbf{1.0 \times 10^{-7}}$.

Imidazole and the imidazolium ion are a conjugate acid–base pair, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_b &= \frac{K_w}{K_a} \\

## Part D: Titrations

<Question number={20} title="A curve that falls twice" topic="8.5" points={5}>

Sodium carbonate, $\ce{Na2CO3}$, is a soluble salt, and the carbonate ion, $\ce{CO3^2-}$, is a base that can take two protons, one after the other:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CO3^2-(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HCO3-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HCO3-(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{H2CO3(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

25.0 mL of 0.0800 M sodium carbonate is titrated with 0.100 M HCl. The flask is closed, so no carbon dioxide escapes, and all of the dissolved carbon dioxide is counted as $\ce{H2CO3}$. A pH probe gives these readings.

| HCl added (mL) | pH |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | 11.61 |
| 10.0 | 10.32 |
| 20.0 | 8.34 |
| 30.0 | 6.35 |
| 40.0 | 3.93 |
| 45.0 | 2.15 |

Use these values for carbonic acid: p$K_{a1}$ = 6.35, for $\ce{H2CO3}$ giving up a proton, and p$K_{a2}$ = 10.33, for $\ce{HCO3-}$ giving up a proton.

<Part label="a">

State how many steep falls the full curve has. Calculate the volume of HCl at each, and explain why the second volume is twice the first.

<Answer>

**Two steep falls, at 20.0 mL and at 40.0 mL.** Each fall marks one proton taken by every carbonate ion, and a carbonate ion takes two.

The flask holds

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(25.0)(0.0800) \\
&= 2.00\ \text{mmol of } \ce{CO3^2-}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step needs 2.00 mmol of H₃O⁺:

$$
\frac{2.00}{0.100} = \mathbf{20.0}\ \text{mL}
$$

The first step turns the 2.00 mmol of $\ce{CO3^2-}$ into 2.00 mmol of $\ce{HCO3-}$. The second step is also 1 to 1, so it needs another 2.00 mmol of H₃O⁺, which is another 20.0 mL. The second fall is at

$$
20.0 + 20.0 = \mathbf{40.0}\ \text{mL}
$$

The second volume is twice the first because each step uses the same number of moles of H₃O⁺: one for every carbonate ion that was in the flask at the start.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For each of the six readings, identify the carbon-containing form or forms that the flask mainly holds: $\ce{CO3^2-}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$ or $\ce{H2CO3}$. Where two forms are present in equal amounts, say so.

<Answer>

Count the H₃O⁺ added by each reading (the middle column), and spend it on the first step until 2.00 mmol is used, then on the second.

| HCl (mL) | H₃O⁺ (mmol) | Mainly holds |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0 | CO₃²⁻ |
| 10.0 | 1.00 | CO₃²⁻, HCO₃⁻ (equal) |
| 20.0 | 2.00 | HCO₃⁻ |
| 30.0 | 3.00 | HCO₃⁻, H₂CO₃ (equal) |
| 40.0 | 4.00 | H₂CO₃ |
| 45.0 | 4.50 | H₂CO₃ |

- At 10.0 mL, 1.00 mmol of H₃O⁺ has turned 1.00 mmol of $\ce{CO3^2-}$ into $\ce{HCO3-}$, and 1.00 mmol of $\ce{CO3^2-}$ is left. That is halfway to the first fall.
- At 20.0 mL, the first fall, every carbonate ion has taken one proton: 2.00 mmol of $\ce{HCO3-}$.
- At 30.0 mL, the third millimole of H₃O⁺ has turned 1.00 mmol of $\ce{HCO3-}$ into $\ce{H2CO3}$, and 1.00 mmol of $\ce{HCO3-}$ is left. That is halfway between the falls.
- At 40.0 mL, the second fall, every ion has taken its second proton: 2.00 mmol of $\ce{H2CO3}$.
- At 45.0 mL the carbon is still all $\ce{H2CO3}$. The extra 0.50 mmol of H₃O⁺ has nothing left to react with.

So the forms come in the order $\ce{CO3^2-}$, then $\ce{HCO3-}$, then $\ce{H2CO3}$: one more proton at each fall.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Match each p$K_a$ to the reading that shows it. Then explain why the larger p$K_a$ is met first in this titration.

<Answer>

A pH equals a p$K_a$ where the acid and the base of that pair are equal in amount.

- **p$K_{a2}$ = 10.33 goes with the reading at 10.0 mL.** There $\ce{HCO3-}$ and $\ce{CO3^2-}$ are equal in amount, and that pair's acid is $\ce{HCO3-}$. The reading, 10.32, is within a hundredth of 10.33.
- **p$K_{a1}$ = 6.35 goes with the reading at 30.0 mL.** There $\ce{H2CO3}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ are equal in amount, and that pair's acid is $\ce{H2CO3}$. The reading is 6.35.

The larger p$K_a$ comes first because this titration starts from the base end. The flask starts with $\ce{CO3^2-}$, the form with no acidic proton left. The first acid that the HCl makes from it is $\ce{HCO3-}$, the weaker of the two acids, with the larger p$K_a$. So the first pair in the flask is $\ce{HCO3-}$ with $\ce{CO3^2-}$, and its p$K_a$ is p$K_{a2}$. The pair with $\ce{H2CO3}$ in it cannot appear until the first fall is past.

The readings agree: the pH falls all the way through an acid's addition, so the higher of the two halfway readings has to come first. If carbonic acid were titrated with a strong base, the curve would rise, and p$K_{a1}$ would be met first.

(The reading at 10.0 mL is a hundredth below p$K_{a2}$ because the carbonate ion is a strong enough base to take a few protons from water, which the count of moles leaves out.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

The reading at 40.0 mL is below 7. Use the moles to decide whether HCl is left over at 40.0 mL, and explain the reading. Then say what sets the pH at 45.0 mL.

<Answer>

**No HCl is left over at 40.0 mL.** The acid added is (40.0)(0.100) = 4.00 mmol of H₃O⁺. The two steps use 2.00 mmol each, 4.00 mmol in all, so every H₃O⁺ from the buret has been used.

What the flask holds there is 2.00 mmol of $\ce{H2CO3}$, with the spectator ions Na⁺ and Cl⁻. $\ce{H2CO3}$ is a weak acid, and a small part of it ionizes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H2CO3(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{HCO3-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

That H₃O⁺ puts the pH below 7, at 3.93. It is the mirror of a weak acid's equivalence point above 7: the flask is acidic because of what the base has become, not because of anything left over.

**At 45.0 mL, leftover H₃O⁺ sets the pH.** The acid added is (45.0)(0.100) = 4.50 mmol, and only 4.00 mmol can react, so 0.50 mmol of H₃O⁺ is left over in 25.0 + 45.0 = 70.0 mL. Leftover strong acid in a known volume is topic 8.2's case, and it gives the reading, 2.15. The strong acid alone accounts for it, and the little that $\ce{H2CO3}$ adds does not show.

</Answer>
