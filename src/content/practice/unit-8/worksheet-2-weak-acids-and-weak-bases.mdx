Work each question on paper before you open its answer.

A weak acid or a weak base ionizes only partly, so its pH comes from an ICE table and its ionization constant. For an acid, $x$ is $[\ce{H3O+}]$. For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$, so find the pOH first and then change it to a pH.

Whenever you leave $x$ out, do the check and write its percentage in your answer.

This site writes H₃O⁺. The exam's equation sheet writes H⁺ for the same ion, and either is accepted.

Use these values.

| Acid or base | Constant |
|---|---|
| $\ce{HF}$ | $K_a = 7.2 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| $\ce{HC2H3O2}$ | $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| $\ce{HOCl}$ | $K_a = 3.5 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| $\ce{HCN}$ | $K_a = 6.2 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | $K_b = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |

The second acid in the table is acetic acid, $\ce{HC2H3O2}$ (also written $\ce{CH3COOH}$).

Every solution here is at 25 °C unless a question says otherwise, so

$$
K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
$$

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Setting up the table" topic="8.3">

Set up the ICE table for 0.250 M $\ce{HOCl}$ and write $K_a$ in terms of $x$.

<Answer>

The acid gives a proton to water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HOCl(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + OCl-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every value is in M.

| | HOCl | H₃O⁺ | OCl⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.250 | 0 | 0 |
| C | −x | +x | +x |
| E | 0.250 − x | x | x |

The last row gives $[\ce{HOCl}] = 0.250 - x$ and $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{OCl-}] = x$, so

$$
K_a = \frac{x^2}{0.250 - x}
$$

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Three weak acids" topic="8.3">

Calculate the pH of each weak acid solution, and show the check.

<Part label="a">

0.100 M $\ce{HC2H3O2}$

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.100)} \\
&= 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pH} = \mathbf{2.87}
$$

The check:

$$
\frac{1.34 \times 10^{-3}}{0.100} \times 100\% = 1.3\%
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

0.500 M $\ce{HOCl}$

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(3.5 \times 10^{-8})(0.500)} \\
&= 1.32 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pH} = \mathbf{3.88}
$$

The check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{1.32 \times 10^{-4}}{0.500} \times 100\% \\
&\quad = 0.026\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

0.0500 M $\ce{HCN}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(6.2 \times 10^{-10})(0.0500)} \\
&= \sqrt{3.1 \times 10^{-11}} \\
&= 5.57 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pH} = \mathbf{5.25}
$$

The check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{5.57 \times 10^{-6}}{0.0500} \times 100\% \\
&\quad = 0.011\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Strong is not concentrated" topic="8.2, 8.3">

0.10 M $\ce{HCl}$ has pH 1.00; 0.10 M acetic acid has pH 2.87. Explain the difference and state what it shows about the words "strong" and "concentrated."

<Answer>

$\ce{HCl}$ ionizes completely, so $[\ce{H3O+}] = 0.10$ M. Acetic acid reaches equilibrium with only about 1.3% ionized, giving a far smaller $[\ce{H3O+}]$. The two solutions are equally *concentrated* but differ enormously in *strength*. Strength is how far the ionization goes, which $K_a$ measures; at one concentration, the stronger acid is the more ionized.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Percent ionization" topic="8.3">

Find each percent ionization, then explain the pattern.

<Part label="a">

For 0.100 M acetic acid: <Blank />

<Answer>

1.3%. This is the check of question 2, part a: for a weak acid alone in water, the check and the percent ionization are the same division.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For 0.0100 M acetic acid,

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = 4.24 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
$$

Percent ionization? <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\frac{4.24 \times 10^{-4}}{0.0100} \times 100\% = 4.24\%
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The dilute solution has the higher percent ionization but the higher pH. Explain.

<Answer>

With $C_0$ for the starting concentration, $x \approx \sqrt{K_a C_0}$. On dilution $x$ falls only as the square root of $C_0$, more slowly than $C_0$ itself, so the *fraction* ionized, $x/C_0$, rises. The absolute $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is nonetheless smaller, so the pH is higher.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="An ionization constant from a pH" topic="8.3">

A 0.20 M solution of a weak acid has pH 3.10.

<Part label="a">

$[\ce{H3O+}] =$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = 7.9 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
$$

($7.94 \times 10^{-4}$ M before rounding; carry it)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate $K_a$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &\approx \frac{(7.94 \times 10^{-4})^2}{0.20} \\
&= \mathbf{3.2 \times 10^{-6}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The $x$ is left out of $0.20 - x$, so do the check:

$$
\frac{7.94 \times 10^{-4}}{0.20} \times 100\% = 0.40\%
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

p$K_a =$ <Blank />

<Answer>

Carry $K_a$ with one more figure, $3.15 \times 10^{-6}$, from part b.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{p}K_a &= -\log(3.15 \times 10^{-6}) \\
&= \mathbf{5.50}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="Ammonia at three concentrations" topic="8.3">

Calculate the pH of each weak base solution:

<Part label="a">

0.150 M $\ce{NH3}$

<Answer>

For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.150)} \\
&= 1.643 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M} \\
&= [\ce{OH-}]
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(1.643 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= 2.78
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.78 \\
&= \mathbf{11.22}
\end{aligned}
$$

The check:

$$
\frac{1.643 \times 10^{-3}}{0.150} \times 100\% = 1.1\%
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

0.400 M $\ce{NH3}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.400)} \\
&= \sqrt{7.2 \times 10^{-6}} \\
&= 2.68 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M} \\
&= [\ce{OH-}]
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(2.68 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= 2.57
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.57 \\
&= \mathbf{11.43}
\end{aligned}
$$

The check:

$$
\frac{2.68 \times 10^{-3}}{0.400} \times 100\% = 0.67\%
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

0.100 M $\ce{NH3}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.100)} \\
&= \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-6}} \\
&= 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M} \\
&= [\ce{OH-}]
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(1.34 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= 2.87
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.87 \\
&= \mathbf{11.13}
\end{aligned}
$$

The check:

$$
\frac{1.34 \times 10^{-3}}{0.100} \times 100\% = 1.3\%
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="A pOH reported as a pH" topic="8.3">

A student calculates $x = 1.643 \times 10^{-3}$ M for 0.150 M $\ce{NH3}$ and reports pH = 2.78. Identify the error and give the correct answer.

<Answer>

For a base, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$, not $[\ce{H3O+}]$. Taking $-\log x$ gives the pOH, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.78 \\
&= 11.22
\end{aligned}
$$

They reported a basic solution as an acidic one; a quick check of the direction would have caught it.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="Acids with more than one proton" topic="8.3">

Phosphoric acid, $\ce{H3PO4}$, can give up three protons, one at a time. Its three ionization constants are

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{a1} &= 7.5 \times 10^{-3} \\
K_{a2} &= 6.2 \times 10^{-8} \\
K_{a3} &= 4.8 \times 10^{-13}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Why is each successive constant so much smaller?

<Answer>

Each proton must be pulled away from an increasingly negative anion, and separating positive from negative charge becomes progressively harder.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b" beyond>

Justify calculating the pH from $K_{a1}$ alone.

<Answer>

$K_{a2}$ is about $10^5$ times smaller, so the second ionization contributes a negligible amount of $\ce{H3O+}$ beside the first.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c" beyond>

For which common acid with more than one proton does the reasoning of part b fail, and why?

<Answer>

$\ce{H2SO4}$: its first ionization is strong and its second ($K_{a2} = 1.2 \times 10^{-2}$) is large enough to contribute measurably.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
