There are twelve ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions on the same skill, with new numbers and no help. Work alone, and work all four before you check any of them.

Unit 8 is worth 11 to 15 percent of the AP exam, second only to Unit 3.

**The distinction that costs the most points.** **Strength** is how far an acid ionizes: a strong acid ionizes essentially completely. **Concentration** is how much was dissolved. The two are independent: a dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are both entirely ordinary things.

**The habit that decides this unit.** Before calculating any pH, ask *strong or weak?* A strong acid's $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is its concentration. A weak one needs an equilibrium calculation with $K_a$. Treating a weak acid as strong is the most common error here.

Every solution here is at 25 °C unless a question says otherwise, so

$$
K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
$$

This site writes H₃O⁺. The exam's equation sheet writes H⁺ for the same ion, and either is accepted.

Acetic acid is written $\ce{CH3COOH}$ here (the worksheets write $\ce{HC2H3O2}$).

**One scope note.** The concentrations along the titration curve of an acid with more than one proton, and a solubility calculated from a pH, are not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for them.

## Ladder 1: Brønsted–Lowry pairs

An acid donates a proton; a base accepts one. A conjugate pair differs by **exactly one** proton, as topic 4.8 taught.

<Worked title="Finding both pairs">

Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HCO3-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H2CO3(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Follow the protons.** $\ce{HCO3-}$ *gains* one to become $\ce{H2CO3}$: it acted as a **base**, and $\ce{H2CO3}$ is its conjugate acid. That is pair one.

$\ce{H2O}$ *loses* one to become $\ce{OH-}$: it acted as an **acid**, and $\ce{OH-}$ is its conjugate base. That is pair two.

**Pairs: $\ce{H2CO3}/\ce{HCO3-}$ and $\ce{H2O}/\ce{OH-}$.**

**The trap:** pairing $\ce{HCO3-}$ with $\ce{OH-}$. They are on opposite sides and look like a pair, but they are not the same species with and without a proton, so they are not a pair. Always check the one-proton rule.

A species such as $\ce{HCO3-}$ can take a proton or give one up (the word for this is **amphiprotic**): here it is a base, but if a strong base is added, the added $\ce{OH-}$ takes a proton from it instead.

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Strong versus weak

Strong means essentially complete ionization: write a single arrow. Weak means partial: write a double arrow, and expect an equilibrium calculation.

<Worked title="What is in the beaker">

Compare 0.10 M $\ce{HCl}$ with 0.10 M $\ce{CH3COOH}$ in terms of the particles present and the conductivity.

**$\ce{HCl}$, strong:** essentially every molecule ionizes.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HCl(aq) + H2O(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The solution contains 0.10 M $\ce{H3O+}$, 0.10 M $\ce{Cl-}$ and almost no intact $\ce{HCl}$. It conducts strongly.

**$\ce{CH3COOH}$, weak:** only about 1.3% ionizes.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The solution is mostly *intact acetic acid molecules*, with roughly 0.0013 M of each ion. It conducts weakly.

**Same concentration, about 75 times the $[\ce{H3O+}]$, and nearly two pH units between them.** Strength, not concentration, made that difference.

**On a particle drawing,** the weak acid must show mostly whole molecules. Drawing it fully ionized is the standard mistake.

</Worked>

## Ladder 3: pH, pOH and $K_w$

$\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$, $\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]$, and at 25 °C they sum to 14.00.

<Worked title="All four quantities from one">

A solution has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}$. Find pH, pOH and $[\ce{OH-}]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(2.5 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= \mathbf{4.60}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pOH} = 14.00 - 4.60 = \mathbf{9.40}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{2.5 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= \mathbf{4.0 \times 10^{-10}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**A quick check of the direction:** pH below 7 means acidic, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ should exceed $[\ce{OH-}]$. It does, by a factor of more than 60,000. ✓

**Significant figures in a logarithm:** only the digits *after* the decimal point count. $[\ce{H3O+}]$ has two significant figures, so the pH is quoted to two decimal places: 4.60, not 4.6 or 4.602.

</Worked>

## Ladder 4: pH of strong acids and bases

For a strong acid or base, the concentration *is* the ion concentration, but watch for a formula supplying more than one ion.

<Worked title="Watch the subscript">

Find the pH of 0.0050 M $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$, assuming that it comes apart into its ions completely.

**Count the hydroxides.** Each formula unit gives **two**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{OH-}] &= 2(0.0050) \\
&= 0.010\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pOH} = -\log(0.010) = 2.00
$$

$$
\text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.00 = \mathbf{12.00}
$$

**Forgetting the factor of 2** gives pOH 2.30 and pH 11.70, close enough to look plausible, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

**A quick check of the direction that catches sign errors:** barium hydroxide is a strong base, so the pH must be well above 7. Any answer below 7 is immediately wrong, and usually means pH and pOH were interchanged.

</Worked>

## Ladder 5: pH of a weak acid

Set up the $K_a$ equilibrium, assume $x$ is small, solve, and then **check** the assumption.

<Worked title="The full routine">

Find the pH of 0.200 M acetic acid, $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$.

**Equilibrium:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

with $x = [\ce{H3O+}]$ formed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &= \frac{x^2}{0.200 - x} \\
&= 1.8 \times 10^{-5}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Assume $x \ll 0.200$:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.200)} \\
&= 1.90 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

($1.897 \times 10^{-3}$ M before rounding; carry it.)

**Check it:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{1.90 \times 10^{-3}}{0.200} \times 100\% \\
&= 0.95\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Well under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(1.897 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= \mathbf{2.72}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Compare with a strong acid** at the same concentration: pH 0.70. The weak acid sits about two units higher because so little of it ionized.

</Worked>

## Ladder 6: Percent ionization

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{percent ionization} \\
&= \frac{[\ce{H3O+}]_\text{eq}}{[\text{acid}]_\text{initial}} \times 100\%
\end{aligned}
$$

It *rises* on dilution, even though the pH rises too.

<Worked title="The result that looks backward">

For the 0.200 M acetic acid of Ladder 5's example, where $x = 1.90 \times 10^{-3}$ M, find the percent ionization and predict what happens on dilution.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{1.90 \times 10^{-3}}{0.200} \times 100\% \\
&= \mathbf{0.95\%}
\end{aligned}
$$

**On dilution the percent ionization increases.** $K_a$ is fixed, and $x \approx \sqrt{K_a C}$, so $x$ falls only as the *square root* of the concentration, while the concentration itself falls linearly. The *ratio* therefore grows.

**Concretely:** at 0.0200 M the ionization is about 3.0%, about three times the fraction, even though $[\ce{H3O+}]$ itself has fallen and the pH has risen.

**Two things moving opposite ways.** Diluting makes the solution *less acidic* (higher pH) but the acid *more ionized* (higher percentage). Both are true, and confusing them is a common error.

</Worked>

## Ladder 7: p$K_a$ and acid strength

$\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a$. A *smaller* p$K_a$ means a *stronger* acid: the minus sign reverses the order.

<Worked title="Converting and comparing">

Acid A has $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$; acid B has p$K_a$ = 3.14. Which is stronger?

**Put them on the same scale.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{p}K_a(\text{A}) &= -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= \mathbf{4.74}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Compare:** B's 3.14 is *smaller*, so **B is the stronger acid**.

**Check by converting back:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a(\text{B}) &= 10^{-3.14} \\
&= 7.2 \times 10^{-4}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is indeed larger than $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, about forty times. ✓

**Why the reversal:** the minus sign in the definition. Every factor of ten *up* in $K_a$ is one unit *down* in p$K_a$. It works exactly as pH does: lower pH means more acidic.

</Worked>

## Ladder 8: Structure and acid strength

Two separate trends, governed by two different factors, and one of them runs opposite to what electronegativity alone would suggest.

<Worked title="The two trends">

Explain why acid strength increases along

$$
\ce{HF} < \ce{HCl} < \ce{HBr} < \ce{HI}
$$

and along

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HClO} < \ce{HClO2} \\
&< \ce{HClO3} < \ce{HClO4}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Down the halogens: bond strength decides.** The halogen gets larger, so the $\ce{H-X}$ bond is longer and weaker and the proton is released more easily. Electronegativity *falls* down this group, so it would predict the opposite order: it is not the controlling factor here.

**Across the oxyacids: electron withdrawal decides.** Each added oxygen atom is highly electronegative and pulls electron density away from the $\ce{O-H}$ bond, which makes the bond more polar and the proton easier to remove. The extra oxygen atoms also spread the negative charge of the resulting anion over more atoms, so the anion accommodates it better and the ionization proceeds further.

**The point:** identify which factor is varying before reaching for a rule. Electronegativity, bond strength and the spreading of the anion's charge each dominate in different comparisons.

</Worked>

<Part label="a">

Stronger acid, $\ce{HBr}$ or $\ce{HCl}$? Why?

<Answer>

$\ce{HBr}$: weaker bond.

**$\ce{HBr}$.** Bromine is larger, so the $\ce{H-Br}$ bond is longer and weaker and the proton leaves more readily.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Stronger acid, $\ce{HClO4}$ or $\ce{HClO}$? Why?

<Answer>

$\ce{HClO4}$: more electron withdrawal.

**$\ce{HClO4}$.** Three additional oxygen atoms withdraw far more electron density from the $\ce{O-H}$ bond and spread the anion's charge over four oxygen atoms rather than one.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Answer>

**$\ce{HClO3}$** is expected to be the stronger. With the same number of oxygen atoms, the more electronegative central atom withdraws more density; chlorine is more electronegative than bromine.

</Answer>

<Answer>

Because fluorine is the *most* electronegative halogen, so an electronegativity argument predicts $\ce{HF}$ to be the strongest, yet it is by far the weakest of the four. Bond strength, not polarity, is what decides down that group.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Ladder 9: Acid–base properties of salts

For each ion, ask whether it is the conjugate of something weak. If it is, it reacts with water. The anion of a strong acid and the cation of a strong base do not.

<Worked title="Three salts, three verdicts">

Predict whether solutions of $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{NH4Cl}$ and $\ce{NaCH3COO}$ are acidic, basic or neutral.

**$\ce{NaCl}$: neutral.** $\ce{Na+}$ is the cation of the strong base $\ce{NaOH}$, and $\ce{Cl-}$ is the conjugate base of the strong acid $\ce{HCl}$. Neither is the conjugate of something *weak*, so neither reacts appreciably with water.

**$\ce{NH4Cl}$: acidic.** $\ce{NH4+}$ is the conjugate acid of the weak base ammonia, so it donates a proton to water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\ce{Cl-}$ is a spectator.

**$\ce{NaCH3COO}$: basic.** Acetate is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it accepts a proton from water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\ce{Na+}$ is a spectator.

**The rule in one line:** the conjugate of something *weak* reacts with water; the anion of a strong acid and the cation of a strong base do not.

</Worked>

</Answer>

## Ladder 10: Buffers

A buffer holds appreciable amounts of *both* members of a conjugate pair, so it can neutralize either added acid or added base.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= \text{p}K_a \\
&\quad + \log\frac{[\text{base form}]}{[\text{acid form}]}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Worked title="pH and how it holds">

A buffer is 0.40 M in $\ce{CH3COOH}$ and 0.25 M in $\ce{CH3COO-}$ (p$K_a$ = 4.74). Find its pH, and explain how it resists change.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= 4.74 + \log\frac{0.25}{0.40} \\
&= 4.74 + (-0.20) \\
&= \mathbf{4.54}
\end{aligned}
$$

Below the p$K_a$, as it must be when the acid form dominates.

**Added $\ce{H3O+}$** is used up by the base form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Strong acid becomes weak acid, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ barely rises.

**Added $\ce{OH-}$** is used up by the acid form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why the pH barely moves:** it depends on the *ratio* of the pair, and a small addition changes that ratio only slightly. A logarithm of a slightly changed ratio is a barely changed number.

</Worked>

## Ladder 11: Buffer capacity

Buffer capacity is how much acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH moves sharply. It depends on the **absolute amounts** of the pair, which the pH does not.

<Worked title="Same pH, different capacity">

Two buffers, 1.00 L of each, both have pH 4.74. Buffer X is 1.0 M in each form; buffer Y is 0.010 M in each. Compare them.

**Same pH:** both have a 1 to 1 ratio, and pH depends on the ratio, so both read 4.74.

**Very different capacity:** X holds a hundred times more of each form. Adding 0.005 mol of strong acid barely dents X's reservoir, but would use up half of Y's base form and move its pH substantially.

**The distinction to hold on to:** **ratio** sets the pH; **amount** sets the capacity. Two independent properties, controlled by two different things.

**Best capacity when pH = p$K_a$,** because the ratio is then 1 and the buffer has equal reserves in both directions. Useful range: within about one unit of the p$K_a$.

</Worked>

## Ladder 12: Titration curves

Four landmarks on a weak acid's titration curve: the start, the half-equivalence point (pH = p$K_a$), the equivalence point (above 7), and the region of excess base.

<Worked title="Reading the four regions">

A 25.00 mL sample of 0.100 M acetic acid ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, p$K_a$ = 4.74) is titrated with 0.100 M $\ce{NaOH}$. Describe each landmark.

**Start (0 mL):** weak acid alone. The $K_a$ calculation, with $x$ for $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and $x$ left out beside 0.100, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.100)} \\
&= 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{1.34 \times 10^{-3}}{0.100} \times 100\% = 1.3\%
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(1.34 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= \mathbf{2.87}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 2.87, not 1.00, because the acid is weak.

**Half-equivalence (12.50 mL):** half the acid has become acetate, so $[\ce{HA}] = [\ce{A-}]$ and

$$
\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a = \mathbf{4.74}
$$

Reading a p$K_a$ straight off a curve at this point is a standard exam task.

**Equivalence (25.00 mL):** all the acid is now acetate, a weak base. It takes protons from water, so the pH is **above 7**. The 2.500 mmol of acetate is in 50.00 mL:

$$
\frac{2.500\ \text{mmol}}{50.00\ \text{mL}} = 0.0500\ \text{M}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_b &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= 5.6 \times 10^{-10}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(5.6 \times 10^{-10})(0.0500)} \\
&= 5.3 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{5.3 \times 10^{-6}}{0.0500} \times 100\% \\
&= 0.011\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Under 5 percent, so the shortcut holds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pOH} &= -\log(5.3 \times 10^{-6}) \\
&= 5.28
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{pH} = 14.00 - 5.28 = \mathbf{8.72}
$$

This is the landmark most often missed; equivalence does *not* mean pH 7. Of the titrations in this unit, only a strong acid with a strong base ends there.

**Beyond equivalence:** excess strong base dominates and the pH approaches that of the titrant.

**Indicator choice follows:** of topic 8.7's indicators, thymol blue (8.0 to 9.6) holds 8.72; bromocresol green (3.8 to 5.4) would change far too early, in the buffer region.

</Worked>
