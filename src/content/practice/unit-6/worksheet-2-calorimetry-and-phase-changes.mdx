Work each question on paper before you open its answer.

You will need these relationships. The first is for a substance whose temperature changes while its phase does not. The second is for a phase change, where the temperature stays the same.

$$
q = mc\Delta T
$$

$$
q = n\,\Delta H
$$

Use these values. All of them are for water: most questions on this page are about water, or use water to measure something else.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Specific heat capacity of liquid water | 4.18 J/(g·°C) |
| Specific heat capacity of ice | 2.09 J/(g·°C) |
| Specific heat capacity of steam | 2.03 J/(g·°C) |
| Molar enthalpy of fusion of water, $\Delta H_\text{fus}$ | 6.02 kJ/mol |
| Molar enthalpy of vaporization of water, $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ | 40.7 kJ/mol |
| Molar mass of water | 18.02 g/mol |

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Warming and cooling water" topic="6.4">

Straight substitution.

<Part label="a">

Energy to raise 50.0 g of water from 20.0 °C to 80.0 °C: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (50.0)(4.18)(60.0) \\
&= 12{,}540\ \text{J} \\
&= \mathbf{12.5}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Energy released when 250.0 g of water cools by 15.0 °C: <Blank />

<Answer>

15.7 kJ is released. As a signed quantity:

$$
q = -15.7\ \text{kJ}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why is water's specific heat capacity unusually large? Name one consequence.

<Answer>

Hydrogen bonding means much of the added energy goes into disrupting the hydrogen-bond network instead of into the molecules' kinetic energy, so a large input produces a small temperature rise. One consequence: coastal climates are milder than inland ones.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="A reaction in a coffee-cup calorimeter" topic="6.4, 6.6">

A reaction is run in 100.0 g of water in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The temperature rises 5.20 °C, and 0.0250 mol of the limiting reactant was consumed.

The limiting reactant has a coefficient of 1 in the balanced equation. Take the mass of the solution as 100.0 g, and use the specific heat capacity of water for it.

<Part label="a">

Calculate $q_\text{water}$.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{water} &= (100.0)(4.18) \\
&\quad \times (5.20) \\
&= \mathbf{2{,}174}\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

This energy is absorbed by the water.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State $q_\text{rxn}$ and explain the sign.

<Answer>

$$
q_\text{rxn} = -2{,}174\ \text{J}
$$

The water gained that energy, so the reaction must have lost it. The two are equal in size and opposite in sign.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate $\Delta H$ in kJ/mol.

<Answer>

The limiting reactant's coefficient is 1, so the moles of it consumed are the moles of reaction. Carry the energy from part (a) before it was rounded, 2,173.6 J.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= \frac{-2{,}173.6}{0.0250} \\
&= -86{,}900\ \text{J/mol} \\
&= \mathbf{-86.9}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why is the answer reported per mole and not in kilojoules alone?

<Answer>

Because the energy per mole of reaction is a property of the reaction: it does not depend on how much was run, while the energy of one experiment does. Reporting bare kilojoules describes only this experiment.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="The specific heat capacity of a metal" topic="6.4">

A 50.0 g metal block at 100.0 °C is dropped into 100.0 g of water at 22.0 °C. The final temperature is 24.6 °C.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the heat gained by the water.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (100.0)(4.18)(2.6) \\
&= \mathbf{1{,}087}\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal.

<Answer>

The metal lost the energy that the water gained, so its $q$ is negative. Its temperature change is its final temperature minus its starting temperature.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{metal} &= -1{,}087\ \text{J} \\
&= (50.0)(c) \\
&\quad \times (24.6 - 100.0) \\
&= (50.0)(c)(-75.4)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
c = \mathbf{0.288}\ \text{J/(g·°C)}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The metal's temperature changed by more than 75 °C while the water's changed by less than 3 °C. Give two reasons.

<Answer>

Water has a specific heat capacity more than 14 times larger, so the same energy changes its temperature far less. There is also twice as much water by mass. Both effects push the same way.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Melting and vaporizing water" topic="6.5">

Phase changes.

<Part label="a">

Energy to melt 36.0 g of ice at 0 °C: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{36.0}{18.02} \\
&= 2.00\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (2.00)(6.02) \\
&= \mathbf{12.0}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Energy to vaporize 18.0 g of water at 100 °C: <Blank />

<Answer>

40.7 kJ

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Energy to melt 90.0 g of ice: <Blank />

<Answer>

The same two steps as in part (a): moles first, then moles times the molar enthalpy of fusion.

$$
n = 4.994\ \text{mol}
$$

$$
q = \mathbf{30.1}\ \text{kJ}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Vaporizing a mole costs about seven times what melting a mole costs. Explain in terms of intermolecular forces.

<Answer>

Melting only has to loosen the lattice so molecules can move past one another, and many hydrogen bonds survive. Vaporizing must separate the molecules completely, breaking essentially all of the remaining attractions.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Why q = mcΔT fails during melting" topic="6.5">

Why can $q = mc\Delta T$ not be used during melting?

<Answer>

Because $\Delta T$ is zero while a substance melts, so the expression would give $q = 0$, which is plainly wrong. The energy added is going into overcoming intermolecular forces, raising potential energy and not kinetic energy, so the temperature does not change.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="Ice to steam" topic="6.5">

Take 25.0 g of ice at $-10.0$ °C all the way to steam at 110.0 °C.

<Part label="a">

Which equation applies to the sloped regions of the heating curve?

<Answer>

$$
q = mc\Delta T
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Which applies to the flat regions?

<Answer>

$$
q = n\,\Delta H
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the total energy required.

<Answer>

**The amount of water.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{25.0}{18.02} \\
&= 1.387\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each warming step comes out in joules. Change it to kilojoules before you add.

**Ice, from $-10$ °C to 0 °C.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (25.0)(2.09)(10.0) \\
&= 522.5\ \text{J} \\
&= 0.52\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Melt.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (1.387)(6.02) \\
&= 8.35\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Water, from 0 °C to 100 °C.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (25.0)(4.18)(100.0) \\
&= 10{,}450\ \text{J} \\
&= 10.45\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Boil.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (1.387)(40.7) \\
&= 56.45\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Steam, from 100 °C to 110 °C.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (25.0)(2.03)(10.0) \\
&= 507.5\ \text{J} \\
&= 0.51\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Total.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{total} &= 0.52 + 8.35 + 10.45 \\
&\quad + 56.45 + 0.51 \\
&= 76.28\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

To three significant figures, the total is $\mathbf{76.3}$ kJ.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Which single step dominates, and what fraction of the total is it?

<Answer>

Boiling, about 74%.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="A steam burn" topic="6.5">

Explain why a steam burn at 100 °C is worse than a burn from liquid water at the same temperature.

<Answer>

Before its temperature can fall at all, the steam must condense, releasing 40.7 kJ/mol. That energy is delivered to the skin in addition to whatever the subsequent cooling releases, so far more total energy is transferred.

</Answer>

</Question>
