Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through the worksheet: when a reaction or a dissolving happens in water, the thermometer measures the surroundings, not the system.

Signs are written from the **system's** point of view.

- Exothermic: the system releases energy. When the energy leaves as heat, $q < 0$ and the surroundings warm.
- Endothermic: the system absorbs energy. When the energy enters as heat, $q > 0$ and the surroundings cool.

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Five changes and their signs" topic="6.1">

Classify each change as exothermic or endothermic, and give the sign of $q_{\text{system}}$.

<Part label="a">

Combustion of propane: <Blank />

<Answer>

Exothermic, $q < 0$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

An instant cold pack activating: <Blank />

<Answer>

Endothermic, $q > 0$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Water freezing: <Blank />

<Answer>

Exothermic, $q < 0$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Ice melting: <Blank />

<Answer>

Endothermic, $q > 0$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

Photosynthesis: <Blank />

<Answer>

Endothermic, and the sign is positive. This change is not like the other four: the energy enters as light, not as heat. So the sign to give is that of the system's energy change, which is positive, and not that of a heat $q$.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="A thermometer in a reacting solution" topic="6.1">

A reaction is run in solution and the thermometer reading rises from 22.0 °C to 28.4 °C.

<Part label="a">

Sign of $q_{\text{water}}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

Positive.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Sign of $\Delta H_{\text{rxn}}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

Negative.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why the two signs are opposite even though only one event occurred.

<Answer>

They describe the same energy from opposite sides of the system boundary. The reaction released energy, so from the system's point of view $q$ is negative; the water absorbed that same energy, so from its point of view $q$ is positive.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A student writes "the temperature went up, so the reaction is endothermic." Correct them in one sentence.

<Answer>

The thermometer was in the surroundings, not the system: a rising temperature means the surroundings gained energy, so the reaction *released* it and is exothermic.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Energy diagrams" topic="6.2">

Four short questions on energy diagrams and energy profiles.

<Part label="a">

Products drawn below reactants means the reaction is: <Blank />

<Answer>

Exothermic.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\Delta H$ on a diagram is measured between: <Blank />

<Answer>

Reactants and products.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

An energy profile adds the path between the two levels: a hill, which belongs to topic 5.6. Does the height of the barrier between them affect $\Delta H$?

<Answer>

No. $\Delta H$ depends only on the relative energies of reactants and products; the barrier affects the rate, not the enthalpy change.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Sketch, in words, the energy profile for an endothermic reaction with a large activation barrier.

<Answer>

Reactants low on the left, a tall peak in the middle, which is the transition state, and products at a level above the reactants but below the peak.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="A hot metal block in cool water" topic="6.3">

A hot metal block is dropped into cool water.

<Part label="a">

In which direction does energy flow?

<Answer>

Hot to cold.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain the transfer at the particle level.

<Answer>

The metal's atoms have more kinetic energy, on average, than the water molecules. Atoms at the metal's surface collide with water molecules and pass some of that kinetic energy to them. The metal's average kinetic energy falls and the water's rises, which is what the two temperature changes register.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

When does the transfer stop, and what is happening then?

<Answer>

Net transfer stops at thermal equilibrium, when both reach the same temperature. Collisions and energy exchange continue, but now at equal rates in both directions, so there is no further net change.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why is "the block has a lot of heat" a poor way to put it?

<Answer>

Heat is energy *in transit* because of a temperature difference, not a quantity a body stores. What the block has is energy, and a temperature.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="An ammonium nitrate cold pack" topic="6.1">

An ammonium nitrate cold pack drops from 21.0 °C to 4.0 °C.

<Part label="a">

Sign of $\Delta H$ for the dissolving: <Blank />

<Answer>

Positive.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Where did the energy come from?

<Answer>

From the water, which is the surroundings here. The dissolving process absorbed it, which is exactly why the water cooled.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Does an endothermic process being favorable contradict anything you know? Answer briefly.

<Answer>

No. Energy is not the only thing that decides whether a change happens. The other factor is called entropy, and here dissolving spreads the ions out through the water. Unit 9 takes that up.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="Two identical blocks" topic="6.3, 6.4">

Two identical blocks, one at 80 °C and one at 20 °C, are placed in contact and insulated from everything else. Predict the final temperature and justify.

<Answer>

The blocks have the same mass, and they are the same substance, so they have the same specific heat capacity. With both equal, the final temperature is about 50 °C. Energy transfers of equal size produce temperature changes of equal size: one falls by as much as the other rises, meeting in the middle.

</Answer>

</Question>
