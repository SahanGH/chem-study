There are ten ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions on the same skill, with no help. Work alone, and work all four before you check any of them.

**The habit that decides this unit: track the sign, every time.** Energy leaving the system is negative; energy entering is positive. Most lost points here are not arithmetic. They are a correct number with the wrong sign, or a $\Delta T$ taken for the wrong substance.

**Use these values.**

- The specific heat capacity of water: $c(\ce{H2O})$ = 4.18 J/(g·°C)
- The enthalpy of fusion of water: $\Delta H_{\text{fus}}$ = 334 J/g
- The enthalpy of vaporization of water: $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ = 2,260 J/g

The last two are given per gram. They are water's molar values, 6.02 kJ/mol and 40.7 kJ/mol, divided by its molar mass, 18.02 g/mol, and rounded to three significant figures.

A $\Delta H$ written beside an equation is for the equation as written.

**One scope note.** The difference between enthalpy and the energy of the system is not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for it.

## Ladder 1: Endothermic and exothermic

Define the *system* (the reaction) and the *surroundings* (everything else, including the beaker and the thermometer). Then ask which way energy flowed.

<Worked title="Reading a temperature change backward">

A beaker becomes cold when two solids are mixed. State the sign of $\Delta H$ and explain the flow.

**The surroundings got colder**, so the surroundings *lost* energy. That energy went into the system.

**The system absorbed energy**, so the process is **endothermic** and $\Delta H$ is **positive**.

**The reversal that trips people:** what you feel is the *surroundings*, but the sign refers to the *system*. A cold beaker means a positive $\Delta H$; a hot beaker means a negative one.

**What never to write:** "the reaction gives off cold". Cold is not a substance. Energy flowed one way only: into the system.

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Energy diagrams

The vertical axis is energy. Where the products sit relative to the reactants gives the sign of $\Delta H$. The picture with a hill between them is an energy profile, and the hill is topic 5.6's: its top is the transition state, and its height above the reactants is the activation energy, which does not set $\Delta H$.

<Worked title="Reading both quantities off one picture">

An energy profile shows reactants at 60 kJ, a peak (the transition state) at 150 kJ and products at 25 kJ. Find $E_a$ and $\Delta H$, and classify the reaction.

**$E_a$ (forward):**

$$
150 - 60 = \mathbf{90}\ \text{kJ}
$$

**$\Delta H$:** products minus reactants,

$$
25 - 60 = \mathbf{-35}\ \text{kJ}
$$

Negative, so **exothermic**.

**The visual signature:** products *below* reactants means exothermic; above means endothermic. Read the two *ends*, never the peak, for $\Delta H$.

**Why the peak is irrelevant to $\Delta H$:** a catalyst lowers the peak without moving either end, so it changes $E_a$ and leaves $\Delta H$ untouched. The height of the peak does not set $\Delta H$.

</Worked>

## Ladder 3: Heat transfer and thermal equilibrium

Energy flows from hotter to cooler until both reach the same temperature. Same temperature does not mean same energy.

<Worked title="What equalizes and what does not">

Two blocks at different temperatures are placed in contact and isolated. Describe what happens at the particle level, and state what is equal at equilibrium.

**At the boundary:** particles of the hotter block have greater average kinetic energy. They collide with particles of the cooler block and, on balance, transfer energy to them.

**Over time:** the hotter block's average kinetic energy falls, the cooler block's rises, until the two averages match. Collisions continue, but with no further *net* transfer: the flow is now equal in both directions.

**What is equal at equilibrium: the temperature**, and with it the average kinetic energy per particle.

**What is *not* equal: the total thermal energy.** That depends on mass and specific heat capacity too. A large tank of water at 25 °C holds far more energy than a small block of copper at the same temperature.

</Worked>

## Ladder 4: Specific heat capacity

The relationship is $q = mc\,\Delta T$. Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one gram by 1 °C: a property of the substance, not of the sample.

<Worked title="Energy to warm water">

How much energy warms 125 g of water from 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C?

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= mc\,\Delta T \\
&= (125)(4.18)(25.0) \\
&= \mathbf{1.31 \times 10^{4}}\ \text{J} \\
&= 13.1\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**$\Delta T$ is final minus initial**, and its size in °C equals its size in kelvins, so either unit works here, as long as you use a *difference*.

**Why water's value is so large.** At 4.18 J/(g·°C), water takes far more energy per gram for each degree than most substances (copper is 0.385 J/(g·°C)). Much of the energy goes into its hydrogen-bond network instead of into the molecules' kinetic energy, which is why water moderates climate and makes a good coolant.

</Worked>

## Ladder 5: Calorimetry

In a coffee-cup calorimeter the *solution* gains the energy a reaction releases, or gives up the energy it absorbs, so $q$ is computed for the solution, and the reaction's $\Delta H$ is the opposite sign, per mole.

<Worked title="A neutralization">

A 50.0 mL sample of 1.00 M $\ce{HCl}$ and 50.0 mL of 1.00 M $\ce{NaOH}$, both at 21.0 °C, are mixed. The temperature peaks at 28.2 °C. Take the density of the solution as 1.00 g/mL and its specific heat capacity as 4.18 J/(g·°C). Find $\Delta H$ per mole of water formed.

**Mass of solution**, at the density given:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(100.0\ \text{mL})(1.00\ \text{g/mL}) \\
&= 100.0\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

Use the *total* volume, not one of them.

**Temperature change of the solution**, final minus initial:

$$
28.2 - 21.0 = 7.2\ \text{°C}
$$

**Energy absorbed by the solution:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (100.0)(4.18)(7.2) \\
&= 3{,}010\ \text{J} \\
&= 3.01\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Moles of water formed:**

$$
(0.0500)(1.00) = 0.0500\ \text{mol}
$$

Both reactants supply exactly this, so neither is in excess.

**Per mole, with the sign flipped:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= -\frac{3.01}{0.0500} \\
&= \mathbf{-60.2}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Negative because the solution *gained* what the reaction *released*.

**Two traps:** using 50.0 g instead of 100.0 g, and forgetting to flip the sign.

</Worked>

Why is the total mass of solution used, not one reactant's?

<Answer>

The whole solution absorbs the energy released.

Because the energy released spreads through *all* the liquid present. The whole mixed solution is what warms up, so its whole mass appears in $q = mc\,\Delta T$.

</Answer>

<Part label="b">

<Answer>

$\Delta T$ would be essentially **unchanged**: twice the energy is released, but it warms twice as much solution. $\Delta H$ per mole is also **unchanged**. The energy per mole of reaction is a property of the reaction: it does not depend on how much was run, while the energy of one experiment does.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why is $\Delta H$ negative when the temperature rose?

<Answer>

The system released the energy.

Because the sign convention refers to the **system**. The solution (surroundings) gained energy, so the reaction released it, making $\Delta H$ negative.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Answer>

**Some of the energy escapes from the solution** as heat to the cup, the thermometer and the air, so the measured temperature rise, and hence the computed $q$, is smaller than the true value. Another sound answer: the heat capacity of the calorimeter itself is neglected.

</Answer>

## Ladder 6: Energy of phase changes

A phase change uses $q = m\,\Delta H_{\text{phase}}$: mass only, no $\Delta T$, because the temperature does not change.

<Worked title="Ice to steam in three steps">

Find the energy to convert 40.0 g of ice at 0 °C into steam at 100 °C.

**Step 1: melt** (no $\Delta T$):

$$
q = (40.0)(334) = 13.4\ \text{kJ}
$$

**Step 2: warm the liquid** (this one has a $\Delta T$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (40.0)(4.18)(100) \\
&= 16.7\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3: vaporize** (no $\Delta T$):

$$
q = (40.0)(2{,}260) = 90.4\ \text{kJ}
$$

Each product is in joules (13,360 J, 16,720 J and 90,400 J), and each result is written in kilojoules to three significant figures. Add the values before they are rounded:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Total} &= 13.36 + 16.72 + 90.40 \\
&= 120.48\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is $\mathbf{120}$ kJ to three significant figures. (The three rounded values add to 120.5 kJ, which is too close to round safely.)

**Notice which step dominates.** Vaporizing takes more than the other two combined, because melting only loosens the lattice while vaporizing must separate the molecules completely, overcoming essentially all the hydrogen bonding.

**The structural error to avoid:** putting a $\Delta T$ into a phase-change step. During melting or boiling the temperature is constant, so there is no $\Delta T$ to use.

</Worked>

## Ladder 7: Hess's law

Enthalpy changes add. Reverse a reaction and the sign flips; multiply a reaction and $\Delta H$ multiplies too.

<Worked title="Manipulating to reach a target">

You are given

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C(s, graphite) + O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g)} \\
&\Delta H = -393.5\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CO(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> CO2(g)} \\
&\Delta H = -283.0\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Find $\Delta H$ for

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C(s, graphite) + 1/2O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Target check:** $\ce{CO}$ must end on the *right*, but in the second equation it is on the left. So reverse the second and flip its sign:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CO2(g) -> CO(g) + 1/2O2(g)} \\
&\Delta H = +283.0\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Add to the first:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C(s, graphite) + O2(g) +} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) + CO(g) +} \\
&\quad \ce{1/2O2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The $\ce{CO2}$ cancels and the oxygens net to $\ce{1/2 O2}$ on the left.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= (-393.5) + (+283.0) \\
&= \mathbf{-110.5}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why it works:** energy is conserved, so the enthalpy change between a given start and a given finish cannot depend on the route. Any sequence of steps connecting the same start and finish must sum to the same total, which is what lets us obtain a value for a reaction too difficult to measure directly, as this one is.

</Worked>

<Answer>

$\mathbf{+50}$ kJ: reversing flips the sign.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Answer>

$\mathbf{-100}$ kJ: doubling the amounts doubles the energy.

</Answer>

<Part label="c">

Why can enthalpy changes be added at all?

<Answer>

Enthalpy depends only on the states.

Because energy is conserved. If two routes between the same reactants and the same products gave different totals, going out by one and back by the other would release energy with nothing used up. So enthalpy depends only on the current state of the system, not on how it got there: the difference between reactants and products is fixed, and any path between them sums to the same total.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why is Hess's law useful in practice?

<Answer>

It gives values that cannot be measured directly.

It supplies $\Delta H$ for reactions that cannot be run cleanly in a calorimeter. The example above is exactly such a case: burning carbon to give *only* $\ce{CO}$ is not achievable in practice, because burning carbon in a limited supply of oxygen gives a mixture and cannot be made to stop at $\ce{CO}$, so the value must be obtained indirectly.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 8: Bond enthalpies

Breaking bonds costs energy; forming them releases it.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &\approx \sum(\text{bonds broken}) \\
&\quad - \sum(\text{bonds formed})
\end{aligned}
$$

<Worked title="A simple gas-phase reaction">

Estimate $\Delta H$ for

$$
\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}
$$

from these bond enthalpies, in kJ/mol: $\ce{H-H}$ 436, $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ 243, $\ce{H-Cl}$ 431.

**Broken** (energy in):

$$
436 + 243 = 679\ \text{kJ}
$$

**Formed** (energy out):

$$
2 \times 431 = 862\ \text{kJ}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &\approx 679 - 862 \\
&= \mathbf{-183}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Negative, so exothermic: more energy is released making the new bonds than was needed to break the old ones. The accepted value is $-185\ \text{kJ}$, so the estimate is good to about 1%.

**Why only an estimate:** for most bonds, the tabulated bond enthalpy is an *average* across many different compounds, and a particular bond in a particular molecule differs slightly from the average. Here each kind of bond is found in one molecule only ($\ce{H-H}$ in $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ in $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{H-Cl}$ in $\ce{HCl}$), so these three values are not averages, and the estimate is close. The small gap that is left comes from rounding in the table's values. The method also assumes every species is a gas.

**Direction of the subtraction:** broken *minus* formed. Reversing it gives the right size with the wrong sign, which is a whole-question error.

</Worked>

## Ladder 9: Enthalpy of formation

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} &= \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) \\
&\quad - \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})
\end{aligned}
$$

An element in its standard state has $\Delta H_f^\circ = 0$.

<Worked title="A combustion from standard enthalpies of formation">

Find $\Delta H^\circ$ for

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C2H4(g) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

using these values of $\Delta H_f^\circ$, in kJ/mol: $\ce{C2H4}$ $+52.3$, $\ce{CO2}$ $-393.5$, $\ce{H2O(l)}$ $-285.8$.

**Products**, with coefficients applied:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(-393.5) + 2(-285.8) \\
&= -787.0 - 571.6 \\
&= -1{,}358.6\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Reactants:**

$$
(+52.3) + 3(0) = 52.3\ \text{kJ}
$$

Oxygen is an element in its standard state, so its value is **zero**. It is not omitted; it is genuinely zero.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ &= -1{,}358.6 - 52.3 \\
&= \mathbf{-1{,}411}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Two things to get right:** apply the coefficients (a missed factor of 2 here changes the answer by hundreds of kilojoules), and subtract in the right direction: products minus reactants.

</Worked>

## Ladder 10: Choosing the right method

Four routes give $\Delta H$. Knowing which one the data are asking for is half the skill.

<Worked title="Matching data to method">

State which method to use for each set of data.

**Given a temperature change in a calorimeter:** use $q = mc\,\Delta T$, then divide by moles and flip the sign. This is the only *experimental* route.

**Given a table of $\Delta H_f^\circ$ values:** use products minus reactants. Unlike the bond-enthalpy route, this is not an estimate, and it is the usual default on the exam.

**Given a table of bond enthalpies:** use broken minus formed. Only an estimate, and only for gases.

**Given other reactions with their $\Delta H$ values:** use Hess's law: reverse and scale until they add to the target.

**The tell:** the data given *is* the instruction. Temperatures mean calorimetry; a $\Delta H_f^\circ$ table means the formation formula; bonds mean the estimate; whole reactions mean Hess's law.

</Worked>

<Question number={10} label="Your turn" topic="6.4, 6.6, 6.7, 6.8, 6.9">

<Part label="a">

You are given the mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change. Which method?

<Answer>

You are given two reactions with known $\Delta H$. Which method?

<Answer>

Hess's law.

**Hess's law**: reverse and scale them until they sum to the target reaction.

<Part label="c">

Which method gives only an estimate, and why?

<Answer>

Bond enthalpies: averaged values.

**Bond enthalpies**, because most of the tabulated values are averages across many compounds rather than values for the specific molecules in question. It also assumes all species are gaseous.

</Answer>

Which method is the only truly experimental one?

<Answer>

Calorimetry.

**Calorimetry.** The other three all begin from tabulated data that someone else measured; calorimetry is the measurement itself.
