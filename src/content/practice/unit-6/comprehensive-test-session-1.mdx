## Part A: Systems, signs and dissolving

Four questions on where energy goes when a solid dissolves, when two liquids mix, and when a gas is squeezed or made. You will weigh energy taken in against energy released, test a table of trials for entries that cannot be right, and tell heat from work.

<Question number={1} title="Three salts, energy in and energy out" topic="6.1" points={5}>

Three salts dissolve in water: lithium bromide, potassium bromide and lithium fluoride. Lithium fluoride is only slightly soluble, but a little of it does dissolve.

For each salt the table gives two amounts of energy, both for 1 mol of the salt dissolving.

- **Energy taken in:** to separate the ions of the solid from one another and to make room for them in the water.
- **Energy released:** as attractions form between the ions and the water molecules.

Use these values. Each is rounded to the nearest kilojoule.

| Salt | Energy taken in (kJ) | Energy released (kJ) |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{LiBr}$ | 807 | 856 |
| $\ce{KBr}$ | 682 | 662 |
| $\ce{LiF}$ | 1,036 | 1,031 |

The system is the ions and the attractions that are overcome and formed. The water's temperature, which a thermometer reads, belongs to the surroundings.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the net energy change of the system when 1 mol of each salt dissolves. Give each with its sign.

<Answer>

Energy taken in raises the system's energy. Energy released lowers it. So the net change is the energy taken in minus the energy released.

Lithium bromide:

$$
807 - 856 = \mathbf{-49}\ \text{kJ}
$$

Potassium bromide:

$$
682 - 662 = \mathbf{+20}\ \text{kJ}
$$

Lithium fluoride:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1{,}036 - 1{,}031 \\
&= \mathbf{+5}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

A negative sign means the system ends with less energy than it started with. A positive sign means it ends with more.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State, for each salt, whether the water warms or cools as the salt dissolves. Justify your answer for lithium bromide.

<Answer>

- Lithium bromide: the water **warms**.
- Potassium bromide: the water **cools**.
- Lithium fluoride: the water **cools**, though only slightly.

For lithium bromide, forming the ion–water attractions releases 856 kJ, which is 49 kJ more than the 807 kJ taken in. Energy is not destroyed, so that extra 49 kJ passes to the surroundings. The water is part of the surroundings, so it warms. The dissolving is exothermic.

For the other two salts, more energy is taken in than released. The difference comes out of the surroundings, so the water cools. Those dissolvings are endothermic.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student claims: "The salt whose ions attract water most strongly warms the water most." Use the table to decide whether the claim holds, and state what does decide whether the water warms or cools.

<Answer>

The claim **does not hold**.

Lithium fluoride releases the most energy as its ions attract water: 1,031 kJ, against 856 kJ for lithium bromide and 662 kJ for potassium bromide. By the claim it should warm the water most. But the water cools when lithium fluoride dissolves, because separating its ions takes in even more, 1,036 kJ. Lithium bromide releases less than lithium fluoride does, and it is the only one of the three that warms the water.

What decides the direction is the **comparison** of the two amounts, not the size of either one. The water warms when more energy is released than taken in, and cools when more is taken in than released.

</Answer>

</Part>

Signs or directions with no sizes (for example "negative, positive, positive") earn the second point only.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for warms, cools, cools, with the reason for lithium bromide: more energy is released than taken in, and the surroundings (the water) gain the difference. The three directions with no reason earn 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for rejecting the claim with evidence from the table: lithium fluoride releases the most energy (1,031 kJ) and its water still cools, or lithium bromide releases less than lithium fluoride and is the one that warms; 1 point for saying that the direction depends on which is larger, the energy taken in or the energy released. Rejecting the claim with no values or salts named earns the second point at most.</RubricLine>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Mixing two liquids" topic="6.1" points={4}>

A student mixes two liquids in an insulated cup, three times. Every liquid starts at 22.0 °C. No reaction takes place: the molecules only mix. The table gives the temperature just after mixing. (The readings are for illustration.)

| Mixture | Liquids | After mixing (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | acetone and chloroform | 32.5 |
| 2 | 1-propanol and heptane | 18.5 |
| 3 | heptane and octane | 22.0 |

Some facts about the molecules:

- Acetone, $\ce{CH3COCH3}$, is polar. Its oxygen atom has lone pairs. All of its hydrogen atoms are bonded to carbon.
- Chloroform, $\ce{CHCl3}$, is polar. Its three chlorine atoms pull electrons away from the carbon, so its one hydrogen atom is strongly δ+. That hydrogen is attracted strongly to a lone pair on the oxygen of an acetone molecule. It is attracted much less to another chloroform molecule.
- 1-Propanol, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$, has an O–H group.
- Heptane, $\ce{C7H16}$, and octane, $\ce{C8H18}$, are nonpolar chains of nearly the same length.

Treat mixing as you treat dissolving. The system is the molecules and the attractions between them. The temperature that the thermometer reads counts as surroundings.

<Part label="a">

State whether the mixing in mixture 1 is exothermic or endothermic, and explain the temperature change in terms of the attractions overcome and the attractions formed.

<Answer>

The mixing is **exothermic**. The reading rose from 22.0 °C to 32.5 °C, so the surroundings gained energy, and the system lost it.

To mix, acetone molecules must move apart from other acetone molecules, and chloroform molecules from other chloroform molecules. Overcoming those attractions takes energy in. In the mixture, new attractions form between unlike molecules: the δ+ hydrogen of a chloroform molecule is held by a lone pair on the oxygen of an acetone molecule. Neither pure liquid has this attraction, and it is a strong one. Forming the new attractions releases more energy than overcoming the old ones took. The extra goes to the surroundings, and the liquid warms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For mixture 2, identify the strongest attraction between molecules in each pure liquid and the attractions between a 1-propanol molecule and a heptane molecule. Then explain why the temperature fell.

<Answer>

**Before mixing.** In 1-propanol the strongest attractions are **hydrogen bonds**: the hydrogen of one molecule's O–H group is attracted to a lone pair on the oxygen of the next molecule. In heptane, which is nonpolar, the only attractions are **London dispersion forces**.

**After mixing.** Heptane has no O–H group and no lone pairs, so a 1-propanol molecule cannot form a hydrogen bond with it. Between the two kinds of molecule there are London dispersion forces, with a weak attraction between the alcohol's dipole and the dipole it induces in the heptane.

**Why it cooled.** Heptane molecules come between the 1-propanol molecules, so some of the hydrogen bonds are broken up. Overcoming them takes a lot of energy in. The attractions that replace them are weaker, so forming them releases less energy than was taken in. The difference comes out of the surroundings, and the temperature falls from 22.0 °C to 18.5 °C. The mixing is endothermic.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify the mixing that shows no measurable energy change. A student says of it, "The temperature did not change, so no attractions were overcome and none were formed." Explain what is wrong with the student's statement.

<Answer>

**Mixture 3**, heptane with octane. The temperature stayed at 22.0 °C, so no energy that the thermometer can show passed between the system and the surroundings.

The student is wrong about the attractions. Heptane molecules did move apart from one another, and so did octane molecules. That overcame London dispersion forces and took energy in. New London dispersion forces then formed between heptane and octane molecules, and that released energy. The two liquids are nonpolar chains of nearly the same length, so the new attractions are of nearly the same strength as the old ones. The energy released very nearly equals the energy taken in. The net change is too small to see, not because nothing happened, but because the two amounts balance.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for exothermic with the reason: forming the new attractions between acetone and chloroform molecules releases more energy than overcoming the attractions in the two pure liquids takes in. "Exothermic because the temperature rose", with nothing about attractions, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for hydrogen bonds in 1-propanol and London dispersion forces in heptane and between the unlike molecules (the dipole–induced dipole attraction is welcome but not required; an answer that gives only dipole–induced dipole attractions between the unlike molecules, with the other two right, also earns the point); 1 point for the explanation that overcoming hydrogen bonds takes in more energy than forming the weaker new attractions releases, so the surroundings lose energy. Saying that 1-propanol and heptane form hydrogen bonds with each other does not earn the first point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for mixture 3 with the correction: attractions were overcome and formed, and the energy taken in and the energy released were nearly equal, because the old and new attractions (London dispersion forces) are of nearly the same strength. Naming mixture 3 with no correction earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Four trials of one dissolving" topic="6.1" points={4}>

Sodium thiosulfate pentahydrate, $\ce{Na2S2O3*5H2O}$, is the "hypo" that photographers use to fix film. A student dissolves the solid in water in a foam cup four times. Each time the water starts at room temperature and all of the solid dissolves. The student records $\Delta T$, the final temperature minus the initial temperature. (The data are for illustration.)

| Trial | Solid (g) | Water (g) | $\Delta T$ (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5.0 | 100.0 | $-2.2$ |
| 2 | 10.0 | 100.0 | $-4.2$ |
| 3 | 10.0 | 200.0 | $+2.2$ |
| 4 | 20.0 | 100.0 | $-3.8$ |

The student made two mistakes in recording. One value of $\Delta T$ has the wrong sign and the right size. Another has the right sign and the wrong size. The masses are all correct.

<Part label="a">

Identify the trial whose $\Delta T$ has the wrong sign. Justify your choice, and state what the value should be.

<Answer>

**Trial 3.** It should read about $\mathbf{-2.2}$ **°C**.

In trials 1, 2 and 4 the temperature fell, so the surroundings lost energy and the dissolving took it in: dissolving this solid in water is endothermic. Trial 3 is the same solid dissolving in the same liquid. The same attractions are overcome and the same attractions form, so it must be endothermic too. Changing the amounts cannot turn a dissolving that absorbs energy into one that releases it. The temperature must fall, and $\Delta T$ must be negative.

The size is right. Trial 3 has twice the solid of trial 1 and twice the water, so twice the energy is absorbed from twice the mass of solution:

$$
\frac{5.0}{105.0} = \frac{10.0}{210.0}
$$

The temperature change should match trial 1's, $-2.2$ °C.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify the trial whose $\Delta T$ has the wrong size. Justify your choice, and estimate what the value should be near.

<Answer>

**Trial 4.** It should be near $\mathbf{-7.7}$ **°C**.

Trials 2 and 4 use the same 100.0 g of water. Trial 4 has twice as much solid, so the dissolving absorbs twice as much energy. That energy comes out of a solution that is only a little heavier, so the temperature must fall by more than in trial 2, nearly twice as far. The recorded fall, 3.8 °C, is smaller than trial 2's 4.2 °C. That cannot be right.

For the estimate, start with twice trial 2's change:

$$
2 \times (-4.2) = -8.4\ \text{°C}
$$

The energy is taken from 120.0 g of solution in trial 4, not 110.0 g as in trial 2. The same energy taken from a larger mass changes the temperature less:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-8.4 \times \frac{110.0}{120.0} \\
&= \mathbf{-7.7}\ \text{°C}
\end{aligned}
$$

Trials 1 and 2 fit this pattern, which is why trial 2 is not the one in doubt. Trial 2 has twice the solid of trial 1 in the same water, and its fall is a little less than twice as large: 4.2 °C against 4.4 °C.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

State one thing about $\Delta T$ that must be the same in every trial with this solid and water, and one thing that need not be. Give the reason for each.

<Answer>

**Must be the same: the sign.** The sign of $\Delta T$ tells you which way energy passes between the system and the surroundings. That is set by the process: which attractions are overcome and which are formed when this solid dissolves in water. In these trials each gram of the solid absorbs very nearly the same energy as it dissolves.

**Need not be the same: the size.** The size of $\Delta T$ depends on the amounts. More solid absorbs more energy, and a larger mass of solution changes temperature less for the same energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for trial 3 with the reason that the same solid dissolving in water must absorb energy in every trial, as trials 1, 2 and 4 show, so the temperature must fall. Giving $-2.2$ °C is expected but earns no separate point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for trial 4 with the reason that more solid in the same mass of water must give a larger fall than trial 2's, not a smaller one (the same comparison with trial 1, four times the solid for less than twice the fall, also earns it); 1 point for an estimate with its reasoning. Any value from $-7.5$ °C to $-8.4$ °C earns the second point, so "about twice trial 2's, near $-8$ °C" earns it, and so does four times trial 1's change corrected for the mass of solution. Four times trial 1's change with no correction, $-8.8$ °C, earns the point only if the answer says the true value is somewhat smaller in size. A value with no reasoning does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for both: the sign must be the same (or: the temperature must fall every time), because it belongs to the process (the dissolving is endothermic each time), and the size need not be, because it depends on the amounts of solid and water. An answer that gives, as the thing that must be the same, the temperature change for a fixed mass of solid in a fixed mass of solution (or the energy absorbed by each gram of solid) also earns the point with its reason. One of the two alone earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Heat, work or both" topic="6.1" points={4}>

Here are two observations.

**Observation 1.** A diesel engine has no spark plugs. A driver starts one that has stood outdoors overnight, so the piston and the cylinder wall are at the temperature of the outdoor air. On the engine's first compression stroke the valves are closed, and the piston moves quickly up the cylinder. The air trapped in the cylinder is squeezed into a much smaller space. No fuel is in the cylinder yet, and nothing reacts. By the end of the stroke the air is far hotter than the metal around it, hot enough to set the fuel alight when it is sprayed in a moment later. The cylinder wall has warmed a little too. Ignore friction.

**Observation 2.** A student adds a pinch of manganese(IV) oxide to hydrogen peroxide solution in a flask and at once joins the flask to a gas syringe. The manganese(IV) oxide speeds up the breakdown of the hydrogen peroxide and is not used up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2H2O2(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2H2O(l) + O2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The solution and the flask warm, and the plunger of the syringe moves out against the air of the room. Assume that nothing else warms: no energy passes as heat to the syringe or to the air of the room.

<Part label="a">

In observation 1, take the trapped air as the system.

- Use what the observation says about the metal to rule out heat as the way energy entered the air. Then state how the energy did enter, and what did the pushing.
- State whether the energy that warmed the cylinder wall crossed as heat or as work, and whether it entered or left the system. Then decide whether the air ends the stroke hotter or cooler than it would in a cylinder whose wall let no energy through.

<Answer>

**Not heat.** Heat is energy passed from a warmer body to a cooler one. At the start of the stroke the piston and the cylinder wall are at the temperature of the outdoor air, and so is the air that was drawn in. Nothing that touches the air is warmer than the air, so no energy can enter it as heat. Once the air has begun to warm, the metal is the cooler body, so heat could only pass out of the air.

**Work.** The piston pushes the air into a smaller space. That push is **work done on the system** by the piston, and it carries energy **into** the air. The energy of the air rises. Its molecules have more kinetic energy on average, and that is a higher temperature. Nothing has to react for this.

**The wall.** During the stroke the air becomes warmer than the wall. Energy passes from the warmer air to the cooler wall as **heat**, and this energy **leaves** the system.

**Hotter or cooler.** The air ends the stroke **cooler** than it would in a cylinder whose wall let no energy through. The piston's push brings energy in either way, but here some of that energy has already left as heat. The stroke is quick, so little energy has time to leave, and the air is still hot enough to light the fuel.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

In observation 2, identify the system. Two things are seen: the solution and the flask warm, and the plunger moves out. For each, state whether it shows energy crossing as heat or as work, and in which direction. Then state how the work in observation 2 differs from the work in observation 1.

<Answer>

The system is the hydrogen peroxide that reacts, and the water and oxygen it turns into. The rest is surroundings: the water of the solution, the flask, the syringe and the air of the room.

- **The solution and the flask warm: heat, out of the system.** They are surroundings, and they gained energy. The reaction released it.
- **The plunger moves out: work, out of the system.** The oxygen that forms needs room. It pushes the plunger out against the air of the room. That push is work done by the system.

So energy crosses as **both** heat and work, and both carry energy **out of** the system.

**The difference.** In observation 1 the surroundings (the piston) push on the system, so work carries energy into the system. In observation 2 the system (the oxygen) pushes on the surroundings, so work carries energy out of it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Two students describe observation 2.

- Student X: "The energy the system lost equals the energy the solution and the flask gained as they warmed."
- Student Y: "The system lost more energy than the solution and the flask gained as they warmed."

The student then repeats the experiment in a strong flask closed with a clamped stopper, with no syringe. The solution and the flask warm as before, and nothing moves.

Identify the student whose statement is right for observation 2, and the student whose statement is right for the stoppered flask. Explain what makes the difference.

<Answer>

**Student Y** is right for observation 2. **Student X** is right for the stoppered flask.

**Observation 2.** The warming of the solution and the flask shows only the energy that left the system as heat. The system also lost energy as work, when the oxygen pushed the plunger out against the air of the room. A push does not have to warm anything to carry energy, and by the stem nothing else warmed, so no thermometer shows this share. The system lost the heat plus the work, which is more than the warming accounts for. Student X undercounts.

**The stoppered flask.** The stopper stays where it is, so the oxygen moves nothing. A push that moves nothing is not work, so no energy leaves the system as work. Heat is the only way out, and all of the energy the system lost went to warm the solution and the flask. Now the two amounts are equal, as student X says.

**The difference** is whether the gas moves anything as it forms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for work done on the air by the piston (energy entering the system) as the reason the air warms, with heat ruled out because the metal that touches the air is no warmer than the air; 1 point for heat passing from the warmer air to the cooler wall (energy leaving the system), so that the air ends cooler than it would behind a wall that let no energy through. Saying that the air warms because the fuel burns or because something reacts, or that heat from the engine warms the air, does not earn the first point. "Heat, leaving the system" with the wrong comparison or none does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for both routes, each tied to what is seen and each with its direction: the warming shows heat from the system to the solution and flask, and the moving plunger shows work done by the system. Naming the reacting hydrogen peroxide and its products as the system, and saying that work is done on the system in observation 1 and by the system in observation 2, are expected but earn no separate point. Heat alone, or work alone, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for both: student Y for observation 2, because the energy that left as work in pushing the plunger out is not counted in the warming of the solution and the flask; and student X for the stoppered flask, because nothing is pushed out of the way, so no energy leaves as work. Saying that student X undercounts in observation 2 counts as choosing student Y. One of the two alone, or the two names with no reason, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

## Part B: Energy diagrams, and heat transfer

Six short questions: three on reading and comparing energy diagrams, and three in which you test tables of data against what collisions and thermal equilibrium allow.

<Question number={5} title="Five diagrams, five zeros" topic="6.2" points={4}>

Five changes are each carried out in their own insulated cup. Every cup holds the same mass of water, with a thermometer in it. In each cup the water is the surroundings, and all the energy the system releases or absorbs passes to or from the water.

The table gives the two levels of the energy diagram for each change. Each diagram was drawn by a different person, who put zero where it suited. (The values are made up.)

| Diagram | Starting level (kJ) | Final level (kJ) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | $-85$ |
| 2 | 240 | 155 |
| 3 | $-60$ | 150 |
| 4 | 320 | 110 |
| 5 | 25 | 65 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the system's energy change for each of the five diagrams. Give each with its sign.

<Answer>

The energy change is the final level minus the starting level.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{1:}\quad & -85 - 0 = \mathbf{-85}\ \text{kJ} \\
\text{2:}\quad & 155 - 240 = \mathbf{-85}\ \text{kJ} \\
\text{3:}\quad & 150 - (-60) = \mathbf{+210}\ \text{kJ} \\
\text{4:}\quad & 110 - 320 = \mathbf{-210}\ \text{kJ} \\
\text{5:}\quad & 65 - 25 = \mathbf{+40}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

A negative result means the arrow points down and the system released energy. A positive result means the arrow points up and the system absorbed energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Two of the diagrams could show the same change, drawn with zero in different places. Two others could show one change and its reverse. Identify each pair, and justify each choice.

<Answer>

**Diagrams 1 and 2 could show the same change.** Both arrows point down, and both gaps are 85 kJ. The numbers on the levels differ only because zero was put in different places. Where zero sits is a free choice: only the gap and the direction of the arrow describe the change.

**Diagrams 3 and 4 could show one change and its reverse.** The gaps are the same size, 210 kJ, and the arrows point opposite ways: up in diagram 3 and down in diagram 4. Reversing a change keeps the gap and reverses the arrow.

No other two diagrams have gaps of the same size.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Rank the five cups by what the thermometer does, from the greatest rise in temperature to the greatest fall. Justify your ranking.

<Answer>

The thermometer is in the surroundings. When the system releases energy, the water gains it and warms. When the system absorbs energy, the water loses it and cools. Every cup holds the same mass of water, so the more energy that passes, the larger the change in temperature.

**Greatest rise: cup 4, then cups 1 and 2 (equal), then cup 5, then cup 3: greatest fall.**

- Cup 4 releases the most energy, 210 kJ, so its water warms the most.
- Cups 1 and 2 each release 85 kJ, so their water warms by the same, smaller amount.
- Cup 5 absorbs 40 kJ, so its water cools a little.
- Cup 3 absorbs 210 kJ, so its water cools the most.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for all five energy changes with their signs: $-85$, $-85$, $+210$, $-210$ and $+40$ kJ. Five correct sizes with any sign missing or wrong earn 0 points for this part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for diagrams 1 and 2, with the reason that their gaps are equal and their arrows point the same way (or that their energy changes are equal); 1 point for diagrams 3 and 4, with the reason that their gaps are equal and their arrows point opposite ways (or that their energy changes are equal in size and opposite in sign). A pair named with no reason does not earn its point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for the order 4, then 1 and 2 together, then 5, then 3, with a justification that energy the system releases warms the water and energy it absorbs cools the water. An order consistent with the student's own values from part (a) earns the point. The order alone does not earn the point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="One mole or one kilogram" topic="6.2" points={5}>

In a kiln, magnesium carbonate breaks down into magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MgCO3(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{MgO(s) + CO2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Use these values: the system absorbs 100.7 kJ for each mole of magnesium carbonate that breaks down, and the molar mass of magnesium carbonate is 84.31 g/mol.

Two students each describe an energy diagram for this change.

- **Student 1,** for 1 mol of magnesium carbonate: "The level of $\ce{MgCO3(s)}$ is at 0 kJ. The level of $\ce{MgO(s) + CO2(g)}$ is at 100.7 kJ. The arrow points up from the first level to the second."
- **Student 2,** for a 1.00 kg batch of magnesium carbonate: "The level of $\ce{MgCO3(s)}$ is at 0 kJ. The level of $\ce{MgO(s) + CO2(g)}$ is at 1,190 kJ. The arrow points up from the first level to the second."

<Part label="a">

Determine, with a calculation, whether the two students' diagrams disagree. Explain your answer.

<Answer>

Find how much energy a 1.00 kg batch absorbs. A mass of 1.00 kg is $1.00 \times 10^{3}$ g.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{1.00 \times 10^{3}\ \text{g}}{84.31\ \text{g/mol}} \\
&= 11.86\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&11.86\ \text{mol} \times 100.7\ \text{kJ/mol} \\
&= 1{,}194\ \text{kJ} \\
&\approx \mathbf{1.19 \times 10^{3}}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

To three significant figures that is 1,190 kJ, the gap on Student 2's diagram.

**The two diagrams do not disagree.** The energy a change absorbs depends on how much of the substance changes, so a diagram with numbers is drawn for a stated amount. Student 1's gap is for 1 mol and Student 2's gap is for 11.86 mol. Both show the same change: the same substances on the two levels, the products on the higher level, and the arrow pointing up.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A third student sketches a diagram for 1 mol of magnesium carbonate and describes it this way:

\begin{aligned}
&(0.325)(4.18)(T - 92.0) \\

\end{aligned}
$$

and solves it to get $T = 18.8$ °C.

**Student 2** writes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(325)(4.18)(T - 92.0) \\

\end{aligned}

\begin{aligned}

&\text{steel: } (245)(0.500) \\

\end{aligned}

<Question number={13} title="Which measurements are enough" topic="6.4" points={5}>

A student wants to find the specific heat capacity of ethylene glycol, the liquid in engine coolant. On the bench are a nickel slug, a balance, a thermometer, a beaker of hot water and a foam cup with a lid. The specific heat capacity of nickel is known: 0.444 J/(g·°C).

The plan is to warm the slug in the hot water until it is at the water's temperature, lift it out, dry it quickly and lower it into ethylene glycol in the foam cup. Assume the cup lets no energy in or out and takes in none itself. The student lists eight things that could be measured:

| Letter | Measurement |
|---|---|
| A | The mass of the nickel slug |
| B | The volume of the nickel slug |
| C | The temperature of the hot water, read just before the slug is lifted out |
| D | The mass of the ethylene glycol in the cup |
| E | The temperature of the ethylene glycol before the slug goes in |
| F | The temperature of the room |
| G | The highest temperature the ethylene glycol reaches with the slug in it |
| H | The time the ethylene glycol takes to reach its highest temperature |

<Part label="a">

Identify the measurements that are needed to find the specific heat capacity of ethylene glycol, and explain why each of the others is not needed.

<Answer>

**Needed: A, C, D, E and G.**

The nickel's heat is found from $q = mc\Delta T$ for the nickel. Its $c$ is known, its mass is A, and its temperature change runs from C, where it starts, to G, where it and the liquid end. The ethylene glycol gains what the nickel loses. To turn that energy into a specific heat capacity, you need the liquid's mass, D, and its own temperature change, from E to G.

**Not needed: B, F and H.**

- **B, the slug's volume.** The equation uses the slug's mass, and the nickel's specific heat capacity is per gram. The volume appears nowhere.
- **F, the room's temperature.** The cup is taken to let no energy in or out, so the nickel and the liquid exchange energy only with each other. The room has no part in the calculation.
- **H, the time.** $q = mc\Delta T$ depends on where each temperature starts and ends, not on how long the change takes.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Write an expression for the specific heat capacity of ethylene glycol in terms of the measurements you chose and the specific heat capacity of nickel, $c_\text{Ni}$. Use $m_\text{Ni}$ and $m_\text{liq}$ for the two masses, and $T_\text{C}$, $T_\text{E}$ and $T_\text{G}$ for the three temperatures.

<Answer>

The liquid gains what the nickel loses:

$$
q_\text{liq} = -q_\text{Ni}
$$

Write each $q$ as that substance's own $mc\Delta T$, with each $\Delta T$ as final minus initial:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&m_\text{liq}\,c_\text{liq}\,(T_\text{G} - T_\text{E}) \\
&\quad = -m_\text{Ni}\,c_\text{Ni}\,(T_\text{G} - T_\text{C})
\end{aligned}
$$

The minus sign turns $(T_\text{G} - T_\text{C})$ into $(T_\text{C} - T_\text{G})$, which is positive because the slug cools. Divide by the liquid's mass and temperature change:

$$
c_\text{liq} = \frac{m_\text{Ni}\,c_\text{Ni}\,(T_\text{C} - T_\text{G})}{m_\text{liq}\,(T_\text{G} - T_\text{E})}
$$

Every quantity on the right is positive, so $c_\text{liq}$ comes out positive, as a specific heat capacity must.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The slug cools in the air on its way from the hot water to the cup, so it enters the ethylene glycol a little cooler than measurement C says. Every other measurement is correct. State whether the student's value for the specific heat capacity of ethylene glycol comes out too high or too low, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

**Too high.**

Follow the error through two steps.

First, the energy. The student takes the slug's temperature change as running from C down to G. The slug really started lower than C, so its real fall in temperature inside the cup was smaller. The student's $m_\text{Ni}\,c_\text{Ni}\,(T_\text{C} - T_\text{G})$ is therefore larger than the energy the slug really gave to the liquid. Some of the energy the student counted went into the air before the slug reached the cup.

Second, the specific heat capacity. The liquid's mass and its temperature change, E to G, were measured correctly. The student divides an energy that is too large by a correct $m_\text{liq}\,(T_\text{G} - T_\text{E})$, so the quotient, $c_\text{liq}$, is too large.

In words: the liquid seems to have taken in more energy than it did for the rise that was seen, so it seems harder to warm than it is.

</Answer>

</Part>

A pure substance stays at one temperature while it changes phase. Below 4.04 kJ every reading is higher than the one before it, so the metal was only warming there.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the molar enthalpy of fusion of the metal from the record.

<Answer>

The flat part of the record is the energy that melted the whole sample. It runs from the first liquid to the last solid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= 6.42 - 4.04 \\
&= 2.38\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

That melted 0.500 mol. For melting, $q = n\,\Delta H_\text{fus}$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H_\text{fus} &= \frac{2.38}{0.500} \\
&= \mathbf{4.76}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the molar heat capacity of the solid metal from the record.

<Answer>

Use two rows where the metal is solid and only warming. The first and the fourth are the farthest apart of the rows marked solid. Between them the heater added 3.00 kJ, which is 3,000 J, and the reading rose by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T &= 246.7 - 22.0 \\
&= 224.7\ \text{°C}
\end{aligned}
$$

On a sloped part $q = nC\Delta T$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= \frac{q}{n\,\Delta T} \\
&= \frac{3{,}000}{(0.500)(224.7)} \\
&= \mathbf{26.7}
\end{aligned}
$$

in J/(mol·°C). Any two of the first five rows give the same value to three figures, because the solid's part of the record is a straight line.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Identify the metal. State which one of your three results (the melting reading, the enthalpy of fusion, the heat capacity) settles the question, and explain why each of the other two does not.

<Answer>

The metal is **lead**, and the **enthalpy of fusion** settles it.

The record gives 4.76 kJ/mol. Lead's value is 4.77 kJ/mol and cadmium's is 6.19 kJ/mol, which is about 30 percent larger. Half a mole of cadmium would have needed

$$
(0.500)(6.19) = 3.095\ \text{kJ}
$$

to melt, about 3.1 kJ, and the flat part is only 2.38 kJ long. The length of the flat part is a difference of two energies from the heater, so the thermocouple's error does not touch it.

The melting reading does not settle it. A reading may be off by 5 °C, and 324.5 °C is within 5 °C of both melting points: it is 3.0 °C below lead's 327.5 °C and 3.4 °C above cadmium's 321.1 °C.

The heat capacity does not settle it either. The record gives 26.7 J/(mol·°C), which is lead's value, but the two metals differ by only 0.7 J/(mol·°C), less than 3 percent. The value rests on a difference of two readings, 224.7 °C, and if each reading can be off by 5 °C that difference can be off by as much as 10 °C, about 4.5 percent. Even over the widest span of the record, from the first row to the fifth (302.6 °C), 10 °C is 3.3 percent. A gap of less than 3 percent cannot be trusted to a measurement that may be off by more than that.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={17} title="Three cooling records" topic="6.5" points={5}>

Pure lauric acid is a white, waxy molecular solid that melts at 43.8 °C. A student who makes lotion bars buys lauric acid, sold as pure, from three sellers. She melts a sample of each in a test tube, stands the three tubes in a rack in a room at 20 °C, and reads a thermometer in each tube every minute. Nothing heats the tubes once they are in the rack. In each tube the first solid appears at 3 min and the last liquid is gone at 7 min.

(The records are made up.)

| Time (min) | A (°C) | B (°C) | C (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 55.0 | 55.0 | 55.0 |
| 1 | 50.6 | 50.6 | 50.6 |
| 2 | 46.7 | 46.7 | 46.7 |
| 3 | 43.8 | 43.1 | 43.8 |
| 4 | 43.8 | 42.3 | 43.8 |
| 5 | 43.8 | 41.5 | 44.9 |
| 6 | 43.8 | 40.6 | 46.2 |
| 7 | 43.8 | 39.8 | 47.3 |
| 8 | 40.8 | 37.3 | 44.1 |
| 9 | 38.2 | 35.1 | 41.2 |

<Part label="a">

Identify the record that is consistent with a pure substance, and justify your choice with the data.

<Answer>

**Record A.**

A pure substance stays at one temperature while it changes phase. From 3 min to 7 min, while the liquid in tube A freezes, the reading holds at 43.8 °C, which is also the melting point of lauric acid. Before 3 min the liquid cools, and after 7 min the solid cools.

The tube is losing energy to the cooler room the whole time. During the hold, the energy that leaves is potential energy, released as the molecules settle into the solid, so the average kinetic energy, and with it the temperature, does not change.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State what record B shows about the claim that sample B is pure, and justify your answer with the data.

<Answer>

Sample B is **not a pure substance**.

It froze between 3 min and 7 min, and over that time its temperature fell from 43.1 °C to 39.8 °C:

$$
43.1 - 39.8 = 3.3\ \text{°C}
$$

A pure substance freezes at one temperature. Sample B froze over a range of 3.3 °C, so it is a mixture: lauric acid with something else in it. Its freezing also began below 43.8 °C, the value for pure lauric acid.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify the record that cannot be right, and explain why, in terms of the transfer of energy.

<Answer>

**Record C.** From 4 min to 7 min its reading rises, from 43.8 °C to 47.3 °C.

The tube is warmer than the room, so the net transfer of energy is from the tube to the room, and nothing is heating the tube. For the temperature to rise, the average kinetic energy of the molecules would have to rise, and no energy is coming in from outside to raise it.

Freezing does release energy, but that cannot do it either. The energy that freezing releases is what holds a sample at its freezing temperature while it loses energy to the room. It cannot carry the sample above that temperature, because above it the solid would not be forming at all. And the tube holds both solid and liquid from 3 min to 7 min, which for a pure substance means one temperature.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

The student repeats the run for sample A with twice the mass in the tube. Assume the tube loses energy to the room at the same rate as before. State what is the same and what is different about the flat part of the new record, and explain each.

<Answer>

The flat part is at the **same temperature, 43.8 °C**. The freezing temperature belongs to the substance, not to the amount of it.

The flat part lasts **twice as long: 8 min, not 4 min**. Freezing releases $n$ times the molar enthalpy of freezing. Twice the mass is twice the moles, so twice as much energy has to leave the tube before the last liquid is gone, and at the same rate of loss that takes twice the time.

(The sloped parts change too. Twice the mass has twice the heat capacity, so the reading falls half as fast before and after the flat part.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={18} title="A canister that cools as it empties" topic="6.4, 6.5" points={6}>

A camping stove burns butane, $\ce{C4H10}$, from a steel canister. Inside the canister the butane is a liquid under its own vapor. As the stove runs, vapor leaves through the valve and liquid vaporizes to replace it.

A canister at 22.0 °C stands on a wooden table in a room at 22.0 °C. The stove runs for a few minutes, and 12.5 g of butane vaporizes. Afterward 85.0 g of liquid butane is left in the canister.

Take these values:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Molar mass of butane | 58.12 g/mol |
| $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ of butane | 22.4 kJ/mol |
| Mass of the steel canister | 140 g |
| $c$ of the steel | 0.47 J/(g·°C) |
| $c$ of liquid butane | 2.4 J/(g·°C) |

<Part label="a">

Calculate $q$ for the 12.5 g of butane as it vaporizes, and state its sign.

<Answer>

This is a phase change, so $q = n\,\Delta H_\text{vap}$. First the moles:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{12.5}{58.12} \\
&= 0.2151\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then the energy:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (0.2151)(22.4) \\
&= 4.818\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Vaporizing separates the molecules against their attractions, so the butane absorbs this energy: $q = \mathbf{+4.82}\ \text{kJ}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Suppose all of that energy came from the steel canister and the 85.0 g of liquid butane left inside it, and none from anywhere else. Calculate the change in their temperature, and the temperature they would end at.

<Answer>

The steel and the liquid lose what the vaporizing butane gains, so for them $q = -4.818$ kJ, which is $-4{,}818$ J.

They cool together, so add their heat capacities. For the steel, mass times specific heat capacity is

$$
(140)(0.47) = 65.8\ \text{J/°C}
$$

and for the liquid

$$
(85.0)(2.4) = 204\ \text{J/°C}
$$

Together that is 65.8 + 204 = 269.8 J/°C. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T &= \frac{-4{,}818}{269.8} \\
&= -17.9\ \text{°C}
\end{aligned}
$$

The temperature would fall by about **18 °C** (the two specific heat capacities have two figures). The canister would end at

$$
22.0 - 17.9 = 4.1\ \text{°C}
$$

which is about **4 °C**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A thermometer taped to the canister reads 13.5 °C when the stove is shut off. (The masses and this reading are made up.) Compare this with your prediction. Calculate how much of the energy the steel and the liquid supplied, and explain where the rest came from.

<Answer>

The canister fell by

$$
22.0 - 13.5 = 8.5\ \text{°C}
$$

which is less than half of the 18 °C predicted. So the steel and the liquid supplied only part of the energy. The energy they lost was

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(269.8)(8.5) \\
&= 2{,}293\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is 2.293 kJ, about **2.3 kJ**. The butane still took in 4.82 kJ to vaporize: that is set by the amount that vaporized, not by where the energy came from. The rest,

$$
4.818 - 2.293 = 2.525\ \text{kJ}
$$

about **2.5 kJ**, came from the **surroundings of the canister**: the air of the room and the table.

As soon as the canister is colder than the room, there is a net transfer of energy into it from the warmer air and table. The colder the canister gets, the more comes in. So the supposition in part (b), that no energy comes from anywhere else, does not hold, and the prediction of 4 °C is a limit the canister does not reach.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={19} title="Cooling and condensing xenon: two setups to mark" topic="6.5" points={5}>

A laboratory stores xenon by condensing it in a cold trap at 1 atm. A 65.6 g sample of xenon gas at 25.0 °C is cooled to its boiling point, $-108.1$ °C, and then condensed to liquid at that temperature. Use these values:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Molar mass of xenon | 131.29 g/mol |
| $C$ of xenon gas | 20.8 J/(mol·°C) |
| $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ of xenon | 12.6 kJ/mol |

Two students each calculate $q$ for the xenon. Both find the amount correctly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{65.6}{131.29} \\
&= 0.4997\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Student 1 writes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_1 &= (0.4997)(20.8) \\
&\times (-133.1) \\
&= -1{,}383\ \text{J} = -1.38\ \text{kJ} \\
q_2 &= (65.6)(-12.6) \\
&= -827\ \text{kJ} \\
q &= -1.38 + (-827) \\
&= -828\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Student 2 writes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_\text{initial} &= 25.0 + 273.15 \\
&= 298.15\ \text{K} \\
\Delta T &= -108.1 - 298.15 \\
&= -406.25 \\
q_1 &= (0.4997)(20.8) \\
&\times (-406.25) \\
&= -4{,}222\ \text{J} = -4.22\ \text{kJ} \\
q_2 &= (0.4997)(-12.6) \\
&= -6.30\ \text{kJ} \\
q &= -4.22 + (-6.30) \\
&= -10.52\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Identify the slip in Student 1's work, and state whether it makes the size of the answer too large or too small.

<Answer>

In the second step Student 1 **multiplied grams by a molar enthalpy**. The 12.6 kJ/mol is for each mole, so it must be multiplied by 0.4997 mol, not by 65.6 g.

The slip makes the size of the answer **far too large**. A mole of xenon is 131.29 g, so counting in grams makes the condensing step about 131 times too big: 827 kJ where it should be 6.30 kJ.

Student 1's first step is right, and so are the signs and the change from joules to kilojoules.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Student 2's work has exactly one slip. Identify it, and explain why the answer would have been right if Student 2 had changed both temperatures to kelvins.

<Answer>

**The slip.** Student 2 changed the starting temperature to kelvins and left the boiling point in degrees Celsius, then subtracted one from the other. The two temperatures in a $\Delta T$ must be on the same scale. With both in degrees Celsius:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T &= -108.1 - 25.0 \\
&= -133.1\ \text{°C}
\end{aligned}
$$

Student 2's $-406.25$ is about three times that size, so the cooling step comes out as $-4.22$ kJ where it should be $-1.38$ kJ, and the total is too large in size.

**Both in kelvins.** Changing to kelvins adds the same 273.15 to each temperature, and a number added to both drops out of a difference:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_\text{final} &= -108.1 + 273.15 \\
&= 165.05\ \text{K} \\
\Delta T &= 165.05 - 298.15 \\
&= -133.10\ \text{K}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the same size as the change in degrees Celsius, so $C$ in J/(mol·°C) can be used with it as it stands. Student 2 added 273.15 to one temperature only, so it stayed in the difference:

$$
-133.10 - 273.15 = -406.25
$$

Everything else in Student 2's work is right: the moles in both steps, both signs, and the change from joules to kilojoules before adding.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate $q$ for the xenon, with its sign.

<Answer>

There are two steps: the gas cools, and then it condenses.

Cooling the gas from 25.0 °C to $-108.1$ °C, with $q = nC\Delta T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_1 &= (0.4997)(20.8) \\
&\times (-133.1) \\
&= -1{,}383\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

\begin{aligned}

&= -6.30\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Add, in kilojoules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= -1.383 + (-6.30) \\
&= \mathbf{-7.68}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

The xenon releases 7.68 kJ, and the cold trap must carry that energy away. Condensing accounts for about four fifths of it, though the temperature does not change during that step.

</Answer>
