Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through the questions on bonds: the expression is *broken minus formed*, the one rule in this unit that puts the reactants first.

You will need these relationships.

From bond enthalpies, an estimate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta H \approx \\
&\quad \text{(sum of bond enthalpies} \\
&\quad\ \ \text{of bonds broken)} \\
&\quad - \text{(sum of bond enthalpies} \\
&\quad\ \ \text{of bonds formed)}
\end{aligned}
$$

From standard enthalpies of formation, where $n$ is each substance's coefficient in the balanced equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ ={} & \textstyle\sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) \\
& - \textstyle\sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})
\end{aligned}
$$

An element in its standard state has $\Delta H_f^\circ = 0$.

Use these values. Tables round a little differently from one to the next, so the values a question gives are the ones to use, here and on the exam.

Bond enthalpies, in kJ/mol:

| Bond | kJ/mol | Bond | kJ/mol |
|---|---|---|---|
| H–H | 432 | Cl–Cl | 239 |
| H–Cl | 427 | C–H | 413 |
| C–C | 347 | O–H | 467 |
| O=O | 495 | C=O (in $\ce{CO2}$) | 799 |
| C=C | 614 | N≡N | 941 |
| N–H | 391 | | |

Standard enthalpies of formation, $\Delta H_f^\circ$, in kJ/mol. Every substance in this table is a gas, apart from the liquid water marked (l).

| Substance | $\Delta H_f^\circ$ | Substance | $\Delta H_f^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | $-393.5$ | $\ce{CO}$ | $-110.5$ |
| $\ce{H2O(l)}$ | $-286$ | $\ce{H2O(g)}$ | $-242$ |
| $\ce{CH4}$ | $-75$ | $\ce{NH3}$ | $-46$ |
| $\ce{C2H4}$ | $+52$ | $\ce{C2H6}$ | $-84.7$ |

The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 J/(g·°C).

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Breaking and forming bonds" topic="6.7">

Bond enthalpy basics.

<Part label="a">

Breaking a bond is: <Blank />

<Answer>

endothermic

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Forming a bond is: <Blank />

<Answer>

exothermic

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The expression is broken minus formed. Why does that ordering look backward next to every other rule in this unit?

<Answer>

Every other difference in this unit is end minus start: products minus reactants, final minus initial. Bonds broken belong to the *reactant* side, so the order appears inverted. It must be, because a bond enthalpy is the energy to take something apart: the bonds broken are counted as energy in, and the bonds formed as energy out.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Hydrogen and chlorine" topic="6.7">

Estimate $\Delta H$ for this reaction.

$$
\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}
$$

<Part label="a">

Total energy of bonds broken: <Blank />

<Answer>

671 kJ

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Total energy of bonds formed: <Blank />

<Answer>

854 kJ

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$\Delta H$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$-183\ \text{kJ}$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Does the sign make sense?

<Answer>

Yes. More energy was released forming the two H–Cl bonds than was spent breaking H–H and Cl–Cl, so the reaction is exothermic and $\Delta H$ must come out negative.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Burning methane, from bond enthalpies" topic="6.7">

Estimate $\Delta H$ for this reaction from bond enthalpies.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Answer>

Broken:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&4(413) + 2(495) \\
&= 1{,}652 + 990 \\
&= 2{,}642\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Formed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(799) + 4(467) \\
&= 1{,}598 + 1{,}868 \\
&= 3{,}466\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &\approx 2{,}642 - 3{,}466 \\
&= \mathbf{-824}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Burning methane, from enthalpies of formation" topic="6.8">

Now calculate $\Delta H^\circ$ for that same reaction, methane burning to carbon dioxide and water vapor, from standard enthalpies of formation.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta H^\circ \\
&= [-393.5 + 2(-242)] \\
&\quad - [-75 + 0] \\
&= -877.5 + 75 \\
&= \mathbf{-802.5}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Two routes, two answers" topic="6.7, 6.8">

For burning methane, the two routes gave $-824\ \text{kJ}$ and $-802.5\ \text{kJ}$.

<Part label="a">

Which is the more reliable, and why?

<Answer>

The value from standard enthalpies of formation, $-802.5\ \text{kJ}$. Those are measured for the actual substances, whereas most bond enthalpies are averages over many different molecules and ignore the specific environment a bond sits in.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

When should you use bond enthalpies at all?

<Answer>

When standard enthalpies of formation are not available. The result should then be described as an estimate rather than an exact value.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Is a discrepancy of about 20 kJ evidence that one calculation is wrong?

<Answer>

No. A difference of that size is what a table of bond enthalpies can produce: the C–H and O–H values are averages over many molecules, and tables round a little differently. Both calculations can be correct as performed.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="Elements, and making ammonia" topic="6.7, 6.8">

Enthalpies of formation.

<Part label="a">

$\Delta H_f^\circ$ of $\ce{O2(g)}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\Delta H_f^\circ$ of $\ce{C(s, graphite)}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

0 kJ/mol

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why are these zero by definition?

<Answer>

A standard enthalpy of formation is the change on forming a substance from its elements in their standard states. For an element already in that state there is nothing to form, so the change is zero.

</Answer>

<Question number={8} title="Methane in a calorimeter" topic="6.4, 6.6, 6.8" points={10}>

Methane is burned in a calorimeter. A 0.0400 mol sample is completely combusted in 500.0 g of water, whose temperature rises from 21.00 °C to 38.05 °C. Take the energy the water gains as equal in size to the energy the reaction releases, and treat that energy as a change in enthalpy.

<Part label="a">

Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of methane, with liquid water as a product.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the heat absorbed by the water.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (500.0)(4.18)(17.05) \\
&= 35{,}634.5\ \text{J} \\
&= \mathbf{35.6}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the experimental $\Delta H$ of combustion in kJ/mol.

<Answer>

The water gained the energy, so the reaction released it. Carry the energy from part (b) with one more figure than its rounded answer, 35.63 kJ. Dividing the rounded 35.6 kJ gives $-890\ \text{kJ/mol}$ instead.

$$
q_{\text{rxn}} = -35.63\ \text{kJ}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= \frac{-35.63}{0.0400} \\
&= \mathbf{-891}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the accepted value from standard enthalpies of formation.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta H^\circ \\
&= [-393.5 + 2(-286)] \\
&\quad - [-75] \\
&= -965.5 + 75 \\
&= \mathbf{-890.5}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

Comment on the agreement, and state one reason a real calorimetry experiment usually gives a value whose size is too *small*.

<Answer>

The two agree closely, within about 1 kJ/mol, and here the measured size is very slightly the larger, so these data show no loss. In a real experiment the measured size is usually low because some of the energy released escapes to the calorimeter walls and the surroundings rather than going into the water, so the temperature rise, and therefore the calculated energy, under-reports the reaction.

</Answer>

</Part>
