This is the second of two sittings that together test all of Unit 6. It covers topics 6.6 to 6.9: the enthalpy of a reaction, bond enthalpies, enthalpies of formation and Hess's law. Session 1 covers systems and signs, energy diagrams, heat transfer, calorimetry and phase changes.

There are 18 questions worth 90 points. Give yourself about 95 minutes, and use a calculator, the periodic table and the equation sheet, as on the AP exam. Every question gives the values it needs. Try each part on paper before you open its answer, then score yourself with the rubric.

## Part E: Enthalpy of reaction

Five questions on the enthalpy change that belongs to an equation as written. You will compare fuels on three different bases, read a table of temperature rises backward to a mole ratio, and run the calorimeter calculation in the directions you have not practiced: from an enthalpy change to a mass of water, from an energy to a mass, and from a temperature rise to the amount that reacted.

<Question number={1} title="Three stove fuels, three ways to compare" topic="6.6" points={6}>

Three fuels are sold for camping stoves: methanol, a liquid alcohol; hexamine, a white solid pressed into tablets; and hexane, one of the hydrocarbons in the liquid fuel sold as white gas. Each equation below is given with its enthalpy change as written. Use these values.

Methanol:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2CH3OH(l) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -1{,}452\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

Hexamine:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C6H12N4(s) + 9O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l)} \\
&\quad +\ \ce{2N2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -4{,}200\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

Hexane:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2C6H14(l) + 19O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{12CO2(g) + 14H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -8{,}326\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

| Fuel | Molar mass (g/mol) |
|---|---|
| Methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$ | 32.04 |
| Hexamine, $\ce{C6H12N4}$ | 140.20 |
| Hexane, $\ce{C6H14}$ | 86.17 |

<Part label="a">

Determine the energy released, in kJ, when one mole of each fuel burns.

<Answer>

Each value of $\Delta H$ goes with its equation as written, so divide it by the number of moles of fuel in that equation. An energy released is a size, so it carries no sign.

The methanol equation is written for 2 mol of methanol:

$$
\frac{1{,}452}{2} = \mathbf{726.0}\ \text{kJ}
$$

The hexamine equation is written for 1 mol of hexamine, so the value stands as it is: **4,200 kJ**.

The hexane equation is written for 2 mol of hexane:

$$
\frac{8{,}326}{2} = \mathbf{4{,}163}\ \text{kJ}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the energy released, in kJ, when one gram of each fuel burns.

<Answer>

One mole of a fuel has a mass equal to its molar mass. So divide the energy for one mole of fuel by the molar mass: kJ/mol over g/mol leaves kJ/g.

Methanol:

$$
\frac{726.0}{32.04} = \mathbf{22.7}\ \text{kJ/g}
$$

Hexamine:

$$
\frac{4{,}200}{140.20} = \mathbf{30.0}\ \text{kJ/g}
$$

Hexane:

$$
\frac{4{,}163}{86.17} = \mathbf{48.3}\ \text{kJ/g}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Rank the three fuels from the most energy released to the least on each basis: per mole of reaction as written, per mole of fuel, and per gram of fuel.

<Answer>

**Per mole of reaction as written:** hexane (8,326 kJ), then hexamine (4,200 kJ), then methanol (1,452 kJ).

**Per mole of fuel:** hexamine (4,200 kJ), then hexane (4,163 kJ), then methanol (726.0 kJ).

**Per gram of fuel:** hexane (48.3 kJ), then hexamine (30.0 kJ), then methanol (22.7 kJ).

Methanol is last every time. Hexane and hexamine change places.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Per mole of fuel, hexamine and hexane are nearly tied. Per mole of reaction and per gram, hexane is far ahead. Explain both differences.

<Answer>

**Per mole of reaction.** Hexane's equation is written for 2 mol of fuel and hexamine's for 1 mol. So hexane's value of $\Delta H$ counts twice as much fuel. The lead comes from how the equation is written, not from the fuel. Written for 1 mol of hexane, the same reaction would have half the value, and the two fuels would be nearly tied again.

**Per gram.** A mole of hexamine has a mass of 140.20 g and a mole of hexane only 86.17 g. The two moles release nearly the same energy (4,200 kJ and 4,163 kJ), but hexane's is spread over far fewer grams. Put the other way: one gram of hexane holds more moles of fuel than one gram of hexamine, about 1.6 times as many, so it releases about 1.6 times the energy.

$$
\frac{140.20}{86.17} = 1.63
$$

For a hiker, who carries fuel by mass, the comparison per gram is the one that matters.

</Answer>

</Part>

<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for dividing an energy for one mole of fuel by that fuel's molar mass; 1 point for all three values: 22.7, 30.0 and 48.3 kJ/g (22.6 to 22.7, 29.9 to 30.0, and 48.3). Values that follow correctly from a wrong answer to (a) earn both points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for all three rankings: hexane, hexamine, methanol (per mole of reaction); hexamine, hexane, methanol (per mole of fuel); hexane, hexamine, methanol (per gram). Rankings that agree with the student's own values in (a) and (b) earn the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for saying that hexane's equation is written for 2 mol of fuel and hexamine's for 1 mol, so the values as written do not compare equal amounts of fuel; 1 point for saying that hexane has the smaller molar mass, so a gram of it holds more moles (or the same energy per mole is spread over fewer grams). "Hexane is a better fuel" with no reference to the equation or the molar mass earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Five cups of bleach and sulfite" topic="6.6" points={6}>

In solution, hypochlorite ions, $\ce{OCl-}$ (the active ingredient of bleach), react with sulfite ions, $\ce{SO3^2-}$. A student wants to find the mole ratio in which the two ions react, and the enthalpy change of the reaction.

The student has 0.200 M sodium hypochlorite solution and 0.200 M sodium sulfite solution, both at the same temperature. In each of five foam cups the student mixes the two in a different proportion, always 50.0 mL in all, and records the rise in temperature. (The data are for illustration.)

| Cup | Hypochlorite (mL) | Sulfite (mL) | Rise (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10.0 | 40.0 | 3.20 |
| 2 | 20.0 | 30.0 | 6.39 |
| 3 | 25.0 | 25.0 | 7.99 |
| 4 | 30.0 | 20.0 | 6.39 |
| 5 | 40.0 | 10.0 | 3.20 |

Take the mass of each mixture as 50.0 g and its specific heat capacity as 4.18 J/(g·°C).

<Part label="a">

Identify the cup in which neither reactant is left over, and determine the mole ratio in which hypochlorite ions and sulfite ions react. Justify both answers with the data.

<Answer>

**Cup 3. The ions react in a 1 : 1 mole ratio.**

Every cup holds the same mass of mixture, so a larger rise means more energy released, and more energy means more moles of reaction. Cup 3 has the largest rise, so the most reaction took place there.

To see that nothing is left over in cup 3, divide each rise by the smaller of the two volumes in its cup:

| Cup | Smaller volume (mL) | Rise per mL (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10.0, hypochlorite | 0.32 |
| 2 | 20.0, hypochlorite | 0.32 |
| 3 | 25.0, both | 0.32 |
| 4 | 20.0, sulfite | 0.32 |
| 5 | 10.0, sulfite | 0.32 |

In cups 1 and 2 the rise goes up in step with the hypochlorite, so the hypochlorite is the reactant that runs out and sulfite is left over. In cups 4 and 5 the rise goes in step with the sulfite, so the sulfite runs out and hypochlorite is left over. Cup 3 fits both patterns: all of its hypochlorite and all of its sulfite reacted.

Cup 3 holds equal volumes of two solutions of the same concentration, so it holds equal amounts of the two ions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0250)(0.200) \\
&= 0.00500\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since 0.00500 mol of one used up exactly 0.00500 mol of the other, the ratio is 1 : 1.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Take one mole of reaction to use up one mole of hypochlorite ions. Use the data for the cup you chose in part (a) to calculate $\Delta H$, in $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$. Give its sign.

<Answer>

The products are chloride ions and sulfate ions. With the 1 : 1 ratio, the equation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{OCl-(aq) + SO3^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cl-(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The energy.** For the mixture in cup 3, in joules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{mixture} &= (50.0)(4.18)(7.99) \\
&= 1{,}670\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mixture gained this energy, so the reaction released it. Its $q$ has the same size and the opposite sign:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{reaction} &= -1{,}670\ \text{J} \\
&= -1.670\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The amount.** Cup 3 used up 0.00500 mol of hypochlorite ions (part a), so 0.00500 mol of reaction took place.

**Divide.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= \frac{-1.670}{0.00500} \\
&= \mathbf{-334}\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sign is negative: the mixture warmed, so the reaction is exothermic.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The student fills a sixth cup with 15.0 mL of the hypochlorite solution and 35.0 mL of the sulfite solution. Predict the rise in temperature.

<Answer>

There is less hypochlorite than sulfite, and they react 1 : 1, so the hypochlorite runs out. The mass of the mixture is the same as in every other cup, so the rise is in proportion to the hypochlorite used up. Cup 3 used up 25.0 mL of it and rose 7.99 °C:

$$
\frac{15.0}{25.0} \times 7.99 = \mathbf{4.79}\ \text{°C}
$$

The longer route gives the same answer. The cup holds this many moles of hypochlorite ions, which is also the moles of reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0150)(0.200) \\
&= 0.00300\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

With the size of $\Delta H$ from part (b), the energy released is:

$$
(0.00300)(334) = 1.002\ \text{kJ}
$$

That is 1,002 J, gained by the mixture:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T &= \frac{1{,}002}{(50.0)(4.18)} \\
&= 4.79\ \text{°C}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Cup 5 holds the most hypochlorite of the five cups, 40.0 mL, and its rise, 3.20 °C, is as small as any in the table. A student says, "So in cup 5 the reaction released less energy per mole of reaction than in cup 3." Use the table to judge the student's claim.

<Answer>

**The claim is wrong. Cup 5 released less energy because less reaction took place there, not because each mole of reaction released less.**

In cup 5 the sulfite runs out (part a), so the 10.0 mL of sulfite sets the moles of reaction, and the 40.0 mL of hypochlorite does not:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0100)(0.200) \\
&= 0.00200\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is two fifths of the 0.00500 mol in cup 3. The two cups hold the same mass of mixture, so their rises compare their energies, and the rise in cup 5 is two fifths of the rise in cup 3 as well:

$$
\frac{3.20}{7.99} = 0.40
$$

$$
\frac{0.00200}{0.00500} = 0.40
$$

Two fifths of the energy from two fifths of the reaction: the energy per mole of reaction is the same in both cups.

The full calculation for cup 5 agrees. The mixture gained:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(50.0)(4.18)(3.20) \\
&= 668.8\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $q$ for the reaction is $-0.6688$ kJ, and:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &= \frac{-0.6688}{0.00200} \\
&= -334\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the value cup 3 gave in part (b). Most of the hypochlorite in cup 5 is left over. What is left over does not react, so it releases nothing.

</Answer>

</Part>

A ratio with no justification, or one justified only by balancing an equation and not by the data, does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the energy from cup 3 with the sign change (1,670 J gained by the mixture, so the reaction's $q$ is negative); 1 point for dividing by 0.00500 mol to reach $-334$ kJ/mol of reaction ($-333$ to $-335$). <RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for rejecting the claim with the table: cup 5 has fewer moles of reaction (0.00200 mol against 0.00500 mol, because the sulfite runs out), and its rise or its energy is smaller in the same proportion, so the energy per mole of reaction is the same. Calculating $-334$ kJ/mol of reaction from cup 5 ($-333$ to $-335$) and comparing it with part (b) also earns the point. </Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Water for a tank of potassium hydroxide" topic="6.6" points={6}>

A plant that makes alkaline batteries prepares its electrolyte by dissolving potassium hydroxide pellets in water in an open steel tank. Dissolving the pellets releases energy:

$$
\ce{KOH(s) -> K+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -57.6\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

One batch uses 2.00 kg of pellets. The water and the pellets start at 18.0 °C, and the solution must not go above 40.0 °C. The molar mass of KOH is 56.11 g/mol. Take the specific heat capacity of the solution as 4.18 J/(g·°C), and take the mass that warms as the mass of the water plus the mass of the pellets.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the energy released, in kJ, when 2.00 kg of potassium hydroxide dissolves.

<Answer>

Grams to moles first. One mole of reaction dissolves one mole of KOH, so the moles of KOH are the moles of reaction:

$$
\frac{2{,}000}{56.11} = 35.64\ \text{mol}
$$

Then multiply by $\Delta H$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (35.64)(-57.6) \\
&= -2{,}053\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

The minus sign says the energy leaves the dissolving potassium hydroxide. The energy released is **2,053 kJ**, or $2.05 \times 10^3$ kJ to three significant figures.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Assume that all of this energy stays in the solution. Calculate the least mass of water, in kg, that keeps the solution from going above 40.0 °C.

<Answer>

The solution gains what the dissolving releases, so for the solution $q$ is positive: 2,053 kJ, which is 2,053,000 J.

The largest rise allowed is final minus initial: 40.0 − 18.0 = 22.0 °C.

Solve $q = mc\Delta T$ for the mass that warms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= \frac{q}{c\,\Delta T} \\
&= \frac{2{,}053{,}000}{(4.18)(22.0)} \\
&= 22{,}325\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 22.325 kg of solution, and 2.00 kg of it is the pellets. The rest is water:

$$
22.325 - 2.00 = \mathbf{20.3}\ \text{kg}
$$

This is the least mass. With less water the same energy would be spread through a smaller mass, and the rise would be more than 22.0 °C.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

In the real tank, with the mass of water from part (b), the solution stays below 40.0 °C. Identify the assumption in part (b) that explains this.

<Answer>

The assumption that **all of the energy stays in the solution**.

In the real tank some of the energy warms the steel of the tank, and some passes to the air above the open surface. The solution keeps less than 2,053 kJ, so its temperature rises by less than 22.0 °C. The answer to part (b) is on the safe side.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A second batch dissolves twice the pellets, 4.00 kg, in the mass of water found in part (b), again from 18.0 °C. Take $\Delta H$ per mole as unchanged, and assume again that all of the energy stays in the solution. Explain, without calculating, why the rise is more than 22.0 °C but less than twice 22.0 °C. Then calculate the final temperature.

<Answer>

**More than 22.0 °C.** Twice the pellets is twice the moles that dissolve, so twice the energy is released. The water is the same, so most of the mass that warms is the same. Much more energy in nearly the same mass gives a larger rise, and the solution goes above the 40.0 °C limit.

**Less than twice 22.0 °C.** The mass that warms is the water plus the pellets, and the extra 2.00 kg of pellets is part of it. Twice the energy is spread through a little more mass than before, so the rise is a little less than doubled.

**The final temperature.** The energy is twice that of part (a):

$$
2(2{,}053) = 4{,}106\ \text{kJ}
$$

That is 4,106,000 J. The mass that warms, in kilograms:

$$
20.325 + 4.00 = 24.325
$$

That is 24,325 g.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T &= \frac{4{,}106{,}000}{(24{,}325)(4.18)} \\
&= 40.4\ \text{°C}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
18.0 + 40.4 = \mathbf{58.4}\ \text{°C}
$$

The rise, 40.4 °C, lies between 22.0 °C and 44.0 °C, as predicted. The energy released goes up in step with the amount that dissolves. The rise does not, because the mass that shares the energy changed too.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for the energy released, 2,053 kJ (2,050 to 2,055), from the moles of KOH times 57.6. A minus sign on an energy released is not penalized. Multiplying 2,000 g by 57.6 with no change to moles earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for solving $q = mc\Delta T$ for the mass with a rise of 22.0 °C and the energy in joules (about 22.3 kg of solution); 1 point for subtracting the 2.00 kg of pellets to reach 20.3 kg of water (20.2 to 20.4). An answer left at 22.3 kg earns 1 point. Kilojoules used as joules, giving 22.3 g, earns at most 1 point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for the assumption that all the energy stays in the solution, with what really happens: some warms the tank or passes to the air, so the solution gains less. "Human error" or "the measurements are not exact" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for both reasons: twice the pellets releases twice the energy into nearly the same mass, so the rise is larger; the pellets add to the mass that warms, so the rise is less than doubled. 1 point for the final temperature, 58.4 °C (58.3 to 58.5), from 4,106 kJ and 24.3 kg of solution. Scaling the first rise, 22.0 times 2 times 22.325 over 24.325, is an equally sound route. A final temperature of 62.0 °C, from doubling the rise, or an answer left at the rise of 40.4 °C, earns at most 1 point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Sucrose and a food label" topic="6.6" points={4}>

Sucrose, $\ce{C12H22O11}$, is table sugar. When it is oxidized completely, the products are carbon dioxide and water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C12H22O11(s) + 12O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{12CO2(g) + 11H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -5{,}640\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

The body oxidizes sucrose to the same products in many small steps. Take the energy released in the body to be the same as for this equation. The molar mass of sucrose is 342.30 g/mol.

<Part label="a">

An energy gel for runners is to supply 425 kJ, all of it from sucrose. Calculate the mass of sucrose the gel needs.

<Answer>

This is the usual route run backward: from kilojoules, to moles of reaction, to moles of sucrose, to grams.

Each mole of reaction releases 5,640 kJ, so 425 kJ takes:

$$
\frac{425}{5{,}640} = 0.07535\ \text{mol}
$$

The equation uses 1 mol of sucrose each time it runs, so this is also the moles of sucrose. Then moles to grams:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= (0.07535)(342.30) \\
&= \mathbf{25.8}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is sensible: 425 kJ is a small part of 5,640 kJ, so the mass is a small part of the 342.30 g in a mole.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the energy released per gram of sucrose, in kJ/g.

<Answer>

One mole of sucrose releases 5,640 kJ and has a mass of 342.30 g:

$$
\frac{5{,}640}{342.30} = \mathbf{16.5}\ \text{kJ/g}
$$

The same value comes from part (a): 425 kJ from 25.8 g is also 16.5 kJ/g.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Food labels in many countries count 17 kJ for each gram of carbohydrate, whichever carbohydrate it is. A student says, "So the label is wrong for sucrose." Use your answers to judge the student's claim.

<Answer>

The student is right that the two numbers differ, but "wrong" overstates it. **The label's figure is close to the value for sucrose, and a little high.**

The label's 17 kJ/g is 0.5 kJ/g above the 16.5 kJ/g calculated for sucrose:

$$
\frac{17 - 16.5}{16.5} \times 100\% \approx 3\%
$$

For the gel in part (a), the label would count:

$$
(25.8)(17) = 439\ \text{kJ}
$$

That is 14 kJ more than the 425 kJ calculated.

A label uses one round figure, given to two significant figures, for every carbohydrate. One figure cannot match each carbohydrate exactly. For sucrose it is off by about 3 percent.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the moles of reaction from the energy (425 divided by 5,640, about 0.0754 mol); 1 point for the mass, 25.8 g (25.7 to 25.8). Finding 16.5 kJ/g first and dividing 425 by it is an equally sound route and earns both points. Multiplying 425 by the molar mass, or by 5,640, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for 16.5 kJ/g (16.4 to 16.5), from 5,640 over 342.30 or from 425 kJ over the mass found in (a).</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for a judgment that rests on a comparison of numbers: the label's 17 kJ/g is about 0.5 kJ/g, or about 3 percent, above the value for sucrose (or 439 kJ against 425 kJ), so it is close but a little high. Either verdict earns the point if it rests on this comparison and gives the size of the difference. "The label is wrong" or "the label is right" with no comparison of the two values earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={5} title="How much magnesium is in the turnings" topic="6.6" points={5}>

A machine shop sweeps up a mix of magnesium turnings and copper turnings, and a recycler wants to know the mass percent of magnesium in it. Copper does not react with hydrochloric acid. Magnesium does, and hydrogen bubbles out of the mixture:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Mg(s) + 2H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + H2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -467\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

A technician drops 0.500 g of the mixed turnings into 150.0 mL of hydrochloric acid in an open foam cup and stirs until the bubbling stops. The acid is in excess. The temperature rises from 21.40 °C to 30.30 °C. Take the density of the acid as 1.00 g/mL, so that the mixture that warms has a mass of 150.5 g (the acid and the turnings), and take its specific heat capacity as 4.18 J/(g·°C). The molar mass of magnesium is 24.30 g/mol.

<Part label="a">

Calculate $q$ for the reaction in the cup, in kJ. Give its sign.

<Answer>

The temperature change is final minus initial: 30.30 − 21.40 = 8.90 °C. For the mixture, in joules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{mixture} &= (150.5)(4.18)(8.90) \\
&= 5{,}599\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mixture gained this energy, so the reaction released it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_\text{reaction} &= -5{,}599\ \text{J} \\
&= \mathbf{-5.599}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

To three significant figures this is $-5.60$ kJ. Keep the extra figure for the next part.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the moles of magnesium that reacted, and the mass percent of magnesium in the turnings.

<Answer>

Here $\Delta H$ is known and the amount is not, so divide the energy by $\Delta H$:

\begin{aligned}
n_\text{rxn} &= \frac{-5.599}{-467} \\
&= 0.01199\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.01199)(24.30) \\
&= 0.2914\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

As a percent of the 0.500 g of turnings:

$$
\frac{0.2914}{0.500} \times 100\% = \mathbf{58.3\%}
$$

The rest of the sample, a little over 0.2 g, is the copper, which is still in the cup as metal.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Suppose some energy escapes from the cup before the highest temperature is read. State whether the calculated mass percent is then too high or too low, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

**Too low.**

If energy escapes, the mixture's temperature rises by less than it should. A smaller $\Delta T$ gives a smaller $q$, a smaller $q$ divided by the same $\Delta H$ gives fewer moles of reaction, and so a smaller mass of magnesium and a smaller percent.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

The label on the acid bottle says only "about 1 M". Explain why not knowing the concentration exactly does not spoil the result.

At about 1 M, 150.0 mL of acid holds about 0.150 mol, which is more than enough.

</Answer>

## Part F: Bond enthalpies

<Question number={6} title="A bond enthalpy found backward" topic="6.7" points={5}>

A molecule of hydrazine, $\ce{N2H4}$, is $\ce{H2N-NH2}$: two nitrogen atoms joined by a single bond, each holding two hydrogen atoms. A student's table of bond enthalpies has no value for the N–N single bond, and the student sets out to find one from measured data.

Use these bond enthalpies, in kJ/mol:

| Bond | kJ/mol |
|---|---|
| N≡N | 941 |
| H–H | 436 |
| N–H | 391 |
| O=O | 495 |
| O–H | 463 |

Three measured values are on offer, all at 25 °C. Nitrogen and hydrogen are gases in their standard states.

- **Datum 1.** The standard enthalpy of formation of hydrazine gas, $\ce{N2H4(g)}$, is $+95.4\ \text{kJ/mol}$.
- **Datum 2.** The standard enthalpy of formation of liquid hydrazine, $\ce{N2H4(l)}$, is $+50.6\ \text{kJ/mol}$.
- **Datum 3.** For hydrazine gas burning to nitrogen and steam, $\Delta H^\circ = -579.0\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{N2H4(g) + O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{N2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Use data 1 and 2 to calculate $\Delta H^\circ$ for one mole of liquid hydrazine turning into hydrazine gas. Then state what a count of bonds broken and bonds formed gives for the same change. Use your two results to identify the one datum of the three that cannot be used with a table of bond enthalpies.

<Answer>

The change is:

$$
\ce{N2H4(l) -> N2H4(g)}
$$

Products minus reactants, with the two formation values:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ &= 95.4 - 50.6 \\
&= \mathbf{+44.8}\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now count bonds. The molecule is the same before and after: 1 N–N and 4 N–H on the left, 1 N–N and 4 N–H on the right. No bond is broken and none is formed, so a bond count gives **zero** for this change.

The two results do not agree. The measurement says the change takes in 44.8 kJ, and the table has nothing to put against it. That energy goes to pulling the molecules of the liquid away from one another, and no entry in the table stands for it. So the table can describe hydrazine only where its molecules are already apart, in the gas.

**Datum 2**, the value for liquid hydrazine, cannot be used. A bond count that used it would put all 44.8 kJ into the N–N bond and make it look that much stronger than it is.

Data 1 and 3 can be used: every substance in them is a gas.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Use datum 1 to calculate the N–N bond enthalpy in hydrazine.

<Answer>

Datum 1 belongs to the formation equation, which makes one mole of hydrazine gas from its elements:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{N2(g) + 2H2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{N2H4(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Call the N–N bond enthalpy $x$.

Bonds broken: 1 N≡N and 2 H–H.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&941 + 2(436) \\
&= 941 + 872 \\
&= 1{,}813\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Bonds formed: 1 N–N and 4 N–H.

$$
x + 4(391) = x + 1{,}564
$$

Set broken minus formed equal to the measured value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1{,}813 - x - 1{,}564 = \\
&\quad 95.4 \\
&249 - x = 95.4 \\
&x = 249 - 95.4 \\
&\quad = 153.6
\end{aligned}
$$

The other bond values are whole numbers, so the result is good to a whole number: the N–N bond enthalpy is about $\mathbf{154}\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Use datum 3 to calculate the N–N bond enthalpy in hydrazine.

<Answer>

Bonds broken: 1 N–N and 4 N–H in hydrazine, and 1 O=O.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&x + 4(391) + 495 \\
&= x + 1{,}564 + 495 \\
&= x + 2{,}059
\end{aligned}
$$

Bonds formed: 1 N≡N, and 4 O–H, because each of the 2 water molecules has two O–H bonds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&941 + 4(463) \\
&= 941 + 1{,}852 \\
&= 2{,}793\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Set broken minus formed equal to the measured value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&x + 2{,}059 - 2{,}793 = \\
&\quad -579.0 \\
&x - 734 = -579.0 \\
&x = 734 - 579.0 \\
&\quad = 155.0
\end{aligned}
$$

From datum 3 the N–N bond enthalpy is about $\mathbf{155}\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

The results of parts (b) and (c) differ by about 1 kJ/mol, and neither calculation has a slip in it. Explain why the two do not come out exactly the same.

<Answer>

Each calculation leans on the other values in the table, and none of those is exact. Some are **averages**: the N–H value, 391, and the O–H value, 463, each stand for a bond that differs a little from compound to compound. Every value is also rounded to a whole number, and tables differ from one another by a few kJ/mol.

Whatever those values miss by ends up in $x$, because $x$ is the only unknown. Both calculations use 4 N–H and an N≡N, so whatever those two values miss by moves both results by the same amount. The other bonds are different: part (b) also uses 2 H–H, and part (c) also uses 4 O–H and an O=O. Those values carry different small misses, so the two values of $x$ differ a little. A gap of about 1 kJ between totals of about 2,000 to 3,000 kJ is as close as a table of bond enthalpies can be expected to come.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={7} title="An estimate tested against combustion data" topic="6.7, 6.9" points={5}>

|---|---|

| Cyclopropane | $-2{,}091.3$ |

Use the bond enthalpies to estimate $\Delta H$ for turning propene into cyclopropane:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{propene(g)} \;\ce{->} \\
&\quad \text{cyclopropane(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Answer>

Bonds broken, every bond in propene: 1 C=C, 1 C–C and 6 C–H.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&614 + 347 + 6(413) \\
&= 614 + 347 + 2{,}478 \\
&= 3{,}439\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}

Bonds formed, every bond in cyclopropane: 3 C–C and 6 C–H.

$$
\begin{aligned}

&= 3{,}519\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H &\approx 3{,}439 - 3{,}519 \\
&= \mathbf{-80}\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}

The estimate says that energy is released.

$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Use the combustion data to calculate $\Delta H^\circ$ for the same change.

<Answer>

$$
\Delta H^\circ = -2{,}058.0\ \text{kJ}
$$

Then run the burning of cyclopropane backward, from the products to cyclopropane and oxygen. Reversing an equation changes the sign:

$$
\Delta H^\circ = +2{,}091.3\ \text{kJ}
$$

The carbon dioxide, the water and the oxygen cancel, and propene turning into cyclopropane is left. Add the two values:

$$
\begin{aligned}

&= \mathbf{+33.3}\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Energy is absorbed. Another way to see it: cyclopropane releases 33.3 kJ more than propene on the way to the same products, so a mole of cyclopropane is 33.3 kJ higher in energy than a mole of propene.

</Answer>

</Part>

A student reads the result of part (a) and concludes that turning propene into cyclopropane releases energy.

Evaluate the student's conclusion. Then take the table's values as right for propene and for the C–H bonds of cyclopropane, and determine whether the three C–C bonds of the ring are stronger or weaker than the table's value, and by about how much each. Justify your answer with the results of parts (a) and (b).

<Answer>

$$

or about 113 kJ, which is enough to give the wrong sign.

$$
3(347) = 1{,}041\ \text{kJ}
$$

The count put cyclopropane 80 kJ below propene, and the measurements put it 33.3 kJ above. So forming the ring releases 113.3 kJ less than the count supposed, and that is shared among three bonds:

\frac{113.3}{3} = 37.8\ \text{kJ}

The C–C bonds of the ring are **weaker** than the table's value, by about **38 kJ/mol** each. That makes each about

or about 309 kJ/mol. The ring holds its carbon atoms at angles of 60°, which the compounds behind the table's average do not, so one value cannot serve for both.

</Answer>

\begin{aligned}
&\ce{POCl3(l) ->} \\

&\Delta H^\circ = +277.4\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}

Add them. $\ce{POCl3(l)}$ cancels and so does ½ $\ce{O2}$. What is left is laboratory A's equation:

$$
\ce{P(s) + 3/2 Cl2(g) -> PCl3(l)}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ &= -597.1 + 277.4 \\
&= -319.7\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the value laboratory A reported. The two new values **confirm laboratory A's report**.
