## Part E: The atom and its configuration

Five questions on the electrons of atoms and ions. You will name species from their electrons, find the ions that share a number of electrons and estimate a radius, judge a configuration given for an ion, compare two forces with Coulomb's law, and count the electrons in two shells of one atom.

<Question number={20} title="Naming a species from its electrons" topic="1.5" points={5}>

Each row of the table gives the ground-state electron configuration and the charge of one species, A, B or C. A charge of 0 means a neutral atom.

| Species | Configuration | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| A | [Ar] 4s² 3d³ | 0 |
| B | [Xe] 6s² 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ | 1+ |
| C | [Xe] 6s² 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ | 3+ |

A fourth species, D, is an ion with a charge of 3+. In its ground state it has 2 electrons in the first shell, 8 in the second, 18 in the third and 18 in the fourth.

<Part label="a">

Identify species A, B and C. For each one, show the number of electrons and the number of protons that lead to your answer.

<Answer>

A configuration gives the number of electrons. The charge of a species is its protons minus its electrons, so its protons are its electrons plus its charge. The number of protons is the atomic number, and that names the element.

**Species A.** The symbol [Ar] stands for the 18 electrons of an argon atom.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&18 + 2 + 3 \\
&= 23\ \text{electrons}
\end{aligned}
$$

The charge is 0, so A has 23 protons as well. Element 23 is vanadium: A is a **vanadium atom**, V.

**Species B.** The symbol [Xe] stands for the 54 electrons of a xenon atom.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&54 + 2 + 14 + 10 \\
&= 80\ \text{electrons}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
80 + 1 = 81\ \text{protons}
$$

Element 81 is thallium: B is the thallium ion **$\ce{Tl+}$**.

**Species C.** The configuration is the one given for B, so C also has 80 electrons.

$$
80 + 3 = 83\ \text{protons}
$$

Element 83 is bismuth: C is the bismuth ion **$\ce{Bi^3+}$**.

B and C have the same 80 electrons in the same arrangement. They differ in the nucleus, which holds 81 protons in one and 83 in the other. So a configuration alone does not name a species. With a charge of 0, this same configuration would belong to a mercury atom, which has 80 protons.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify species D and write its ground-state electron configuration.

<Answer>

Add up the shells.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2 + 8 + 18 + 18 \\
&= 46\ \text{electrons}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
46 + 3 = 49\ \text{protons}
$$

Element 49 is indium: D is the indium ion **$\ce{In^3+}$**.

For the configuration, divide the electrons of each shell among its subshells. In a ground state the subshells of one shell fill in the order s, p, d. An s subshell holds up to 2 electrons, a p subshell up to 6 and a d subshell up to 10.

- Shell 1 holds 2 electrons: 1s²
- Shell 2 holds 8: 2s² 2p⁶
- Shell 3 holds 18: 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰
- Shell 4 holds 18: 4s² 4p⁶ 4d¹⁰

The fourth shell also has a 4f subshell, and here it is empty.

Written in the order of filling, that is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁶ 4d¹⁰. Everything up to 4p⁶ is the configuration of a krypton atom, which has 36 electrons. So the configuration of D is **[Kr] 4d¹⁰**.

Check the count against the shells:

$$
36 + 10 = 46\ \text{electrons}
$$

It also agrees with the indium atom, which is [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p¹. The ion has lost the three electrons of the atom's fifth shell, and the shell counts given for D show no fifth shell.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for each species identified, with its number of electrons or of protons shown: a vanadium atom (23 electrons, 23 protons); $\ce{Tl+}$ (80 electrons, 81 protons); $\ce{Bi^3+}$ (80 electrons, 83 protons). The element's name with its charge (thallium, 1+) may stand in place of the symbol $\ce{Tl+}$. A species named with neither number shown earns 0 for that species. For B or C, mercury (the 80 electrons taken as the atomic number), or gold or iridium (the charge subtracted, giving 79 or 77 protons), earns 0 for that species.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for $\ce{In^3+}$ (46 electrons, 49 protons; the numbers need not be shown); 1 point for [Kr] 4d¹⁰. The same configuration written in full, or with its subshells in order of shell number, also earns the second point. [Kr] 5s² 4d⁸ has 46 electrons, but it puts two of them in a fifth shell, which the shell counts rule out: it does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={21} title="Seven ions and a missing radius" topic="1.5" points={5}>

Here are seven ions: $\ce{Sc^3+}$, $\ce{Ga+}$, $\ce{Ga^3+}$, $\ce{Ge^2+}$, $\ce{Ge^4+}$, $\ce{As^3+}$ and $\ce{As^5+}$.

<Part label="a">

Identify every other ion in the list that has the same number of electrons as $\ce{Ge^4+}$. Support your answer with the number of electrons in each of the seven ions.

<Answer>

The number of electrons in an ion is its number of protons minus its charge. The protons are the atomic number, from the periodic table.

| Ion | Protons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Sc^3+}$ | 21 | 18 |
| $\ce{Ga+}$ | 31 | 30 |
| $\ce{Ga^3+}$ | 31 | 28 |
| $\ce{Ge^2+}$ | 32 | 30 |
| $\ce{Ge^4+}$ | 32 | 28 |
| $\ce{As^3+}$ | 33 | 30 |
| $\ce{As^5+}$ | 33 | 28 |

$\ce{Ge^4+}$ has 28 electrons. So do **$\ce{Ga^3+}$ and $\ce{As^5+}$**, and no other ion in the list.

Neither the element nor the charge settles the count. $\ce{Ge^2+}$ is the same element as $\ce{Ge^4+}$ and has 30 electrons. $\ce{Sc^3+}$, $\ce{Ga^3+}$ and $\ce{As^3+}$ all have a charge of 3+, and they have 18, 28 and 30.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{Ge^4+}$ and the ions you identified in part (a) also have the same electron configuration, so they are isoelectronic. Their radii have been worked out from distances measured in crystals in which each ion has six neighbors. Of the ions you identified, the one with the fewest protons has a radius of 62 pm, and the one with the most protons has a radius of 46 pm. (A picometer, pm, is 10⁻¹² m.)

Decide whether the radius of $\ce{Ge^4+}$ must be larger than 62 pm, smaller than 46 pm, or between the two values. Justify your answer with Coulomb's law.

<Answer>

The ion with the fewest protons is $\ce{Ga^3+}$, with 31, and the one with the most is $\ce{As^5+}$, with 33. $\ce{Ge^4+}$ has 32.

The three ions have the same 28 electrons in the same arrangement, so their electrons shield one another in the same way. Only the nucleus differs.

By Coulomb's law, a larger nuclear charge attracts the same electrons more strongly and holds them closer in. So $\ce{Ge^4+}$, with 32 protons, is smaller than $\ce{Ga^3+}$ and larger than $\ce{As^5+}$.

Its radius must lie **between 46 pm and 62 pm**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Estimate the radius of $\ce{Ge^4+}$. Explain how you made your estimate.

<Answer>

In its number of protons, $\ce{Ge^4+}$ is halfway between the other two ions: 32 is halfway between 31 and 33. So a fair estimate is the radius halfway between theirs.

$$
\frac{62 + 46}{2} = 54\ \text{pm}
$$

The estimate is **about 54 pm**.

The measured radius is 53 pm, so the estimate is 1 pm too large, which is about 2 percent.

There was no reason to expect it to be exact. The reasoning of part (b) gives the two limits. It does not say that each added proton shrinks the ion by the same amount, and here it does not: the radius falls by 9 pm from $\ce{Ga^3+}$ to $\ce{Ge^4+}$ and by 7 pm from $\ce{Ge^4+}$ to $\ce{As^5+}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for finding the electrons of an ion as its protons minus its charge, shown at least for $\ce{Ge^4+}$ and for each ion chosen (28 electrons); 1 point for $\ce{Ga^3+}$ and $\ce{As^5+}$ and no other ion. Adding the charge to the protons gives 36 for $\ce{Ge^4+}$ and picks out $\ce{As^3+}$: that answer earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for between 46 pm and 62 pm, with an ordering of the three ions as the reason (by protons, 31, 32 and 33, or by charge); 1 point for the reason from Coulomb's law: the ions have the same electrons (or the same configuration), and more protons hold the same electrons closer in. "Between" with no reason earns 0 for the part. A reason that names only the size of the ion's charge ("4+ is more than 3+"), with nothing about protons or the nucleus, does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for an estimate of about 54 pm (any value from 52 pm to 56 pm), reasoned from 32 protons being halfway between 31 and 33. A value given with no reasoning, or a value outside 52 pm to 56 pm, earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={22} title="An entry for a zinc ion" topic="1.5" points={4}>

A table of electron configurations on a website has this entry for the zinc ion, $\ce{Zn^2+}$:

[Ar] 4s² 3d⁸

<Part label="a">

Classify the website's entry as the ground state of $\ce{Zn^2+}$, an excited state of $\ce{Zn^2+}$, or an impossible configuration. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

Take three checks in turn.

**The count.** Zinc is element 30, and the charge 2+ means two electrons fewer than the atom.

$$
30 - 2 = 28\ \text{electrons}
$$

Now count the entry.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&18 + 2 + 8 \\
&= 28\ \text{electrons}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the right number for $\ce{Zn^2+}$.

**The limits.** An s subshell holds up to 2 electrons and a d subshell up to 10. Neither 4s² nor 3d⁸ is over its limit, so the configuration is possible.

**The ground state.** Find it from the atom. Zinc is the last box of the d block in row 4 of the periodic table, so a zinc atom is [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰. The electrons a cation has lost come out of the highest-numbered shell first, because they are the farthest from the nucleus and the least tightly held. For zinc that is shell 4, so the two 4s electrons leave, and the ground state of $\ce{Zn^2+}$ is [Ar] 3d¹⁰.

$$
18 + 10 = 28\ \text{electrons}
$$

The entry is not that.

So the entry is an **excited state** of $\ce{Zn^2+}$. It has the right electrons, but two of them are out in the fourth shell, in 4s, while the 3d subshell has room for two more.

A caution. The test used for a neutral atom would pass this entry, because 4s fills before 3d and here 4s is full. That test cannot be trusted for a cation: it gets some cations wrong, and this is one. For a cation, find the ground state from the atom, as above, and compare.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify the neutral atom that has the same number of electrons as $\ce{Zn^2+}$ and write its ground-state configuration. Then state what your answer shows about the website's entry.

<Answer>

The ion $\ce{Zn^2+}$ has 28 electrons. A neutral atom has as many protons as electrons, so this atom has 28 protons. It is **nickel**.

Nickel is in row 4 of the periodic table, the eighth box of the d block. Reading the table up to it gives **[Ar] 4s² 3d⁸**.

That is the website's entry. So the entry is a ground state after all, but of a nickel atom, not of $\ce{Zn^2+}$.

The two species have the same number of electrons and different nuclei, with 28 protons in one and 30 in the other. Each ground state is found by its own rule: the filling order for a neutral atom, and the loss of electrons from the highest shell for a cation. Here the two rules give different arrangements for the same 28 electrons.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for showing that the entry is a possible configuration of $\ce{Zn^2+}$: it has 28 electrons, the right count, and no subshell is over its limit; 1 point for the ground state of $\ce{Zn^2+}$, [Ar] 3d¹⁰, found by taking the atom's electrons from its highest shell first; 1 point for an excited state, because the entry is not that ground state. An answer of "impossible" can earn only the second point, and an answer of "ground state" only the first.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for nickel with [Ar] 4s² 3d⁸, and the statement that this is the website's entry (so the entry is the ground state of a nickel atom, not of the ion). [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s² is the same configuration and also earns the point. Nickel with its configuration and no statement about the entry earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={23} title="Two forces compared" topic="1.5" points={4}>

Coulomb's law gives the size of the force between two charged particles.

$$
F_{\text{coulombic}} \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}
$$

Here $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the two charges and $r$ is the distance between them. (The sign ∝ means "is proportional to".)

Consider two pairs of particles. Each pair is far from the other pair and from every other charge.

- Pair 1 is one electron and a nucleus that holds 9 protons. They are a distance $d$ apart.
- Pair 2 is two electrons. They are the same distance $d$ apart.

The charge of a proton is 1+ and the charge of an electron is 1−, so the nucleus has a charge of 9+.

(The arrangement is made up for this question. The electrons of a real atom do not sit at fixed distances.)

<Part label="a">

For each pair, state whether the two particles attract or repel each other, and give the reason. Then determine how many times as strong the force in pair 1 is as the force in pair 2.

<Answer>

**Pair 1 attracts.** The nucleus is positive and the electron is negative, and opposite charges attract.

**Pair 2 repels.** Both electrons are negative, and like charges repel.

The size of a force is proportional to the sizes of the two charges multiplied together, divided by the square of the distance. The distance is the same in both pairs, so it cancels, and the charges alone decide.

$$
\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{9 \times 1}{1 \times 1} = 9
$$

The attraction in pair 1 is **9 times** as strong as the repulsion in pair 2.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The two electrons of pair 2 are moved until they are $2d$ apart. Pair 1 is not changed. Determine what happens to the size of each force. Then determine how many times as strong the force in pair 1 now is as the force in pair 2.

<Answer>

Nothing about pair 1 has changed, so its force is the same as before.

In pair 2 the distance has doubled. The distance is squared in Coulomb's law, so the force is divided by 4: the repulsion falls to **one quarter** of what it was.

$$
\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{9}{1/4} = 36
$$

The force in pair 1 is now **36 times** as strong as the force in pair 2.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Pair 2 is put back at the distance $d$. Then the electron of pair 1 is moved away from the nucleus, until the force in pair 1 is the same size as the force in pair 2. Determine the new distance between the nucleus and its electron, as a multiple of $d$. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

At the distance $d$ the force in pair 1 is 9 times as strong as the force in pair 2. So the move has to cut the force in pair 1 to one ninth.

The force falls with the square of the distance, and the square of 3 is 9. So the distance must be tripled: the new distance is **$3d$**.

Check:

$$
\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{9}{3^2} = 1
$$

The two forces are then the same size. They are still not the same kind: one pulls its two particles together, and the other pushes its two apart.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for attraction in pair 1 and repulsion in pair 2, with the reason (opposite charges attract and like charges repel); 1 point for 9 times as strong, from the products of the charges at the same distance.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for the force in pair 2 falling to one quarter while the force in pair 1 stays the same, so that the force in pair 1 is 36 times as strong. A ratio of 18, from a distance that was not squared, earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for $3d$, justified by the square of the distance: three times the distance gives one ninth of the force. A distance of $9d$, from a distance that was not squared, earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={24} title="Two shells of a krypton atom" topic="1.5" points={6}>

A krypton atom in its ground state has the electron configuration [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁶.

<Part label="a">

Determine how many electrons a krypton atom has in its third shell, and state how many of them are in each subshell of that shell. Then decide whether the third shell is full. Justify your decision with the number of orbitals in the shell.

<Answer>

The subshells of the third shell are 3s, 3p and 3d. The number in a subshell's name is its shell.

In krypton's configuration the 3s² and the 3p⁶ are inside the [Ar]. The 3d¹⁰ is written after 4s², because 3d fills after 4s, but it belongs to the third shell all the same.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2 + 6 + 10 \\
&= 18\ \text{electrons}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the third shell holds **18 electrons**: 2 in 3s, 6 in 3p and 10 in 3d.

**The shell is full.** An s subshell is 1 orbital, a p subshell is 3 and a d subshell is 5, and the third shell has no other subshell.

$$
1 + 3 + 5 = 9\ \text{orbitals}
$$

An orbital holds at most two electrons.

$$
9 \times 2 = 18\ \text{electrons}
$$

Krypton's third shell holds 18, so every one of its orbitals holds two.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Determine how many electrons a krypton atom has in its fourth shell, and how many more electrons that shell could hold. State which subshells have the room.

<Answer>

Krypton's fourth-shell electrons are the 4s² and the 4p⁶.

$$
2 + 6 = 8\ \text{electrons}
$$

The fourth shell has four subshells, 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f, and an f subshell is 7 orbitals.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1 + 3 + 5 + 7 \\
&= 16\ \text{orbitals}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
16 \times 2 = 32\ \text{electrons}
$$

So the shell could hold 32.

$$
32 - 8 = 24\ \text{more}
$$

The room is in the two subshells that are empty: 10 places in 4d and 14 in 4f.

Krypton is a noble gas, and its outer s and p subshells are full. Its outer shell is not. It holds **8 electrons** of a possible 32, a quarter of what it could hold, with room for **24 more**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The element that follows krypton in the periodic table has 37 electrons in its atom. In the ground state of that atom, the thirty-seventh electron is in the 5s subshell, although the fourth shell is not full. Explain why, in terms of the energies of subshells.

<Answer>

A ground state is the arrangement of lowest energy. So each electron goes into the lowest-energy subshell that still has room.

In an atom with more than one electron, the subshells of one shell do not have the same energy. Within a shell, s is the lowest, then p, then d, and f is higher still. The steps are large enough that the shells overlap: in this atom the 5s subshell is lower in energy than 4d and 4f, just as 4s fills before 3d one row up.

In krypton everything up to 4p is full. For the thirty-seventh electron, the lowest subshell with room is 5s, so that is where it goes. In 4d or 4f it would be at a higher energy.

The periodic table shows the same order. Element 37, rubidium, begins row 5, in the s block. The 4d subshell fills later in row 5, and 4f fills in row 6.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for 18 electrons in the third shell: 2 in 3s, 6 in 3p and 10 in 3d; 1 point for a full shell, justified by its 9 orbitals holding two electrons each. Saying that each of the shell's three subshells holds its greatest number (2, 6 and 10) also earns the second point. A count of 8, which leaves the 3d¹⁰ electrons out of the third shell, earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for 8 electrons in the fourth shell, the 4s² and the 4p⁶; 1 point for room for 24 more, in the 4d and 4f subshells, from a shell that could hold 32. Counting the 3d¹⁰ electrons in the fourth shell (18 electrons, room for 14 more) earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for saying that in a ground state an electron takes the lowest-energy subshell that has room (or that the ground state is the arrangement of lowest energy); 1 point for saying that the subshells of one shell differ in energy, with 4d higher in energy than 5s in this atom (the shells overlap); naming 4d alone is enough for the second point. "5s fills before 4d" or "a new row starts", with nothing about energy, repeats the order and earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

## Part F: Photoelectron spectra

These five questions are on topic 1.6: what the positions and the heights of the peaks in a photoelectron spectrum say about the electrons of an atom or an ion, and how Coulomb's law explains where the peaks are. Every binding energy is in MJ/mol. You will need the periodic table.

<Question number={25} title="Three printouts, four elements" topic="1.6" points={4}>

A class has printouts of three complete photoelectron spectra, P, Q and R. Each is the spectrum of neutral atoms of one of these four elements: lithium, beryllium, carbon and oxygen. No two printouts are of the same element, so one of the four elements has no printout.

The printouts were made at different sizes. A height on one printout can be compared with the other heights on that printout, but not with a height on another printout. The table gives each peak's binding energy and its height on the paper, measured with a ruler.

| Printout | Binding energy (MJ/mol) | Height (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 28.6 | 30 |
| P | 1.72 | 30 |
| P | 1.09 | 30 |
| Q | 6.26 | 48 |
| Q | 0.52 | 24 |
| R | 11.5 | 18 |
| R | 0.90 | 18 |

<Part label="a">

Determine the number of electrons that each peak stands for on each printout, and explain how you set the scale. Then write the electron configuration that each printout shows and identify its element.

<Answer>

In every element after hydrogen the 1s subshell holds 2 electrons, and the 1s peak is the one at the highest binding energy. So on each printout the peak at the highest binding energy stands for 2 electrons. That sets the scale for that printout, and for that printout only.

| Printout | 1s peak (mm) | mm per electron |
|---|---|---|
| P | 30 | 15 |
| Q | 48 | 24 |
| R | 18 | 9 |

Name the peaks in order from the highest binding energy: 1s, then 2s, then 2p.

- **P:** three peaks of 30 mm stand for 2, 2 and 2 electrons. The configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p².
- **Q:** the peak of 48 mm stands for 2 electrons and the peak of 24 mm for 1. The configuration is 1s² 2s¹.
- **R:** two peaks of 18 mm stand for 2 and 2 electrons. The configuration is 1s² 2s².

Add up the electrons on each printout:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{P:}\quad 2 + 2 + 2 &= 6 \\
\text{Q:}\quad 2 + 1 &= 3 \\
\text{R:}\quad 2 + 2 &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

The atoms are neutral, so the number of electrons is the number of protons. P is **carbon**, Q is **lithium** and R is **beryllium**.

The millimeters themselves name nothing. The tallest peak in the table, 48 mm, stands for 2 electrons, the same as a peak of 18 mm on printout R.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why the fourth element fits none of the three printouts. State what its complete spectrum would show: the number of peaks, and their heights compared with one another.

<Answer>

The element with no printout is **oxygen**, 1s² 2s² 2p⁴.

Oxygen has electrons in three subshells, so its spectrum has three peaks. From the highest binding energy to the lowest they stand for 2, 2 and 4 electrons: the peak at the lowest binding energy is twice as tall as each of the other two.

Printouts Q and R have only two peaks each, so neither shows a 2p subshell at all. Printout P has three peaks, but its three are equally tall, which takes 2 electrons in 2p, where oxygen has 4. So no printout fits oxygen.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Printout Q is put through a copier that doubles every height. Call the copy S. State the height of each peak of S, in millimeters, and the number of electrons each stands for. Then decide which stands for more electrons: the shorter peak of S or a peak of printout P. Explain how your answer fits the heights of those two peaks in millimeters.

<Answer>

The peaks of S are **96 mm and 48 mm** tall, and they stand for **2 electrons and 1 electron**, as on Q.

The copier changes every height by the same factor, so the two peaks keep their ratio, 2 to 1. The scale of S is set by its own 1s peak: 96 mm for 2 electrons is 48 mm for each electron. S is still the spectrum of lithium, with 3 electrons.

**A peak of P stands for more electrons**, 2 against 1, although it is the shorter of the two on paper. The shorter peak of S is 48 mm tall, and on S that is 1 electron. A peak of P is 30 mm tall, and on P, where one electron is 15 mm, that is 2 electrons. A height counts electrons only when it is compared with a peak of its own printout whose number of electrons is known, and the 1s peak is that peak: 48 mm is half of S's 1s peak, and 30 mm is the whole of P's.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for setting the scale of each printout from its own peak at the highest binding energy, the 1s peak, as 2 electrons; 1 point for all three results: P is carbon, 1s² 2s² 2p²; Q is lithium, 1s² 2s¹; R is beryllium, 1s² 2s². Matching each printout's ratio of heights to the configurations of the four elements also earns the first point. An answer that compares a height on one printout with a height on another does not earn the first point. The student's idea does hold in another case: added electrons offset added protons when they lie in shells inside the electron in question. Here they lie outside it.

| Se | 34 | 28 | 120 |
| I | 53 | 46 | 139 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the charge that the valence electrons feel in each atom, by this estimate. Then, for each pair of neighbors on the diagonal (carbon and phosphorus, phosphorus and selenium, selenium and iodine), calculate the ratio of the estimated attractions, upper atom to lower atom. State what the estimate predicts for the first ionization energy along the diagonal.

<Answer>

The charges, protons minus core electrons:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{C:}\quad 6 - 2 &= +4 \\
\text{P:}\quad 15 - 10 &= +5 \\
\text{Se:}\quad 34 - 28 &= +6 \\
\text{I:}\quad 53 - 46 &= +7
\end{aligned}
$$

The attraction is proportional to the charge divided by the square of the radius. So the ratio for two atoms is the ratio of their charges times the square of the ratio of their radii, turned over.

Carbon to phosphorus:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{F_{\text{C}}}{F_{\text{P}}} &= \frac{4}{5} \times \left(\frac{107}{76}\right)^{2} \\
&= 0.800 \times 1.982 \\
&= \mathbf{1.59}
\end{aligned}
$$

Phosphorus to selenium:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{F_{\text{P}}}{F_{\text{Se}}} &= \frac{5}{6} \times \left(\frac{120}{107}\right)^{2} \\
&= 0.8333 \times 1.258 \\
&= \mathbf{1.05}
\end{aligned}
$$

Selenium to iodine:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{F_{\text{Se}}}{F_{\text{I}}} &= \frac{6}{7} \times \left(\frac{139}{120}\right)^{2} \\
&= 0.8571 \times 1.342 \\
&= \mathbf{1.15}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every ratio is greater than 1. By these numbers the upper atom of each pair holds its valence electrons more strongly: the growing distance outweighs the growing charge at every step. So the estimate predicts that **the first ionization energy falls at every step down the diagonal**: highest for carbon, then phosphorus, then selenium, and lowest for iodine.

The charges are rough counts, so the last digit of each ratio means little. What matters is how far each ratio is from 1: 59 percent, 5 percent and 15 percent.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The measured first ionization energies are 1086 kJ/mol for carbon, 1012 kJ/mol for phosphorus, 941 kJ/mol for selenium and 1008 kJ/mol for iodine. For each pair, determine whether the estimate pointed the right way. Then use the three ratios to explain when an estimate like this can be trusted.

<Answer>

- **Carbon and phosphorus:** 1086 against 1012 kJ/mol. Carbon's is higher, by 74 kJ/mol. The estimate was right.
- **Phosphorus and selenium:** 1012 against 941 kJ/mol. Phosphorus's is higher, by 71 kJ/mol. The estimate was right.
- **Selenium and iodine:** 941 against 1008 kJ/mol. Iodine's is higher, by 67 kJ/mol. **The estimate was wrong.** In this pair the larger charge outweighs the larger distance, and the estimate said the opposite.

Now set the results beside the ratios. The estimate was right at 1.59 and wrong at 1.15. It was also right at 1.05, but an estimate that fails when it leans 15 percent one way earns no credit for agreeing when it leans only 5 percent.

**Do not trust the estimate when its ratio is near 1.** There the two levers are too nearly balanced for numbers this rough to decide. A ratio near 1 decides nothing. A ratio far from 1, as for carbon and phosphorus, is a much safer guide, but not a guarantee: the estimate knows nothing of subshells or of paired electrons.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

<Answer>

Either of these earns the point.

It is not the distance from the nucleus of the electron that is removed. Why it matters more for some pairs: a ratio of 1.15 is turned around if the charges and distances are off by about 15 percent between them, and errors of that size are easy to make with inputs this rough. A ratio of 1.59 is not turned around so easily.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For each pair that the claim gets right, explain why its prediction holds there. Use the configurations of the two atoms.

<Answer>

Rubidium is [Kr] 5s¹ and strontium is [Kr] 5s².

**What stays the same.** The electron removed is a 5s electron in both atoms, so it starts in the same shell, at about the same distance from the nucleus. The same 36 core electrons lie inside it in both. (Strontium's other 5s electron is in the same shell, and electrons of the same shell shield one another poorly.)

**What changes.** Strontium's nucleus has one more proton. By the rough count the charge that the 5s electron feels goes from about +1 in rubidium (37 − 36) to about +2 in strontium (38 − 36).

By Coulomb's law, a larger charge at about the same distance is a stronger attraction. So strontium's electron takes more energy to remove: 549 kJ/mol against 403 kJ/mol, which is 146 kJ/mol more.

This is what is right in the claim. When the shell and the shielding stay the same, as they do from rubidium to strontium, more protons generally do mean a higher first ionization energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Hydrogen and rubidium share a group, and so do beryllium and strontium. Use these two pairs to state what the claim leaves out. Support your answer with the charge that the electron removed feels in each atom, and name the law you rely on.

<Answer>

The claim counts the protons. It leaves out what lies between the electron and the nucleus, and how far from the nucleus the electron is.

**Shielding.** Rubidium has 36 more protons than hydrogen, and 36 core electrons that hydrogen does not have. Strontium has 34 more protons than beryllium, and 34 more core electrons. By the rough count the charge felt is the same within each pair:

- hydrogen +1, with nothing between its one electron and the nucleus, and rubidium about +1 (37 − 36);
- beryllium about +2 (4 − 2) and strontium about +2 (38 − 36).

The added protons are offset by the added core electrons.

**Distance.** Hydrogen's electron is in the first shell, and beryllium's valence electrons are in the second. Rubidium's and strontium's are in the fifth shell, much farther from the nucleus. By Coulomb's law the force falls with the square of the distance. About the same charge at a much greater distance is a much weaker attraction.

So rubidium's first ionization energy is less than a third of hydrogen's (403 against 1312 kJ/mol), and strontium's is well below beryllium's (549 against 900 kJ/mol). This is the trend down a group: first ionization energy generally falls, because the valence shell is farther out while the charge felt stays about the same.

The other three pairs that fail (hydrogen with beryllium, hydrogen with strontium, beryllium with rubidium) fail for the same two reasons. Beryllium's valence electrons, for example, feel about twice the charge that hydrogen's electron feels, but from a shell farther out, and hydrogen's electron is still the harder one to remove.

</Answer>

<Question number={35} title="A formula read backward" topic="1.8" points={4}>

An ionic solid has the formula $\ce{M2X3}$. M stands for a metal of group 13, and X stands for a nonmetal. Each element has the usual charge of its group.

<Part label="a">

State the charge of an M ion, and justify it from M's valence electrons.

<Answer>

**3+.** For group 13 the number of valence electrons is the last digit of the group number: three. They are the atom's outermost electrons, far from the nucleus and shielded by the core, so each can be removed at a moderate cost. A fourth electron would have to come from the core, where the attraction to the nucleus is much stronger. So in an ionic compound M has usually lost its three valence electrons and no more: $\ce{M^3+}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Use the formula to determine the charge of an X ion. Show that the charges add up to zero.

<Answer>

Call the charge of an X ion $q$. One formula unit holds two $\ce{M^3+}$ ions and three X ions, and the compound has no charge overall:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2(+3) + 3q &= 0 \\
3q &= -6 \\
q &= -2
\end{aligned}
$$

The ion is $\ce{X^2-}$. Check by adding up:

$$
2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify the group of X, and justify your answer from X's valence electrons.

<Answer>

**Group 16.** A charge of 2− means that an X atom has taken on two electrons. In an ionic compound, a nonmetal has taken on electrons until the s and p subshells of its valence shell are full, and together they hold eight. So an X atom has

$$
8 - 2 = 6
$$

valence electrons. For groups 13 to 17 that number is the last digit of the group number, so X is in group 16.

The charge rule gives the same result. For a nonmetal of groups 15 to 17, the usual charge is the group number minus 18, so the group number is the charge plus 18:

$$
-2 + 18 = 16
$$

The formula cannot say which element of group 16 X is. Oxygen, sulfur and selenium each have six valence electrons and each form a 2− ion, so with this metal each gives a formula of the same pattern.

<Answer>

An atom of Z has seven valence electrons and takes on one more: $\ce{Z-}$. Three $\ce{Z-}$ ions balance one $\ce{M^3+}$ ion, so the formula is **$\ce{MZ3}$**.

$$
(+3) + 3(-1) = 0
$$

<Question number={36} title="Two group 1 metals and selenium" topic="1.8" points={5}>

Sodium and rubidium are metals of group 1, and rubidium lies two rows below sodium. Selenium is a nonmetal of group 16. Each metal reacts with selenium to form an ionic compound.

First ionization energy generally falls down a group. So a chemist predicts that rubidium gives up its valence electron more readily than sodium does, and that it reacts with selenium more readily.

<Part label="a">

Write the formula of each compound, and explain why the two formulas have the same pattern.

<Answer>

**$\ce{Na2Se}$ and $\ce{Rb2Se}$.** Sodium and rubidium are both in group 1, so each has one valence electron and forms a 1+ ion: $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Rb+}$. Selenium, in group 16, has six valence electrons and takes on two more: $\ce{Se^2-}$. Two 1+ ions balance one 2− ion:

$$
2(+1) + (-2) = 0
$$

The pattern is the same because a charge comes from the number of valence electrons, and two metals in one column have the same number.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Two students comment on the chemist's prediction.

- **Student 1:** "If rubidium gives up electrons more readily, then in its compound each rubidium atom has given up two electrons where each sodium atom has given up one."
- **Student 2:** "Sodium and rubidium are in the same group, so they have the same number of valence electrons and must react equally readily. The prediction is wrong before any experiment is done."

Evaluate each student's statement.

<Answer>

**Student 1 is wrong.** How readily an atom gives up an electron and how many electrons it gives up are two different things. The number is set by the atom's valence electrons, and a rubidium atom has one, just as a sodium atom has. So each rubidium atom has given up one electron, its ion is $\ce{Rb+}$, and the formula of part (a) stands. If the prediction is right, a rubidium atom gives up that one electron more easily. It does not give up more of them.

**Student 2 is wrong as well.** The first half of the statement is true: the two metals have the same number of valence electrons, and that is why they have the same charge and the same pattern of formula. But the group does not make the two atoms hold that electron equally tightly. Rubidium's valence electron is in the fifth shell and sodium's in the third, and each feels an effective nuclear charge of about +1 (37 − 36 and 11 − 10). By Coulomb's law the electron that is farther out is held less tightly. That is why first ionization energy generally falls down a group, and why the two metals need not react equally readily. Nothing about the group rules the prediction out, and only an experiment can show whether it is right.

Put together: the column fixes how many electrons each atom gives up, and so the charge and the formula. It does not fix how readily the atom gives them up.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Describe an experiment whose result would test the chemist's prediction. State what is kept the same for the two metals, and what result would support the prediction.

<Answer>

Let each metal react with the same substance under the same conditions, and compare how readily it reacts.

For example, a trained chemist heats a small piece of each metal with selenium in the same way, with air kept out, and compares how easily each reaction starts. A comparison with another substance, such as cold water, tests the first half of the prediction and not the part about selenium.

For the comparison with water, a trained chemist behind a safety screen drops a very small piece of each metal with a clean surface into cold water and compares how fast and how violently each one reacts.

**Kept the same:** the substance the metal reacts with, the size of the piece of metal and how clean its surface is, and the temperature.

**Result that supports the prediction:** rubidium reacts faster or more violently than sodium, or begins to react at a lower temperature. With cold water this is what is seen: sodium fizzes as it skims over the surface, and rubidium reacts explosively.

One caution. An ionization energy is measured on separate atoms in the gas phase. In a reaction the atoms start out in a piece of solid metal, and the ions that form are attracted to the particles around them. So a more violent reaction is evidence that fits the prediction. It is not a measurement of how tightly one atom holds its electron.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={37} title="Three formulas for one compound" topic="1.8" points={5}>

Potassium is a metal of group 1, and phosphorus is a nonmetal of group 15. They form an ionic compound in which each element has the usual charge of its group. Three students each write a formula for it.

- **Student 1** writes $\ce{KP}$: "The potassium ion is 3+ and the phosphide ion is 3−. One of each adds up to zero."
- **Student 2** writes $\ce{K6P2}$: "The potassium ion is 1+ and the phosphide ion is 3−. Six potassium ions carry 6+ and two phosphide ions carry 6−."
- **Student 3** writes $\ce{K3P}$: "The potassium ion is 1+ and the phosphide ion is 3−. Three potassium ions carry 3+ and one phosphide ion carries 3−."

With the charges its writer used, each formula adds up to zero. Only one of the three is the formula of the compound.

<Part label="a">

Identify the right formula, and justify the two charges in it. For each of the other two formulas, state what is wrong with it and how to correct it.

<Answer>

**Student 3 is right: $\ce{K3P}$.** Potassium, in group 1, has one valence electron and forms $\ce{K+}$. Phosphorus, in group 15, has five valence electrons and takes on three more: $\ce{P^3-}$. Three $\ce{K+}$ ions balance one $\ce{P^3-}$ ion:

$$
3(+1) + (-3) = 0
$$

Three to one is also the smallest whole-number ratio that adds up to zero.

**Student 1 gave potassium a charge it does not have.** The sum is zero:

$$
(+3) + (-3) = 0
$$

But a potassium atom, [Ar] 4s¹, has one valence electron. A second and a third electron would have to come from the 3p subshell of the core, a shell closer to the nucleus and far less shielded, where the attraction to the nucleus is much stronger. So the ion is $\ce{K+}$, and with $\ce{K+}$ the formula $\ce{KP}$ no longer adds up:

$$
(+1) + (-3) = -2
$$

It takes three $\ce{K+}$ ions to balance one $\ce{P^3-}$ ion: $\ce{K3P}$. Adding up to zero is not enough when the charges are not the ones the groups give.

**Student 2 did not reduce the formula.** The charges are right and the sum is zero:

$$
6(+1) + 2(-3) = 0
$$

But the formula of an ionic compound gives the smallest whole-number ratio of its ions. Six to two is the same ratio as three to one, so divide both subscripts by 2: $\ce{K3P}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the number of moles of electrons that pass from potassium atoms to phosphorus atoms when 5.00 g of the compound forms from potassium and phosphorus.

<Answer>

First the molar mass of $\ce{K3P}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3(39.10) + 30.97 \\
&= 148.27\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then the moles of the compound:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{5.00\ \text{g}}{148.27\ \text{g/mol}} \\
&= 0.03372\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.0337 mol to three significant figures.

Now count the electrons. One formula unit holds three $\ce{K+}$ ions, and each was made when a potassium atom gave up one electron. So three electrons change hands for every formula unit. They are the same three electrons that the phosphorus atom took on, so count them once: three, not six.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3 \times 0.03372\ \text{mol} \\
&= \mathbf{0.101}\ \text{mol of electrons}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>
