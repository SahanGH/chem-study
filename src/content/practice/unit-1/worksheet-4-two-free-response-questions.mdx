These are two short free-response questions, the kind the AP exam asks. You will need the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/).

Work each question on paper, and write every answer out completely, before you open anything. Then open each part's answer and the question's scoring rubric, which comes after its last part, and score yourself the way an exam reader would. A point is earned only if what it asks for is written out in your answer. Readers do not award what you meant.

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="The photoelectron spectrum of element Q" topic="1.6" points={4}>

The photoelectron spectrum of element Q shows five peaks:

| Binding energy (MJ/mol) | Relative number of electrons |
|---|---|
| 239 | 2 |
| 22.7 | 2 |
| 16.5 | 6 |
| 2.05 | 2 |
| 1.00 | 4 |

<Part label="a">

Write the full electron configuration of Q and identify it.

<Answer>

The configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴.

The peak at the highest binding energy is 1s, which holds 2 electrons, so the numbers in the table are the counts.

$$
2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 4 = 16
$$

A neutral atom with 16 electrons has 16 protons, so Q is sulfur.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Which peak changes in height when Q forms its most common ion, and what is that ion's charge?

<Answer>

The peak at 1.00 MJ/mol, the 3p peak. A sulfur atom gains 2 electrons into its 3p subshell, so the ion is $\ce{S^2-}$. In the ion's spectrum that peak stands for 6 electrons where the atom's stands for 4. No other peak changes in height.

The positions change as well as the heights. In an anion the same protons hold more electrons, which repel one another more, so every peak of the ion is at a lower binding energy than in the atom.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student claims that the peak at 239 MJ/mol represents the most *chemically important* electrons because they are held most tightly. Evaluate the claim.

<Answer>

The electrons at 239 MJ/mol are the 1s electrons, and they are held most tightly.
The claim is wrong. Chemistry is done by the most loosely held electrons, the valence electrons, which give the peaks at 2.05 and 1.00 MJ/mol. The tightly bound core electrons do not take part in bonding.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>The correct configuration, matching the relative number of electrons in all five peaks.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>Sulfur, tied to 16 total electrons.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>Names the 3p peak (the one at 1.00 MJ/mol) as the peak that changes and gives the charge as 2−. An answer that gives "gains 2 electrons to form $\ce{S^2-}$" without naming the peak also earns the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>Rejects the claim **and** names the valence electrons as the chemically active ones.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Successive ionization energies of element Z" topic="1.8" points={4}>

Here are the first four successive ionization energies of element Z:

| Ionization energy | Value (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|
| First, $IE_1$ | 590 |
| Second, $IE_2$ | 1145 |
| Third, $IE_3$ | 4912 |
| Fourth, $IE_4$ | 6491 |

<Part label="a">

Identify the group of Z and justify your answer from the data.

<Answer>

Group 2.

Look at how much each value rises over the one before. The three rises are 555, 3767 and 1579 kJ/mol.

The rise between $IE_2$ (1145 kJ/mol) and $IE_3$ (4912 kJ/mol) is the first rise that is several times the size of the rise before it: nearly seven times. That jump marks the start of removal from the core. Two electrons come off before it, so Z has 2 valence electrons, as the elements of that group do. (Z is calcium.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Write the formula of the compound Z forms with phosphorus.

<Answer>

$\ce{Z3P2}$, for example $\ce{Ca3P2}$. Z forms $\ce{Z^2+}$, and phosphorus, in group 15, forms $\ce{P^3-}$. The charges add up to zero:

$$
3(+2) + 2(-3) = 0
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why $IE_2 > IE_1$ *even though* both remove valence electrons.

<Answer>

The second electron is removed from a *positive* ion, $\ce{Z+}$, not from a neutral atom. The same nuclear charge now acts on fewer electrons: there is less repulsion among them, and the radius is smaller. Those that remain are held more tightly, so more energy is required to remove one.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>Group 2, with the jump from $IE_2$ to $IE_3$ cited.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>The correct 3:2 formula.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>Removal from a cation, or an argument from reduced repulsion or a smaller radius.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine points={1}>Overall: nowhere is a full shell, an octet or a noble-gas configuration given as the reason (for example, "the third electron is hard to remove because the ion has a noble-gas configuration"). Saying that the third electron comes from the core, a shell closer to the nucleus, is not such a reason. Earned only if parts (a) and (c) each give a reason.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>
