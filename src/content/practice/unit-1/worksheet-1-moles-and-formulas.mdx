Try questions 1 to 4 without a calculator: their numbers are chosen so that you do not need one. You will need Avogadro's number, from the [equation sheet](/tools/equations/), and atomic masses from the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/).

## Questions

<Question number={1} topic="1.1">

Mass of 2.0 mol of $\ce{NaOH}$ ($M = 40.0\ \text{g/mol}$): <Blank />

<Answer>

The mass is 80 g, written as $8.0 \times 10^{1}\ \text{g}$ to show its two significant figures.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={2} topic="1.1">

Moles in 22 g of $\ce{CO2}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
22\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{44.01\ \text{g}} = 0.50\ \text{mol}
$$

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="1.1">

Molecules in 0.50 mol of $\ce{H2O}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.50\ \text{mol} \times \frac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} \\
&\quad = 3.0 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={4} topic="1.1">

Total atoms in 1.0 mol of $\ce{CaCl2}$ (each formula unit counts as 3 atoms): <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1.0\ \text{mol} \times 3 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1} \\
&\quad = 1.8 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

In this compound they are ions: one calcium ion and two chloride ions in each formula unit.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="1.1">

A tablet contains 500.0 mg of $\ce{CaCO3}$ ($M = 100.09\ \text{g/mol}$).

<Part label="a">

Moles of $\ce{CaCO3}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&500.0\ \text{mg} \times \frac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \\
&\quad \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{100.09\ \text{g}} \\
&\quad = 4.996 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Moles of oxygen atoms: <Blank />

<Answer>

Each formula unit holds 3 oxygen atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3 \times 4.996 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad = 1.499 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Find the mass of calcium in the tablet.

<Answer>

One mole of $\ce{CaCO3}$ holds one mole of calcium.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&4.996 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad \times 40.08\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= 0.2002\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="1.3">

Find the percent by mass of nitrogen in $\ce{NH4NO3}$ ($M = 80.05\ \text{g/mol}$).

<Answer>

One formula unit holds two nitrogen atoms, one in $\ce{NH4+}$ and one in $\ce{NO3-}$, so each mole holds this mass of nitrogen:

$$
2(14.01\ \text{g}) = 28.02\ \text{g}
$$

$$
\frac{28.02}{80.05} \times 100\% = 35.00\%
$$

An answer of 17.50% counts only one of them.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="1.3">

A compound is 43.6 percent P and 56.4 percent O by mass.

<Part label="a">

Find the empirical formula.

<Answer>

In 100 g of the compound there are 43.6 g of P and 56.4 g of O. Change each to moles.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{P:}\quad \frac{43.6}{30.97} &= 1.408\ \text{mol} \\
\text{O:}\quad \frac{56.4}{16.00} &= 3.525\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide the larger by the smaller.

$$
\frac{3.525}{1.408} = 2.50
$$

That is 2.50 oxygen atoms for every phosphorus atom. The leftover .50 is a half, so double both: P 2, O 5. The empirical formula is $\ce{P2O5}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The molar mass is about 284 g/mol. Molecular formula: <Blank />

<Answer>

$\ce{P2O5}$ adds up to 141.94 g/mol, and 284/141.94 = 2.00, so double the empirical formula.

$\ce{P4O10}$

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
