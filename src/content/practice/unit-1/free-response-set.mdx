This set covers all of Unit 1: topics 1.1 through 1.8. Unit 1 is worth 7 to 9 percent of the AP exam.

There are 5 questions worth 32 points. Give yourself about 50 minutes. You may use a calculator, and you will need the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/) and Avogadro's number, from the [equation sheet](/tools/equations/). Write out each answer in full before you open the worked answer and the scoring rubric.

Quantum numbers, the configurations of elements that break the filling order (chromium and copper are the usual examples), and mass-spectrum peaks from molecules are not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for them.

Explain every trend with Coulomb's law: nuclear charge, distance and shielding. An answer that rests on "stability" or on "wants a full octet" earns nothing.

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Mass spectrum to mole count" topic="1.1, 1.2" points={10}>

Boron occurs as two isotopes. Its mass spectrum shows peaks at 10.013 amu (19.9%) and 11.009 amu (80.1%).

<Part label="a">

Calculate the average atomic mass of boron.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(10.013)(0.199) \\
&\quad + (11.009)(0.801) \\
&= 1.993 + 8.818 \\
&= \mathbf{10.81}\ \text{amu}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each product is good to three significant figures, 1.99 and 8.82, which is the hundredths place. So the sum is kept to the hundredths place. It is the periodic table's 10.81 amu.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State which peak is taller and explain what peak height represents.

<Answer>

The peak at 11.009 amu. Peak height is proportional to the relative abundance of that isotope, and $\ce{^{11}B}$ accounts for 80.1% of boron atoms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the number of moles in a 5.00 g sample of boron, and the number of atoms it contains.

<Answer>

With boron's molar mass from the periodic table:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{5.00}{10.81} \\
&= 0.4625\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is **0.463** mol to three significant figures.

Multiply by Avogadro's number for the atoms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.4625 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \\
&= \mathbf{2.79 \times 10^{23}}\ \text{atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain why the average atomic mass must be used for the mole calculation in part (c) rather than either isotope mass.

<Answer>

A real sample contains both isotopes in their natural proportions, so the average mass of its atoms is the abundance-weighted average. Using 10.013 amu or 11.009 amu would assume that every atom in the sample is the same isotope, which is not so.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

A second element has two isotopes, and its average atomic mass is almost exactly halfway between their masses. State what this implies about the relative peak heights.

<Answer>

The two peaks are of nearly equal height. With two isotopes, the average sits midway only when the two are almost equally abundant, and a peak's height is its isotope's share of the atoms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for weighting each mass by its fractional abundance; 1 point for 10.81 amu. A plain mean of the two masses gives 10.51 amu and earns 0.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the $\ce{^{11}B}$ peak; 1 point for saying that height means relative abundance. Confusing height with mass earns 0.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={3}>1 point for 0.463 mol; 1 point for multiplying by Avogadro's number; 1 point for $2.79 \times 10^{23}$ atoms.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for saying that the sample contains both isotopes; 1 point for saying that the average reflects their proportions.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="e" points={1}>Nearly equal peak heights.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Photoelectron spectrum to configuration to trend" topic="1.5, 1.6, 1.7, 1.8" points={10}>

A photoelectron spectrum of an unknown element from period 3 shows four peaks with relative heights 2, 2, 6 and 2, and a fifth of height 1, listed from the highest binding energy to the lowest.

<Part label="a">

Identify the element and write its complete electron configuration.

<Answer>

The heights give the electron count in each subshell. Add them up:

$$
2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 1 = 13
$$

So the atomic number is 13, and the element is **aluminum**.

The configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State which peak lies at the highest binding energy and justify it with Coulomb's law.

<Answer>

The 1s peak, of height 2. Those electrons are closest to the nucleus, with no electrons inside them to shield its protons, so they feel the largest effective nuclear charge at the smallest distance. The attraction is strongest, and the most energy is needed to remove one.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The first ionization energies of magnesium and aluminum are 738 and 578 kJ/mol respectively. Magnesium's is *higher*, even though aluminum has the greater nuclear charge. Explain.

<Answer>

The electron removed comes from a *different subshell* in each atom.

Magnesium is [Ne] 3s²: the electron removed is a 3s electron. Aluminum is [Ne] 3s² 3p¹: the electron removed is its 3p electron.

Within a shell, a p electron is held less tightly than an s electron. An s electron spends more of its time close to the nucleus, inside the shielding of the other electrons, so it feels more of the nuclear charge. The filled 3s subshell also shields the 3p electron from part of the nuclear charge.

Less time close to the nucleus and more shielding mean a weaker attraction, so aluminum's electron is easier to remove despite the extra proton.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

State the ion aluminum forms and give the formula of the compound it forms with oxygen.

<Answer>

$\ce{Al^3+}$. With $\ce{O^2-}$, the neutral formula is $\ce{Al2O3}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for summing the peak heights to 13 electrons; 1 point for aluminum; 1 point for the full configuration.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for the 1s peak; 1 point for saying that its electrons are closest to the nucleus, or that no electrons lie inside them to shield it; 1 point for the greatest effective nuclear charge. "It is the innermost" alone earns 1.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for identifying the difference between 3s and 3p; 1 point for a reason the 3p electron is held less tightly: it spends less of its time close to the nucleus than a 3s electron does, the filled 3s subshell shields it, or both. An answer that says the 3p electron is on average farther from the nucleus also earns this point. An answer arguing only from nuclear charge predicts the wrong order and earns **0**.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for $\ce{Al^3+}$; 1 point for $\ce{Al2O3}$.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Combustion analysis" topic="1.3" points={4}>

A 0.500 g sample of a compound containing only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen is burned completely in oxygen, producing 0.733 g of $\ce{CO2}$ and 0.300 g of $\ce{H2O}$. All of the sample's carbon ends up in the carbon dioxide and all of its hydrogen in the water. The compound's molar mass is 60.05 g/mol.

<Part label="a">

Determine the empirical and molecular formulas.

<Answer>

**Carbon**, from the $\ce{CO2}$. Each $\ce{CO2}$ molecule holds one carbon atom, so the moles of carbon equal the moles of $\ce{CO2}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{C}) &= \frac{0.733}{44.01} \\
&= 0.01666\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.01666\ \text{mol} \times 12.01\ \text{g/mol} \\
&\quad = 0.200\ \text{g of C}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Hydrogen**, from the $\ce{H2O}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{H2O}) &= \frac{0.300}{18.02} \\
&= 0.01665\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each $\ce{H2O}$ molecule holds two hydrogen atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2 \times 0.01665\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad = 0.03330\ \text{mol of H} \\
&0.03330\ \text{mol} \times 1.008\ \text{g/mol} \\
&\quad = 0.0336\ \text{g of H}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Oxygen**, *by difference*. It cannot be taken from the products: some of the oxygen in the $\ce{CO2}$ and the $\ce{H2O}$ came from the oxygen gas the sample burned in, not from the sample.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.500 - 0.200 - 0.0336 \\
&= 0.2664\ \text{g of oxygen}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.266 g to three significant figures.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{O}) &= \frac{0.2664}{16.00} \\
&= 0.01665\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide each amount by the smallest, 0.01665 mol: C 1.00, H 2.00, O 1.00.

The ratio $\ce{C} : \ce{H} : \ce{O}$ is $1 : 2 : 1$, so the empirical formula is $\ce{CH2O}$, with a mass of 30.03 g/mol.

$$
\frac{60.05}{30.03} = 2.00
$$

So the molecular formula is $\ce{C2H4O2}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>Carbon from the $\ce{CO2}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>Hydrogen from the $\ce{H2O}$, with the factor of 2.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>Oxygen **by difference**. Taking oxygen from the masses of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ is the classic error and loses this point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>Both formulas.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Composition of a mixture" topic="1.4" points={4}>

A 5.00 g sample of a mixture of sand and salt is stirred with water and filtered. The liquid that comes through the filter, the filtrate, is evaporated to dryness, leaving 3.20 g of $\ce{NaCl}$.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the percent by mass of $\ce{NaCl}$ in the mixture.

<Answer>

$$
\frac{3.20}{5.00} \times 100 = \mathbf{64.0}\%
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why this separation works, in terms of a property that differs between the two components.

<Answer>

The components differ in **solubility in water**. Sodium chloride is ionic and dissolves as separate $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, each surrounded by water molecules. Sand ($\ce{SiO2}$) is a covalent network solid held by strong covalent bonds throughout, and water cannot provide attractions strong enough to break it apart. Filtration then separates the undissolved solid from the solution. Why one solid dissolves in water and another does not is taken further in topics 3.2 and 3.10, and separating mixtures in topic 3.9.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the ratio; 1 point for 64.0%.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for naming the difference in solubility; 1 point for a reason grounded in bonding or in the attractions between particles. "Salt dissolves and sand does not" with no reason earns 1.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Ionic compounds and the attraction between ions" topic="1.8" points={4}>

<Part label="a">

Calcium reacts with nitrogen to form an ionic compound of the two elements. Give the ions formed and the formula of the compound.

<Answer>

$\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{N^3-}$. The neutral formula is $\ce{Ca3N2}$, since:

$$
3(+2) + 2(-3) = 0
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The lattice energy of an ionic solid is the energy needed to pull apart the ions in a mole of the solid. Predict which has the larger lattice energy, $\ce{CaO}$ or $\ce{KCl}$, and justify with Coulomb's law.

<Answer>

$\ce{CaO}$. The attraction between the ions is proportional to the product of the charges. Compare the sizes of the charges. For $\ce{CaO}$:

$$
2 \times 2 = 4
$$

For $\ce{KCl}$:

$$
1 \times 1 = 1
$$

$\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ are also the smaller ions, so the distance between the ions' centers is shorter. The attraction grows as the distance between the ions shrinks, so it is stronger still. Both factors point the same way.

A stronger attraction between the ions means a larger lattice energy. How the charges and the sizes of ions set the strength of their attraction is taken further in topic 2.2.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for both ions; 1 point for $\ce{Ca3N2}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for $\ce{CaO}$; 1 point for a justification from Coulomb's law that names charge (and ideally distance). "$\ce{CaO}$ is more stable" or an answer based on the octet earns **0**.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>
