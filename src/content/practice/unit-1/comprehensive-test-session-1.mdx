This is the first of two sittings that together test all of Unit 1. It covers topics 1.1 to 1.4: moles and counting particles, mass spectra and isotopes, composition and formulas, and mixtures. Session 2 covers electrons and the periodic table.

There are 19 questions worth 91 points. Give yourself about 95 minutes. You may use a calculator, the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/) and Avogadro's number, which is on the [equation sheet](/tools/equations/).

Write out each answer in full before you open the worked answer and the scoring rubric.

Where a later part uses a value you found earlier, a correct method applied to your own earlier value earns the later points, as on the AP exam.

## Part A: Moles and counting particles

Five questions on counting particles by weighing them. You will go from a count of molecules to a mass and from the mass of one atom to an element, judge two students' setups, count the ions and the atoms in a sample of a compound, compare samples that are given in different units, and use a molar mass to tell which of three compounds a solid is.

<Question number={1} title="A crystal of menthol, and one atom" topic="1.1" points={5}>

Menthol, $\ce{C10H20O}$, is a solid that forms colorless crystals. Suppose that one small crystal of pure menthol holds $7.50 \times 10^{19}$ molecules.

In a separate measurement, the atoms of an unknown element are found to have an average mass of $2.022 \times 10^{-22}$ g each.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the mass of the crystal, in milligrams.

<Answer>

The route is molecules to moles to grams, with moles in the middle.

The molar mass of menthol:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&10(12.01) + 20(1.008) \\
&\quad + 16.00 \\
&= 156.26\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The three terms are 120.10, 20.16 and 16.00.

Molecules to moles: divide by Avogadro's number.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{7.50 \times 10^{19}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \\
&= 1.245 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is $1.25 \times 10^{-4}$ mol to three significant figures. Carry the extra digit.

Moles to grams: multiply by the molar mass.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1.245 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad \times 156.26\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= 0.0195\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is **19.5 mg**, with three significant figures, like the count.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the molar mass of the unknown element. Then state the average mass of one of its atoms in amu.

<Answer>

A mole of the element is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. So the mass of a mole is the mass of one atom multiplied by Avogadro's number.

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= 2.022 \times 10^{-22}\ \text{g} \\
&\quad \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1} \\
&= \mathbf{121.8}\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The average mass of one atom is **121.8 amu**. No second calculation is needed: the average mass of one atom in amu is the same number as the molar mass in grams per mole.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify the element. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

**Antimony**, Sb. The periodic table gives its atomic mass as 121.76 amu, which is 121.8 to four significant figures.

No other element comes close. The nearest are tin, at 118.71 amu, and iodine, at 126.90 amu.

</Answer>

</Part>

An answer of 0.0195 g, with its unit, also earns the second point. The count multiplied by the molar mass with no use of Avogadro's number earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for multiplying the mass of one atom by Avogadro's number to get 121.8 g/mol; 1 point for 121.8 amu, the same number as the molar mass. An answer of 121.7 amu, from dividing the mass in grams by $1.661 \times 10^{-24}$ g per amu, also earns the second point. A molar mass of 121.7 g/mol, stated as the same number as that value in amu, also earns the first point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for antimony, justified by its atomic mass on the periodic table, 121.76 amu, matching the value from part (b). The element whose atomic mass matches the student's own value from part (b) earns the point. A name with no justification earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Two setups for one mass" topic="1.1" points={4}>

Glycine, $\ce{C2H5NO2}$, is the simplest of the amino acids that proteins are built from. Its molar mass is 75.07 g/mol.

Two students are asked to find the mass of a sample that holds $3.25 \times 10^{22}$ glycine molecules. Each student's work is shown below as the student wrote it.

**Student 1**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3.25 \times 10^{22} \times 75.07 \\
&= 2.44 \times 10^{24}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Student 2**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{3.25 \times 10^{22}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \\
&= 0.0540\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{0.0540}{75.07} \\
&= 7.19 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each student has made one slip. Neither part below needs the correct mass.

<Part label="a">

Identify the slip in Student 1's work. Then state whether Student 1's answer is too large or too small, and by what factor. Justify the factor.

<Answer>

Student 1 multiplied a number of molecules by the molar mass. A molar mass is the mass of one mole of molecules, not of one molecule. The count has to be changed to moles first, by dividing by Avogadro's number, and Student 1 skipped that step.

The units show it. The setup multiplies molecules by grams per mole:

$$
\text{molecules} \times \frac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}}
$$

Nothing cancels, so the result is not in grams.

The rest of the setup is right. The one thing missing is the division by $6.022 \times 10^{23}$, so Student 1's answer is **too large by a factor of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify the slip in Student 2's work. Then state whether Student 2's answer is too large or too small, and by what factor. Justify the factor.

<Answer>

Student 2's first step is right: the count divided by Avogadro's number is 0.0540 mol. The slip is in the second step. Moles to grams multiplies by the molar mass. Student 2 divided by it, which is the molar mass used upside down, as 1 mol per 75.07 g.

The units show it:

$$
\text{mol} \times \frac{\text{mol}}{\text{g}} = \frac{\text{mol}^2}{\text{g}}
$$

That is not grams.

The correct second step multiplies 0.0540 by 75.07. Student 2 divided 0.0540 by 75.07. So the correct answer is larger than Student 2's by 75.07 twice over, once for the multiplication that was left out and once for the division that was put in:

$$
75.07 \times 75.07 = 5636
$$

Student 2's answer is **too small by a factor of 5636**, about $5.64 \times 10^{3}$.

You did not need it, but the correct second step is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.0540\ \text{mol} \times 75.07\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= 4.05\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the slip: the count of molecules was multiplied by the molar mass without first being changed to moles (or: Avogadro's number was never used; or: the units, written out as molecules × g/mol, do not come out in grams); 1 point for too large by a factor of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$, justified by the missing division by Avogadro's number. "Too large" with no factor does not earn the second point. A factor found by calculating the correct mass, 4.05 g, and comparing it with Student 1's answer ($6.02 \times 10^{23}$ from the printed answers) also earns the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the slip: in the second step the moles were divided by the molar mass where they should be multiplied by it (or: the molar mass is upside down; or: the units, written out as mol × mol/g, do not come out in grams); 1 point for too small by a factor of 75.07 squared: 5636, or about $5.6 \times 10^{3}$ ($5.63 \times 10^{3}$ from the printed answers, or $5.64 \times 10^{3}$), justified by the division in place of a multiplication. A factor of 75.07 does not earn the second point. A factor found by calculating the correct mass, 4.05 g, and comparing it with Student 2's answer also earns the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Ions and atoms in potassium phosphate" topic="1.1" points={5}>

Potassium phosphate, $\ce{K3PO4}$, is one of the phosphates added to some processed foods to control their acidity. It is an ionic solid made of potassium ions, $\ce{K+}$, and phosphate ions, $\ce{PO4^3-}$. A phosphate ion is a single particle: one phosphorus atom and four oxygen atoms joined together, with the charge of 3− on the group as a whole.

A food chemist weighs out 6.37 g of potassium phosphate.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the number of moles of potassium ions and the number of moles of phosphate ions in the sample.

<Answer>

Start with moles of formula units. The molar mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3(39.10) + 30.97 \\
&\quad + 4(16.00) \\
&= 212.27\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The three terms are 117.30, 30.97 and 64.00.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{6.37\ \text{g}}{212.27\ \text{g/mol}} \\
&= 0.03001\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.0300 mol of formula units to three significant figures. Carry the extra digit.

One formula unit is three $\ce{K+}$ ions and one $\ce{PO4^3-}$ ion. So one mole of formula units holds 3 mol of $\ce{K+}$ and 1 mol of $\ce{PO4^3-}$.

Potassium ions:

$$
3 \times 0.03001 = \mathbf{0.0900}\ \text{mol}
$$

Phosphate ions: the same as the formula units, **0.0300 mol**.

A phosphate ion counts once, although it is built of five atoms. The five stay together as one particle.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the number of atoms of each element in the sample, and the total number of atoms. Count each potassium ion as one atom of potassium.

<Answer>

Moles of formula units to a number of formula units:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.03001\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1} \\
&= 1.807 \times 10^{22}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is $1.81 \times 10^{22}$ formula units to three significant figures.

The subscripts say what each formula unit holds: 3 K, 1 P and 4 O. Multiply by each subscript. The last column is the number of atoms in the sample.

| Element | Per unit | Atoms |
|---|---|---|
| K | 3 | $5.42 \times 10^{22}$ |
| P | 1 | $1.81 \times 10^{22}$ |
| O | 4 | $7.23 \times 10^{22}$ |

One formula unit holds $3 + 1 + 4 = 8$ atoms in all.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&8 \times 1.807 \times 10^{22} \\
&= \mathbf{1.45 \times 10^{23}}\ \text{atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the three counts gives $14.46 \times 10^{22}$, the same total to three significant figures.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for 0.0300 mol of formula units, from a molar mass of 212.27 g/mol; 1 point for 0.0900 mol of $\ce{K+}$ and 0.0300 mol of $\ce{PO4^3-}$. Counting the five atoms of a phosphate ion as five ions (0.150 mol of anions) does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for multiplying an amount in moles by Avogadro's number: the formula units, $1.81 \times 10^{22}$, or the moles of each kind of atom in turn; 1 point for the three counts, $5.42 \times 10^{22}$ K, $1.81 \times 10^{22}$ P and $7.23 \times 10^{22}$ O; 1 point for the total, $1.45 \times 10^{23}$ atoms, from 8 atoms in a formula unit or from adding the three counts. Counts of $5.43 \times 10^{22}$ K and $7.24 \times 10^{22}$ O, from the rounded $1.81 \times 10^{22}$ formula units, also earn the point for the three counts. Counts that follow correctly from the student's own amount in part (a) earn all three points of part (b).</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Four samples, four units" topic="1.1" points={5}>

Four samples are set out on a laboratory bench. The table gives the substance in each sample and how much of it there is. Each amount is in a different unit.

| Sample | Substance | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| W | zinc, $\ce{Zn}$ | 4.15 g |
| X | bromine, $\ce{Br2}$ | 0.0425 mol |
| Y | citric acid, $\ce{C6H8O7}$ | 925 mg |
| Z | sulfur, $\ce{S8}$ | $5.50 \times 10^{21}$ molecules |

The amount of bromine is in moles of $\ce{Br2}$ molecules. Ordinary solid sulfur is made of $\ce{S8}$ molecules, each a ring of eight sulfur atoms.

<Part label="a">

Rank the four samples from the most atoms to the fewest. Justify your ranking with a calculation for each sample.

<Answer>

Put all four samples on one scale: moles of atoms. The number of atoms is the moles of atoms multiplied by Avogadro's number, and that factor is the same for every sample. So the sample with the most moles of atoms has the most atoms.

**Sample W, zinc.** The particles of zinc metal are single atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&4.15\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{65.38\ \text{g}} \\
&= 0.0635\ \text{mol of atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sample X, bromine.** Each $\ce{Br2}$ molecule is two atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2 \times 0.0425\ \text{mol} \\
&= 0.0850\ \text{mol of atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sample Y, citric acid.** The molar mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&6(12.01) + 8(1.008) \\
&\quad + 7(16.00) \\
&= 192.12\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mass is 925 mg, which is 0.925 g.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.925\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{192.12\ \text{g}} \\
&= 4.815 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is moles of molecules. Each molecule holds $6 + 8 + 7 = 21$ atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&21 \times 4.815 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \\
&= 0.101\ \text{mol of atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sample Z, sulfur.** Molecules to moles of molecules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{5.50 \times 10^{21}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \\
&= 9.133 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each $\ce{S8}$ molecule is eight atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&8 \times 9.133 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \\
&= 0.0731\ \text{mol of atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The ranking,** from the most atoms to the fewest: **Y, X, Z, W**.

| Sample | Moles of atoms |
|---|---|
| Y | 0.101 |
| X | 0.0850 |
| Z | 0.0731 |
| W | 0.0635 |

Counting molecules instead of atoms would put the samples in the wrong order. Sample Y holds the fewest moles of molecules of the three molecular samples, and the most atoms of all four.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>

A value that differs by 1 in the last figure because an earlier result was rounded ($6.08 \times 10^{22}$ for Y, 0.0730 mol for Z) earns its point. A value for X, Y or Z that leaves out the atoms in each molecule (2, 21 or 8) does not earn that sample's point. A ranking that follows correctly from the student's own four amounts of atoms earns the ranking point. A ranking made from amounts of particles, with each molecule counted as one (W, X, Z, Y), does not earn the ranking point. A ranking with no calculation earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={5} title="Which white solid" topic="1.1" points={4}>

A teacher gives a class a sample of a white ionic solid and two facts about it. Its mass is 3.47 g, and it holds $7.83 \times 10^{21}$ formula units.

The solid is one of three compounds: rubidium chloride, $\ce{RbCl}$; barium carbonate, $\ce{BaCO3}$; or rubidium sulfate, $\ce{Rb2SO4}$.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the molar mass of the solid.

<Answer>

A molar mass is grams per mole. The grams are given. The moles come from the count of formula units.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{7.83 \times 10^{21}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \\
&= 0.01300\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide the mass by the moles.

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \frac{3.47\ \text{g}}{0.01300\ \text{mol}} \\
&= \mathbf{267}\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Three significant figures, like the mass and the count.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify the solid. Justify your choice by calculating the molar mass of each of the three compounds.

<Answer>

Rubidium chloride, $\ce{RbCl}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&85.47 + 35.45 \\
&= 120.92\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Barium carbonate, $\ce{BaCO3}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&137.33 + 12.01 \\
&\quad + 3(16.00) \\
&= 197.34\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Rubidium sulfate, $\ce{Rb2SO4}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(85.47) + 32.06 \\
&\quad + 4(16.00) \\
&= 267.00\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The solid is **rubidium sulfate**, $\ce{Rb2SO4}$. Its molar mass matches the 267 g/mol from part (a).

The other two do not come close. Barium carbonate is 70 g/mol too low and rubidium chloride is 146 g/mol too low. A value good to three significant figures cannot be off by that much.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for 0.0130 mol of formula units, the count divided by Avogadro's number; 1 point for 267 g/mol, the mass divided by the moles. Finding the mass of one formula unit, $4.43 \times 10^{-22}$ g, and multiplying it by Avogadro's number is equally sound and earns both points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the three molar masses, 120.92, 197.34 and 267.00 g/mol; 1 point for rubidium sulfate, chosen because its molar mass matches the value from part (a). The compound that best matches the student's own value from part (a) earns the second point. A compound named with no molar masses shown earns 0 for the part.</RubricLine>

## Part B: Mass spectra and isotopes

The figure is the mass spectrum of a pure sample of one element. Each peak is drawn at an isotope's mass, to the nearest amu. The table under the figure gives each isotope's measured mass and its relative abundance, as a percent of the atoms.

| Mass (amu) | Relative abundance (%) |

| 73.9212 | 36.50 |
| 75.9214 | 7.73 |

<Part label="a">

Use the table to calculate the average atomic mass. Then use the periodic table to identify the element.

<Answer>

Divide each percent by 100 to get the fraction of the atoms: 0.2057, 0.2745, 0.0775, 0.3650 and 0.0773. Multiply each mass by its fraction, then add the products. The values in the table are in amu.

| Mass | Product |
|---|---|
| 69.9242 | 14.383 |
| 71.9221 | 19.743 |
| 72.9235 | 5.652 |
| 73.9212 | 26.981 |
| 75.9214 | 5.869 |

The products add up to 72.628, so the average atomic mass is **72.63 amu**.

On the periodic table that is **germanium**, Ge, 72.63.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Suppose the heights had been lost, and only the positions of the five peaks were known. Use the lightest and the heaviest mass in the table, with the periodic table, to determine which elements the sample could then be. Explain why the positions and the periodic table cannot decide between them.

<Answer>

**Germanium or arsenic.** An average atomic mass is a weighted average, so whatever the heights are, it lies between the lightest mass and the heaviest: between 69.9242 amu and 75.9214 amu. The periodic table has two atomic masses in that range, germanium's 72.63 and arsenic's 74.92. Gallium's 69.72 is just below the lightest peak and selenium's 78.97 is above the heaviest, so neither is possible.

The positions cannot decide between the two, because the average also depends on the heights: on how the atoms are shared among the five isotopes. A peak's position gives an isotope's mass. Only its height gives that isotope's share of the atoms.

For example, put 1 percent of the atoms at each of the three lightest masses, 43.6 percent at 73.9212 amu and 53.4 percent at 75.9214 amu. Weighted as in part (a), the five products are 0.699, 0.719, 0.729, 32.230 and 40.542, and they add up to 74.919: arsenic's 74.92 amu. With the heights in the figure, the same five positions give 72.63 amu.

(Natural arsenic is in fact all one isotope, so it could not give five peaks. Nothing in the positions or on the periodic table says so.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student takes the masses from the figure's axis, 70, 72, 73, 74 and 76 amu, in place of the measured masses, and uses the abundances in the table. Her average is 72.71 amu. Use the table to explain why her average is higher than the one in part (a). Then state whether her average still identifies the element, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

Every measured mass is a little below its whole number on the axis: 70, 72, 73, 74 or 76. The table gives each measured mass and how far below its whole number it is, in amu.

| Mass | Below by |
|---|---|
| 69.9242 | 0.0758 |
| 71.9221 | 0.0779 |
| 72.9235 | 0.0765 |
| 73.9212 | 0.0788 |
| 75.9214 | 0.0786 |

Each whole number is about 0.08 amu too high. So a weighted average of the whole numbers is about 0.08 amu too high, whatever the abundances are:

$$
72.71 - 72.63 = 0.08
$$

**Her average still identifies germanium.** It is 0.08 amu from germanium's 72.63 and more than 2 amu from the nearest other values, gallium's 69.72 and arsenic's 74.92.

It does not match the periodic table to two decimal places, and it should not be expected to. An isotope's mass is close to its mass number, the whole number on the axis, and is almost never exactly equal to it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={7} title="Abundances from an average" topic="1.2" points={4}>

Iridium is a hard metal used for the tips of long-life spark plugs. Natural iridium is a mixture of two isotopes.

| Isotope | Mass (amu) |
|---|---|
| iridium-191 | 190.9606 |
| iridium-193 | 192.9629 |

The periodic table gives iridium's average atomic mass as 192.22 amu.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the percent of iridium atoms that are iridium-191 and the percent that are iridium-193.

<Answer>

Let $x$ be the fraction of the atoms that are iridium-193. The two fractions add up to 1, so the fraction that is iridium-191 is $1 - x$. The weighted average of the two masses must come out as 192.22 amu:

$$
\begin{aligned}
192.22 &= 190.9606(1 - x) \\
&\quad + 192.9629x
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
192.22 &= 190.9606 \\
&\quad + 2.0023x
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \frac{192.22 - 190.9606}{2.0023} \\
&= \frac{1.2594}{2.0023} \\
&= 0.629
\end{aligned}
$$

The subtraction is good only to the hundredths place, 1.26, so $x$ is kept to three significant figures; part (b) tests how far the third can be trusted.

**62.9 percent** of the atoms are iridium-193, and the rest, **37.1 percent**, are iridium-191.

Check: 192.22 amu is above the midpoint of the two masses, 191.96 amu, so the heavier isotope has to be the more abundant one. It is.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The periodic table rounds the average to two decimal places. Repeat the calculation for iridium-193 with an average of 192.21 amu. Then use your two results to explain how many figures of your answer to part (a) can be trusted.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \frac{192.21 - 190.9606}{2.0023} \\
&= \frac{1.2494}{2.0023} \\
&= 0.624
\end{aligned}
$$

With an average of 192.21 amu, **62.4 percent** of the atoms would be iridium-193.

A change of 0.01 amu in the average, one unit in the last figure the periodic table gives, moves the answer by 0.5 percentage point. So the tenths digit of 62.9 percent cannot be trusted.

Rounded to two figures the two results are 63 and 62, so a full 0.01 amu moves even the second figure. The table's rounding hides at most half that, about 0.25 percentage point either way: an average of 192.215 amu gives 62.65 percent, and 192.225 amu gives 63.15 percent. So the answer is good to **two figures at best**: about 63 percent iridium-193 and about 37 percent iridium-191.

The reason is the small gap between the two masses, 2.0023 amu. The answer is the distance of the average from the lighter mass divided by that gap, and 0.01 amu is 0.5 percent of the gap.

The measured abundance of iridium-193 is 62.7 percent, which lies between the two results.

</Answer>

<Question number={9} title="Three claims about one spectrum" topic="1.2" points={6}>

A sample of sulfur from a school's supply room is put through a mass spectrometer. The table gives its mass spectrum, with the masses rounded to two decimal places.

| Mass (amu) | Percent of atoms |
|---|---|

| 32.97 | 0.75 |

Three students each make a claim about these data.

- **Luis:** "Four peaks means four elements. This cannot be sulfur alone: it is a mixture."
- **Mei:** "The peak at 35.97 amu is only 0.01 percent of the atoms. Leave its row out of the sum and the average comes out the same."

<Part label="a">

Evaluate Dana's claim. Justify your answer with a calculation.

<Answer>

**Dana's method is not sound.** Adding the masses and dividing by 4 treats the four isotopes as equally common. They are not: 94.99 percent of the atoms are at 31.97 amu, so the average has to lie close to 32 amu, far below 33.72 amu.

Weight each mass by its fraction of the atoms, the percent divided by 100, and add. The masses are in amu.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(0.9499)(31.97) &= 30.368 \\
(0.0075)(32.97) &= 0.247 \\
(0.0425)(33.97) &= 1.444 \\
(0.0001)(35.97) &= 0.004 \\
\text{sum} &= 32.063
\end{aligned}
$$

The average atomic mass is **32.06 amu**. Dana's 33.72 amu is 1.66 amu too high.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Evaluate Luis's claim. Explain what the four peaks stand for, and give one piece of evidence from the data that bears on whether the sample is sulfur alone.

<Answer>

**Luis is wrong.** Four peaks do not mean four elements: one element gives a peak for each of its isotopes. Isotopes are atoms with the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons, so they have different masses.

Sulfur's atomic number is 16. The four masses lie close to the mass numbers 32, 33, 34 and 36. If the sample is sulfur, the four peaks are atoms with 16 protons and with 16, 17, 18 and 20 neutrons.

The evidence: the weighted average of the four peaks, 32.06 amu from part (a), is the periodic table's value for sulfur. That is what a sample of sulfur alone should give, and nothing in the data points to a second element.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Evaluate Mei's claim about the peak at 35.97 amu, using numbers. Then determine whether the same shortcut, leaving a row out of the sum, would work for the peak at 32.97 amu.

<Answer>

**Mei is right about this peak, to the two decimal places the periodic table gives.** The term she leaves out is small:

$$
(0.0001)(35.97) = 0.0036
$$

Without it, the other three terms add up to 32.059, which is still 32.06 amu. Her claim is not exact, though. The sum does fall, by about 0.004 amu, and that would show in an answer kept to three decimal places.

**The same shortcut does not work for the peak at 32.97 amu**, although 0.75 percent also looks small. Its term is 0.247 amu. Leave that row out and the sum falls from 32.063 to 31.816, which is 31.82 amu: about 0.25 amu too low.

What decides it is the size of the term, the fraction times the mass, against the last decimal place you keep. A term of 0.247 amu always changes the hundredths place. A term of 0.0036 amu changes it only when the sum sits just above a rounding boundary, and here it does not: 32.063 and 32.059 both round to 32.06.

Leaving a row out also leaves fractions that add up to less than 1: 0.9999 without the last row, and 0.9925 without the second. Dividing each sum by the fractions that are left is a different calculation from Mei's shortcut. It gives 32.06 amu both times (32.0625 and 32.056): the last row holds very few of the atoms, and 32.97 amu is close to the average.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Part C: Composition and formulas

Five questions on composition by mass: what it fixes, what it leaves open, and how far a measurement can be trusted. You will compare samples, find a formula from a gain in mass, set two compounds of the same elements side by side, and weigh two claims against the data.

<Question number={11} title="Four samples, one table" topic="1.3" points={5}>

A laboratory analyzes four samples. Each sample contains carbon and hydrogen and no other element. The table gives the mass of each sample and the mass of carbon found in it.

| Sample | Mass of sample (g) | Mass of carbon (g) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 2.400 | 2.190 |
| B | 1.600 | 1.460 |
| C | 3.000 | 2.698 |
| D | 1.280 | 1.168 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the mass percent of carbon in each sample.

<Answer>

Divide the mass of carbon by the mass of the sample.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{A:}\quad \frac{2.190}{2.400} &= 0.9125 \\
\text{B:}\quad \frac{1.460}{1.600} &= 0.9125 \\
\text{C:}\quad \frac{2.698}{3.000} &= 0.8993 \\
\text{D:}\quad \frac{1.168}{1.280} &= 0.9125
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply each fraction by 100 to make it a percent. Sample A is **91.25** percent carbon, B is **91.25** percent, C is **89.93** percent and D is **91.25** percent.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State which samples could all be the same pure compound, and which of the four cannot be that compound. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

**A, B and D could be the same pure compound. C cannot be that compound.**

In any pure sample of a compound, the elements are present in the same ratio by mass. That is the law of definite proportions. So every pure sample of one compound shows the same mass percent of carbon, whatever its size.

A, B and D all show 91.25 percent carbon, although their masses differ. C shows 89.93 percent. Its composition is different, so it cannot be the same pure compound as the other three. C could be a different compound or a mixture. The table cannot say which.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student writes: "A, B and D have the same composition, so they are the same compound." The data are correct. Explain why the data do not prove the student's conclusion, by describing one other possibility that fits them.

<Answer>

Samples of one compound must show the same composition. But samples that are not the same compound can show it too, so a match is not proof. Either of these possibilities fits the data.

**Different compounds with the same ratio of atoms.** For a pure compound, composition by mass gives only the empirical formula, the lowest whole-number ratio of atoms. Compounds whose molecules hold different numbers of atoms in that same ratio have the same mass percents. Toluene, $\ce{C7H8}$, has this composition. Its molar mass is 92.13 g/mol, and its percent of carbon is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{7(12.01)}{92.13} \times 100 \\
&= 91.25\%
\end{aligned}
$$

A compound with the formula $\ce{C14H16}$ has twice as many atoms of each kind in a molecule and a molar mass of 184.27 g/mol. Its percent of carbon is the same:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{14(12.01)}{184.27} \times 100 \\
&= 91.25\%
\end{aligned}
$$

**A mixture that happens to match.** A mixture of two substances, one with more than 91.25 percent carbon and one with less, could hold them in just the proportions that give 91.25 percent overall.

So the table can show that C is different from the others. It cannot show that A, B and D are the same.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Name one further measurement, made on each of A, B and D, that would test the student's conclusion. State a result of that measurement that would show the conclusion is wrong.

<Answer>

Measure the **molar mass** of each of the three.

Compounds that share an empirical formula but have different numbers of atoms in a molecule have different molar masses: 92.13 g/mol for $\ce{C7H8}$ against 184.27 g/mol for $\ce{C14H16}$, for example. If **two of the samples have different molar masses**, they are not the same compound, and the student's conclusion is wrong.

If all three are pure compounds with the same molar mass, they share a molecular formula as well. That supports the student's conclusion, but it is still not proof: different compounds can share a molecular formula when their atoms are joined in different ways. Telling such compounds apart takes another property of each pure substance, such as its boiling point.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={12} title="Bismuth heated in air" topic="1.3" points={5}>

&= \mathbf{2.508}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m(\ce{O}) &= 21.258 - 20.970 \\
&= \mathbf{0.288}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

\end{aligned}
$$

\frac{0.0180}{0.01200} = 1.50
$$

<Question number={13} title="Two oxides of manganese" topic="1.3" points={5}>

Manganese and oxygen form more than one compound. A chemist analyzes a pure sample of each of two of them. Each sample contains manganese and oxygen and nothing else.

| Compound | Mass of sample (g) | Mass of manganese (g) |
|---|---|---|
| I | 2.500 | 1.580 |
| II | 3.000 | 2.088 |

<Part label="a">

For each compound, calculate the mass of oxygen that is combined with 1.000 g of manganese.

<Answer>

The oxygen in each sample is the part that is not manganese.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{I:}\quad & 2.500 - 1.580 \\
&= 0.920\ \text{g of O} \\
\text{II:}\quad & 3.000 - 2.088 \\
&= 0.912\ \text{g of O}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide each by the mass of manganese it is combined with.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{I:}\quad \frac{0.920}{1.580} &= \mathbf{0.582}\ \text{g} \\
\text{II:}\quad \frac{0.912}{2.088} &= \mathbf{0.437}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each gram of manganese is combined with 0.582 g of oxygen in compound I and with 0.437 g of oxygen in compound II. The oxygen masses are differences known to three significant figures, so the answers have three.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Divide the larger of your two answers by the smaller, and write the result as a ratio of small whole numbers. Explain what this ratio shows about the numbers of atoms in the two compounds.

<Answer>

$$
\frac{0.582}{0.437} = 1.33
$$

The value 1.33 is four thirds, so the two masses of oxygen stand in the ratio **4 to 3**. (The unrounded masses from part (a) give 1.33 as well.)

The average mass of a manganese atom is the same in both compounds, so 1.000 g of manganese is the same number of manganese atoms in each. The average mass of an oxygen atom is the same in both too, so the mass of oxygen combined with that gram is in proportion to the number of oxygen atoms.

So the ratio is a ratio of atoms: **for the same number of manganese atoms, compound I holds 4 oxygen atoms for every 3 that compound II holds.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Determine the empirical formula of each compound.

<Answer>

**Compound I**, from its masses:

$$
\begin{aligned}

&= 0.02876\ \text{mol} \\
n(\ce{O}) &= \frac{0.920}{16.00} \\
&= 0.0575\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{0.0575}{0.02876} = 2.00
$$

**Compound II**, from the ratio in part (b). For each manganese atom, compound II has three quarters of the oxygen that compound I has.

$$
2 \times \frac{3}{4} = 1.5

\end{aligned}

$$
\frac{0.0570}{0.03801} = 1.50
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={14} title="A percent and an empirical formula" topic="1.3" points={4}>

The analysis of a colorless liquid gives its empirical formula, $\ce{CH3O}$. A second analysis finds that the liquid is 38.70 percent carbon by mass.

A student uses the second result like this: "One mole of the compound holds 12.01 g of carbon, and that is 38.70 percent of the molar mass. So I can find the molar mass by division."

$$
\frac{12.01}{0.3870} = 31.03\ \text{g/mol}
$$

The student goes on: "That is the mass of one $\ce{CH3O}$, so the molecular formula is $\ce{CH3O}$."

<Part label="a">

Decide whether the student's reasoning is sound. Justify your answer, and include a calculation that supports it.

<Answer>

**It is not sound.**

The student's first sentence assumes that a mole of the compound holds 12.01 g of carbon, which means one carbon atom in each molecule. That is the same as assuming that the molecular formula is $\ce{CH3O}$, which is the thing to be found. An empirical formula gives only the ratio of the atoms. As far as these data go, a molecule could hold one carbon atom, or two, or more.

The percent of carbon cannot decide, because every multiple of $\ce{CH3O}$ has the same percent of carbon. Take $\ce{CH3O}$ itself:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&12.01 + 3(1.008) \\
&\quad + 16.00 \\
&= 31.03\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{12.01}{31.03} \times 100 = 38.70\%
$$

Now take twice as many atoms of each kind, $\ce{C2H6O2}$, which adds up to 62.07 g/mol:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{2(12.01)}{62.07} \times 100 \\
&= 38.70\%
\end{aligned}
$$

Doubling the formula doubles the mass of carbon and the mass of the whole by the same factor, so the percent does not change. The same holds for any multiple.

So the second analysis only repeats what the empirical formula already says. The student's 31.03 g/mol is the mass of one $\ce{CH3O}$. The molar mass is a whole number times that, and the percent cannot say which whole number.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A separate measurement gives the liquid's molar mass: 62.1 g/mol. State what this result supplies that the carbon percent could not, and determine the molecular formula.

<Answer>

The molar mass supplies the size of the molecule: **how many $\ce{CH3O}$ units one molecule holds**. A molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. The multiples all have the same mass percents, but each has a different molar mass.

Divide the molar mass by the mass of one $\ce{CH3O}$.

$$
\frac{62.1}{31.03} = 2.00
$$

The multiplier is 2, so the molecular formula is **$\ce{C2H6O2}$**.

Ethylene glycol, the main ingredient of most engine antifreeze, has this molecular formula.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for "not sound", with the unsupported assumption named: one carbon atom in each molecule, or that the molecular formula is the same as the empirical formula; 1 point for a calculation showing that a multiple of $\ce{CH3O}$ has the same percent of carbon as $\ce{CH3O}$ itself (38.70 percent for $\ce{C2H6O2}$ or another multiple, or the multiplier canceling from top and bottom of the fraction, or the student's own division repeated for two carbon atoms: 24.02 divided by 0.3870 gives 62.07 g/mol), so the percent cannot fix the multiplier. A bare "no" earns 0 for the part, and so does an answer that accepts the student's reasoning.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for saying that the molar mass fixes the whole-number multiplier (how many $\ce{CH3O}$ units are in one molecule), which mass percents cannot; 1 point for the multiplier 2 and $\ce{C2H6O2}$, with the working: 62.1 divided by 31.03 is 2.00, or 2 times 31.03 is 62.06, which matches 62.1. The formula with no working (neither of those two) does not earn the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={15} title="Testing a proposed formula" topic="1.3" points={5}>

Uranium forms more than one oxide. Two of them are $\ce{UO2}$ and $\ce{U3O8}$.

A chemist prepares a dark powder that contains uranium and oxygen and nothing else. From the way the powder was made, the chemist proposes that its formula is $\ce{U3O8}$. Two laboratories each measure the mass percent of uranium in the powder. Each reports its result with a range: the true value could lie anywhere in the range.

| Laboratory | Uranium found (%) | Range (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 86.5 | 84.5 to 88.5 |
| 2 | 84.9 | 84.7 to 85.1 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the mass percent of uranium in $\ce{U3O8}$ and in $\ce{UO2}$.

<Answer>

For $\ce{U3O8}$, with three uranium atoms in each formula unit:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3(238.03) + 8(16.00) \\
&= 714.09 + 128.00 \\
&= 842.09\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{714.09}{842.09} \times 100 = \mathbf{84.80}\%
$$

For $\ce{UO2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&238.03 + 2(16.00) \\
&= 270.03\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{238.03}{270.03} \times 100 = \mathbf{88.15}\%
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State what laboratory 1's result shows about the proposed formula, and what it cannot show. Justify your answer with both values from part (a).

<Answer>

**It shows that the proposed formula could be right.** The percent that $\ce{U3O8}$ must have, 84.80, lies inside laboratory 1's range of 84.5 to 88.5. The result does not contradict the proposal.

**It cannot show that the powder is $\ce{U3O8}$ and not $\ce{UO2}$.** The percent for $\ce{UO2}$, 88.15, lies inside the same range. A result that fits both formulas does not favor either one.

This can happen because the two percents are close together, compared with the width of the range:

$$
\begin{aligned}
88.15 - 84.80 &= 3.35 \\
88.5 - 84.5 &= 4.0
\end{aligned}
$$

Two values only 3.35 apart can both fall inside a range 4.0 wide, and here they do. So laboratory 1's result does not support the proposed formula over the other one.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Decide whether laboratory 2's result supports the proposed formula, and whether it rules out $\ce{UO2}$. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

**It supports the proposed formula, and it rules out $\ce{UO2}$ as the powder's formula.**

The percent for $\ce{U3O8}$, 84.80, lies inside laboratory 2's range of 84.7 to 85.1. The percent for $\ce{UO2}$, 88.15, lies far outside it, about 3 above the top of the range. Laboratory 2's range is only 0.4 wide, so two values 3.35 apart can never both fall inside it. This method can tell the two formulas apart, and it comes down on the side of $\ce{U3O8}$.

"Supports" is the right word. A powder of pure $\ce{U3O8}$ must show this percent, but a match in one percent does not prove that the powder is pure $\ce{U3O8}$ (topic 1.4).

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Laboratory 2's method always gives a range that reaches 0.2 below its result and 0.2 above it. State which results from this method would rule the proposed formula out. Explain your answer.

<Answer>

A result rules out $\ce{U3O8}$ when 84.80 lies outside its range, which means the result is more than 0.2 away from 84.80.

$$
\begin{aligned}
84.80 - 0.2 &= 84.6 \\
84.80 + 0.2 &= 85.0
\end{aligned}
$$

**Any result below 84.6 or above 85.0 would rule the proposed formula out.** For results reported to one decimal place, as these are, that means 84.5 or lower, or 85.1 or higher.

A result of 84.6 or 85.0 would put 84.80 right at the edge of its range. That is too close to call, so such a result does not clearly rule the proposed formula out.

</Answer>

## Part D: Mixtures and purity

This part covers topic 1.4: what an elemental analysis can and cannot say about a mixture, and how to tell a pure substance from one. No formula gives a mixture's composition, so every answer here rests on a measurement.

<Question number={16} title="Three results for a two-compound powder" topic="1.4" points={6}>

A powder holds only two compounds, molybdenum(IV) sulfide, $\ce{MoS2}$, and antimony(III) sulfide, $\ce{Sb2S3}$. Their compositions by mass, from their formulas:

| Compound | Mo or Sb (%) | Sulfur (%) |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{MoS2}$ | Mo 59.94 | 40.06 |
| $\ce{Sb2S3}$ | Sb 71.69 | 28.31 |

A lab analyzes one sample of the powder and reports three results:

- **Result 1:** the mass of the sample, 5.00 g
- **Result 2:** the mass of sulfur in the sample, 1.62 g
- **Result 3:** the mass percent of molybdenum in the sample, 21.0 percent

Only one of the three, taken alone with the table, gives the mass percent of each compound in the powder.

<Part label="a">

Identify the result that is enough alone, and explain how it leads to the mass percent of each compound.

<Answer>

**Result 3.** Molybdenum is in $\ce{MoS2}$ only, so all of the sample's molybdenum belongs to $\ce{MoS2}$. Pure $\ce{MoS2}$ is 59.94 percent molybdenum, so the percent of $\ce{MoS2}$ in the powder is the percent of molybdenum divided by 0.5994:

$$
\frac{21.0\%}{0.5994} = 35.0\%
$$

The powder holds only the two compounds, so the rest is $\ce{Sb2S3}$:

$$
100.0\% - 35.0\% = 65.0\%
$$

A mass percent already has the sample's mass inside it, so no second result is needed.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why Result 1 alone does not give the powder's composition, and why Result 2 alone does not.

<Answer>

**Result 1** says how much powder was analyzed and nothing about what is in it. Any blend of the two compounds can weigh 5.00 g.

**Result 2** is a mass of sulfur with no sample mass beside it, and both compounds hold sulfur. The same 1.62 g of sulfur fits samples of quite different makeup. It is the sulfur in 4.04 g of pure $\ce{MoS2}$:

$$
\frac{1.62\ \text{g}}{0.4006} = 4.04\ \text{g}
$$

It is also the sulfur in 5.72 g of pure $\ce{Sb2S3}$:

$$
\frac{1.62\ \text{g}}{0.2831} = 5.72\ \text{g}
$$

And it is the sulfur in a mixture of the two that weighs anything in between. One number cannot say which of these the sample is.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The lab then sends Results 1 and 2 together. Determine whether the two together give the composition of this powder, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

**Yes.** Together they give the mass percent of sulfur:

$$
\frac{1.62\ \text{g}}{5.00\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 32.4\%
$$

In a mixture of just these two compounds, the percent of sulfur is the average of 40.06 and 28.31, weighted by each compound's share of the mass. So every blend has its own percent of sulfur between those two values, and a measured percent picks out one blend.

Here 32.4 is 4.1 above 28.31, and the two values are 11.75 apart:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{32.4 - 28.31}{40.06 - 28.31} \\
&= \frac{4.1}{11.75} = 0.35
\end{aligned}
$$

About 35 percent of the powder is $\ce{MoS2}$, in agreement with Result 3.

The shared element works here for two reasons: the powder holds nothing else, and the two sulfur values are far apart. With a third substance in the powder, one percent of sulfur could not be shared out among three.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A second powder holds only copper(I) sulfide, $\ce{Cu2S}$, and copper(II) oxide, $\ce{CuO}$.

| Compound | Copper (%) | Other (%) |

The lab reports a mass percent to the nearest 0.1. Explain why the mass percent of copper cannot give this powder's composition, and identify a result that can, with your reason.

Compare the first powder. Its two sulfur values are 11.75 apart, so a step of 0.1 there is under 1 percent of the way from one compound to the other, and the reading pins the blend down closely.

For this powder, follow an element that only one compound holds. **The mass percent of sulfur** will do: sulfur is in $\ce{Cu2S}$ only, so the percent of sulfur divided by 0.2014 is the percent of $\ce{Cu2S}$, just as Result 3 worked for the first powder. The mass percent of oxygen would do as well, since oxygen is in $\ce{CuO}$ only: divide it by 0.2011 for the percent of $\ce{CuO}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

If every formula unit were $\ce{PbO}$, there would be exactly 1 oxygen atom for every lead atom. The powder has 1.617. The oxygen beyond 1 comes from the $\ce{PbO2}$, one extra atom for each formula unit of it:

$$
1.617 - 1 = 0.617
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}

0.383 \times 223.20 &= 85.5\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{147.6}{147.6 + 85.5} = 0.633
$$

That is the 63.3 percent by mass.

</Answer>

$$

A mixture of just these two oxides cannot be richer in lead than that, and 93.60 percent is.

So the jar holds something with a larger share of lead than $\ce{PbO}$ has. Lead metal is one substance that would do it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={18} title="A drum labeled potassium nitrate" topic="1.4" points={5}>

<Part label="a">

A blend that is 29.7 percent $\ce{KCl}$ and 70.3 percent $\ce{KBr}$ by mass holds no potassium nitrate at all. Calculate its mass percent of potassium, and state what the result means for the technician's conclusion.

<Answer>

0.703 \times 32.86 &= 23.10

$$

</Answer>

Identify one more elemental analysis of the same sample that would tell pure potassium nitrate from the blend in part (a), and state what that analysis would show for pure potassium nitrate and for the blend.

<Answer>

**The mass percent of nitrogen.** Pure $\ce{KNO3}$, with a molar mass of 101.11 g/mol, is 13.86 percent nitrogen:

$$
\frac{14.01}{101.11} \times 100\% = 13.86\%
$$

The blend holds no nitrogen at all, so it would read zero.

Oxygen would serve in the same way: it is in $\ce{KNO3}$ and not in the blend. Chlorine or bromine would serve the other way around: there is none in $\ce{KNO3}$ and plenty in the blend.

A pure compound has to match its formula in every element, not in one.

</Answer>

</Part>

The technician then takes five samples from different places in this drum, Drum 1, and five from a second drum with the same label, Drum 2. The mass percent of potassium in each sample:

| Sample | Drum 1 | Drum 2 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 38.7 | 36.9 |
| 2 | 38.6 | 38.7 |

For each drum, state whether its five samples could all be one pure substance, and justify your answer from the data.

<Answer>

**Drum 2 cannot be one pure substance.** Every sample of a pure substance has the same composition. The readings from Drum 2 run from 36.9 to 40.3, a spread of 3.4. The method can put two readings of the same material no more than 0.2 apart, since each is good to 0.1. So the composition really does change from place to place in the drum. That means two or more substances in proportions that vary: a mixture, and an unevenly blended one. Notice sample 2, which reads 38.7. Taken alone, it would have passed the drum.

**Drum 1 could be one pure substance.** Its readings run from 38.6 to 38.8. All five are within 0.1 of 38.7, which is no more than the method's own scatter. So the data are consistent with one substance that is 38.7 percent potassium, as potassium nitrate is.

They do not prove it. An evenly blended mixture would also give the same reading in every sample, and part (a) showed that a blend can match the formula.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={19} title="Tin atoms and lead atoms in solder" topic="1.4" points={5}>

Solder is the soft, easily melted metal that joins the parts of electric circuits. For many years the usual solder was made of tin and lead.

An analysis of a piece of one tin and lead solder finds 2.97 tin atoms for every lead atom. Treat the piece as tin and lead only.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the mass percent of tin in this solder, and the mass of tin in a 5.00 g piece of it.

<Answer>

A ratio of atoms is a ratio of moles. Take an amount of solder that holds 1 mol of lead atoms. It holds 2.97 mol of tin atoms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m(\ce{Sn}) &= 2.97 \times 118.71 \\
&= 352.6\ \text{g} \\
m(\ce{Pb}) &= 207.2\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

The tin's share of the mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{352.6}{352.6 + 207.2} \\
&= \frac{352.6}{559.8} = 0.630
\end{aligned}
$$

The solder is **63.0 percent tin** by mass. In a 5.00 g piece:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.630 \times 5.00\ \text{g} \\
&= \mathbf{3.15}\ \text{g Sn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Tin is about three quarters of the atoms, 2.97 out of every 3.97, but less than two thirds of the mass, because a lead atom is heavier than a tin atom. A share of the atoms is not a share of the mass.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student rounds the ratio to 3 and writes: "Three tin atoms for each lead atom, so solder is a compound with the formula $\ce{Sn3Pb}$." Explain why a ratio of atoms found in one piece, even a ratio of exactly 3 to 1, would not show that solder is a compound.

<Answer>

Atoms can be counted in any sample. An analysis gives a ratio of atoms for a mixture as readily as for a compound. And nothing keeps a mixture's ratio away from whole numbers: tin and lead melt together in any proportion, 3 to 1 included.

What marks a compound is that its ratio is **fixed**. Its atoms are joined in one pattern, so the ratio cannot be anything else. One piece gives one ratio. It cannot show whether any other ratio was possible.

So the student's rounding is fair, but the conclusion does not follow from it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A piece of a second tin and lead solder is found to hold 2.62 tin atoms for every lead atom. Explain what this second piece adds to the evidence about whether solder is a compound or a mixture. Include a reason why the two pieces are not simply two different compounds of tin and lead.

<Answer>

**Solder is not one compound.** A compound has one fixed ratio of atoms, the same in every sample. If solder were $\ce{Sn3Pb}$, the second piece would also hold 3 tin atoms for every lead atom. It holds 2.62.

Could the two pieces be two different compounds of tin and lead? Two elements can form more than one compound. But each compound has one fixed whole-number ratio, and from one compound to the next the ratio jumps, with nothing in between.

A ratio of 2.62 to 1 does not come out in small whole numbers. Multiply it by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7, as you would to clear a fraction in topic 1.3. The results are 5.24, 7.86, 10.48, 13.10, 15.72 and 18.34, and none is within a few hundredths of a whole number. The first fit is 21 to 8 (2.625). Large enough whole numbers will fit any measured ratio, so a fit that needs numbers this large is weak evidence of a formula.

Tin and lead also melt together in any proportion, so the ratio in a solder can be moved up or down by as little as you like. No compound allows that.

So the second piece is best read as a **mixture**: tin atoms and lead atoms, two piles, in a ratio that no formula fixes. The 2.97 of the first piece is one more such ratio, and it only happens to fall near 3.

</Answer>

</Part>
