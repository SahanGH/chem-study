There are ten ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions on the same skill, with no help. Work all four before you check any of them. If you miss one, read the worked example again, then work the question again from the start. Reading the answer a second time does not count.

**Two habits that prevent most lost points in this unit.** Convert a mass to **moles** before you compare amounts or count particles: a common Unit 1 error is a comparison made in grams that should have been made in moles. And when you explain a periodic trend, name **nuclear charge, distance and shielding**. An explanation that rests on what an atom "wants" earns no credit on the AP exam.

You will need atomic masses from the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/).

## Ladder 1: Moles, mass and particles

The mole is a counting unit: one mole is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles. The molar mass in g/mol is the bridge between the mass you can weigh and the number of particles you cannot count.

<Worked title="Mass to moles to atoms">

How many moles, and how many atoms, are in 12.5 g of calcium?

**Mass to moles:** divide by the molar mass.

$$
n = \frac{12.5}{40.08} = \mathbf{0.312}\ \text{mol}
$$

**Moles to atoms:** multiply by Avogadro's number.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.3119 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \\
&= \mathbf{1.88 \times 10^{23}}\ \text{atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sense check:** 12.5 g is well under the mass of one mole (40.08 g), so the answer must be under one mole. It is.

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Molar mass of a compound

A subscript outside parentheses multiplies *everything* inside them. That single rule is where most molar-mass errors come from.

<Worked title="A compound with parentheses">

Find the molar mass of $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$.

**Read the formula first.** There is one calcium, and *two* nitrate groups. Each nitrate is one N and three O, so the compound holds 2 N and 6 O, not 1 N and 3 O.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&40.08 + 2[14.01 + 3(16.00)] \\
&= 40.08 + 2(62.01) \\
&= \mathbf{164.10}\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The trap:** forgetting to distribute the 2 gives 102.09 g/mol, and every later answer built on it is wrong.

</Worked>

## Ladder 3: Mass spectra and average atomic mass

A mass spectrum plots mass on the horizontal axis and abundance on the vertical. Peak *position* gives isotope mass; peak *height* gives how common that isotope is. The tabulated atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average of the isotope masses.

<Worked title="Two isotopes to one atomic mass">

Copper has two naturally occurring isotopes: $\ce{^{63}Cu}$, 62.930 amu, 69.17%; and $\ce{^{65}Cu}$, 64.928 amu, 30.83%. Find the average atomic mass.

**Weight each mass by its fractional abundance and add:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(62.930)(0.6917) \\
&\quad + (64.928)(0.3083) \\
&= 43.53 + 20.02 \\
&= \mathbf{63.55}\ \text{amu}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sense check:** the answer must land between the two isotope masses, and closer to the *more abundant* one. 63.55 amu sits nearer 62.930 amu, as it must with 69% of atoms being the lighter isotope.

**The trap:** averaging 62.930 and 64.928 without weighting gives 63.93 amu. That is wrong, because it assumes equal abundances.

</Worked>

## Ladder 4: Percent composition

Percent composition asks what fraction of a compound's mass each element contributes. Work from *one mole* of the compound.

<Worked title="Percent composition of a nitrate">

Find the percent by mass of each element in $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$ ($M = 164.10$ g/mol).

**Mass of each element in one mole:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Ca} &= 40.08\ \text{g} \\
\text{N} &= 2(14.01) = 28.02\ \text{g} \\
\text{O} &= 6(16.00) = 96.00\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\%\text{Ca} &= \frac{40.08}{164.10} \times 100 = \mathbf{24.4\%} \\
\%\text{N} &= \frac{28.02}{164.10} \times 100 = \mathbf{17.1\%} \\
\%\text{O} &= \frac{96.00}{164.10} \times 100 = \mathbf{58.5\%}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sense check:** the three must sum to 100%.

$$
24.4\% + 17.1\% + 58.5\% = 100.0\%\ \checkmark
$$

</Worked>

## Ladder 5: Empirical and molecular formulas

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number *ratio* of atoms. The molecular formula is the actual count. Percent composition gives you the first. The second needs the molar mass as well.

<Worked title="Percentages to a molecular formula">

A compound is 40.00% C, 6.71% H and 53.29% O by mass, with $M = 180.2$ g/mol. Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

**Step 1: assume 100.0 g,** so the percentages become masses in grams.

**Step 2: convert each to moles:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{C:}\quad \frac{40.00}{12.01} &= 3.331\ \text{mol} \\
\text{H:}\quad \frac{6.71}{1.008} &= 6.657\ \text{mol} \\
\text{O:}\quad \frac{53.29}{16.00} &= 3.331\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3: divide by the smallest** (3.331): C 1.00, H 2.00, O 1.00, so the empirical formula is **$\ce{CH2O}$**.

**Step 4: scale to the molar mass.** Empirical mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 \\
&= 30.03\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide the molar mass by it:

$$
180.2/30.03 = \mathbf{6.00}
$$

$$
\ce{CH2O} \times 6 = \ce{C6H12O6}
$$

**The trap:** stopping at $\ce{CH2O}$. That ratio is shared by formaldehyde ($\ce{CH2O}$), acetic acid ($\ce{C2H4O2}$) and glucose ($\ce{C6H12O6}$). The molar mass is what separates them.

</Worked>

<Answer>

## Ladder 6: Composition of mixtures

A mixture has no formula. Its composition is whatever was put in, so it is described by percent by mass, and its components keep their own properties, which is how they can be separated. Separating mixtures is the subject of topic 3.9; this ladder previews it.

<Worked title="Separating and weighing">

An 8.00 g mixture of $\ce{KCl}$ and sand is stirred with water, filtered, and the filtrate, the liquid that passes through the filter, evaporated to dryness, leaving 4.60 g of $\ce{KCl}$. Find the percent by mass of $\ce{KCl}$, and explain why the method works.

$$
\frac{4.60}{8.00} \times 100 = \mathbf{57.5\%}\ \text{KCl}
$$

**Why it works:** $\ce{KCl}$ dissolves in water: its $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions separate and spread out among the water molecules. Single ions are far smaller than the pores of the filter paper, so they pass through. Sand does not dissolve: its atoms are joined by covalent bonds that run through the whole grain, and water cannot break them, so the grains, far too big for the pores, stay on the filter paper. The difference in **solubility** is the property being used, and filtration separates the two. (Why one solid dissolves in water and another does not is taken further in topics 3.2 and 3.10.)

</Worked>

Name a technique that separates two liquids that have mixed completely, and the property it exploits.

<Answer>

Distillation; the difference in boiling point.

Why can a mixture's composition vary while a compound's cannot?

<Answer>

The parts of a mixture are not bonded to each other in a fixed ratio.

A mixture's components are only physically combined in whatever proportion was used, so the proportion can be anything. A compound's atoms are chemically bonded in a fixed whole-number ratio, so its composition is fixed by its formula.

## Ladder 7: Electron configurations

Fill the lowest-energy subshells first: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. You do not have to memorize that order, because the periodic table is laid out in it: read across each row, top row first. Capacities: an s subshell holds up to 2 electrons, a p subshell up to 6 and a d subshell up to 10. The superscripts must sum to the electron count.

<Worked title="An atom and an ion">

Write the configuration of phosphorus ($Z = 15$) and of the sulfide ion, $\ce{S^2-}$.

**Phosphorus, 15 electrons:**

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³

$$
2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 3 = 15\ \checkmark
$$

**Sulfide.** Neutral sulfur has 16 electrons; the $2-$ charge means it has *gained* two, giving 18:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶

The superscripts sum to 18. ✓

**The trap:** writing the ion's configuration as though it were the atom's. Always work out the ion's electron count from its charge: negative means more electrons, positive means fewer. For a positive ion, write the atom's configuration, then take the electrons from the highest-numbered shell.

</Worked>

## Ladder 8: Reading a photoelectron spectrum

A photoelectron spectrum has one peak per occupied *subshell*. Peak **height** gives the number of electrons in that subshell; peak **position** gives their binding energy. Summing the heights gives the number of electrons. For a neutral atom that is the atomic number, which identifies the element. Every spectrum in this ladder is a neutral atom's.

<Worked title="Peaks to an element">

A spectrum shows five peaks of relative height 2, 2, 6, 2 and 3, running from highest binding energy to lowest. Identify the element and explain which peak is highest in binding energy.

**Sum the heights** to count the electrons:

$$
2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 3 = 15
$$

So $Z = 15$: **phosphorus**, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³.

**Highest binding energy: the first peak, 1s.** Those electrons lie closest to the nucleus and have no electrons inside them to shield the nucleus, so they feel the largest effective nuclear charge over the shortest distance. The attraction is strongest, so the most energy is needed to remove one.

**Lowest binding energy: the 3p peak of height 3.** Outermost and most shielded, so held least tightly. Together with the two 3s electrons, these are phosphorus's five valence electrons, the ones that take part in bonding.

</Worked>

More protons pull the 1s electrons harder.

In both atoms the 1s electrons are in the first shell, with no electrons inside them to shield the nucleus, so the attraction they feel is set almost entirely by the number of protons. More protons means a larger effective nuclear charge acting on the same shell, hence a stronger attraction and a higher binding energy.

## Ladder 9: Periodic trends, explained with Coulomb's law

Across period 2 or 3, each element has one more proton than the last, and each added electron goes into the *same* shell: nuclear charge rises, shielding barely changes, so the atom generally contracts and holds its valence electrons more tightly. Down a group, a new shell is added: distance and shielding both rise, so the atom generally expands and holds its outer electrons more loosely.

<Worked title="Ranking and explaining">

Rank $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{Mg}$ and $\ce{K}$ by first ionization energy, and justify.

**Order** (419, 496 and 738 kJ/mol):

$$
\ce{K} < \ce{Na} < \ce{Mg}
$$

**$\ce{Na}$ against $\ce{Mg}$:** same shell and the same ten core electrons shielding it, but magnesium has one more proton. The extra nuclear charge holds the valence electrons more tightly, so more energy is needed: $\ce{Mg}$ is higher.

**$\ce{Na}$ against $\ce{K}$:** potassium's valence electron is in the next shell out, farther from the nucleus and shielded by eight more core electrons, which offset potassium's eight extra protons. The attraction is weaker, so it is easier to remove: $\ce{K}$ is lower.

**What earns zero:** "$\ce{Mg}$ wants to lose two electrons" or "$\ce{K}$ is more reactive". Name the nuclear charge, the distance and the shielding. An answer that names no force earns no credit.

</Worked>

<Question number={9} label="Your turn" topic="1.7">

<Part label="a">

Rank $\ce{Li}$, $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{K}$ by atomic radius, smallest first.

<Answer>

$\ce{Li} < \ce{Na} < \ce{K}$

$\ce{Li} < \ce{Na} < \ce{K}$. Each step down adds a shell, so distance and shielding both increase. The added protons are offset by the added core electrons.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Rank $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{Mg}$, $\ce{Al}$ by atomic radius, smallest first.

<Answer>

$\ce{Al} < \ce{Mg} < \ce{Na}$

$\ce{Al} < \ce{Mg} < \ce{Na}$. Across the period the nuclear charge rises while the shell and the shielding by the core electrons stay the same, pulling the outer electrons inward.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The first ionization energy of sulfur (1000 kJ/mol) is *lower* than that of phosphorus (1012 kJ/mol), breaking the trend. Explain.

<Answer>

The paired 3p electron is repelled.

Phosphorus's configuration ends in 3p³, with one electron in each of the three 3p orbitals. Sulfur's ends in 3p⁴, so one orbital holds a *pair*. Two electrons confined to the same orbital repel each other, raising that electron's energy and making it easier to remove, enough to outweigh sulfur's extra proton. (This is an argument about electron–electron repulsion, *not* about "half-filled subshells being stable".)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why is the *second* ionization energy of sodium enormously larger than its first?

<Answer>

The second electron comes from the core.

The first electron removed is sodium's single 3s valence electron, far out and well shielded. Once it is gone, $\ce{Na+}$ has a filled 2p subshell, and the next electron must come from that **core** subshell: much closer to the nucleus and with only the two 1s electrons inside it to shield it. Far greater effective nuclear charge at a far shorter distance means an enormously larger energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 10: Valence electrons and ionic compounds

For groups 1, 2 and 13 to 17, the number of valence electrons is the last digit of the group number. For the metals of groups 1, 2 and 13 that number is the usual charge: 1+, 2+ or 3+. For the nonmetals of groups 15 to 17 the usual charge is the group number minus 18: 3−, 2− or 1−. (Hydrogen, boron and group 14 are left out of the charge rule.) Combine ions so the charges cancel. The lattice energy of the compound that results, the energy needed to pull apart the ions in a mole of the solid, follows from Coulomb's law.

<Worked title="From groups to a formula and a comparison">

Magnesium reacts with nitrogen. Give the ions, the formula, and predict whether the compound's lattice energy exceeds that of $\ce{NaF}$.

**Ions:** Mg is in group 2, has two valence electrons and forms $\ce{Mg^2+}$. N is in group 15, has five valence electrons and forms $\ce{N^3-}$.

**Formula:** the charges must cancel. Three $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ($+6$) with two $\ce{N^3-}$ ($-6$) gives **$\ce{Mg3N2}$**.

**Lattice energy against $\ce{NaF}$:** compare with Coulomb's law. The attraction is proportional to the product of the sizes of the ionic charges:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Mg3N2}:&\quad 2 \times 3 = 6 \\
\ce{NaF}:&\quad 1 \times 1 = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

The distance between the ions matters too, but a change in charge usually matters more, so compare the charges first (topic 2.2). $\ce{Mg3N2}$ therefore has a much larger lattice energy.

</Worked>

Which has the larger lattice energy, $\ce{MgO}$ or $\ce{NaF}$? Justify with Coulomb's law.

<Answer>

$\ce{MgO}$: the sizes of the charges multiply to 4 against 1, and the ions are closer together.

**$\ce{MgO}$**, on both counts of Coulomb's law.

$$
2 \times 2 = 4
$$

for $\ce{MgO}$, against

1 \times 1 = 1

for $\ce{NaF}$.

$\ce{MgO}$ melts at 2852 °C, $\ce{NaF}$ at 993 °C. Explain how this follows from your answer to part (c).

<Answer>

Stronger attractions take more energy to overcome.

Melting an ionic solid means giving the ions enough energy to leave their fixed places and move past one another, against the attractions that hold them there. $\ce{MgO}$ has much stronger attractions, so far more energy, and so a far higher temperature, is required.
