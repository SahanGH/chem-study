There are eleven ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions of the same kind: same skill, new substances, no help. Work alone, and work all four before you check any of them.

**The habit that decides this unit.** Almost every question here is answered in two moves: *count the electrons*, then *reason about the attraction*. Counting means valence electrons, domains, or charges. Reasoning means **Coulomb's law**: the product of the charges over the distance between them.

An explanation that says a species "wants a full octet" or "is more stable" earns **zero** on the AP exam. The octet rule tells you *what* the structure is; it never tells you *why* a property follows.

**One scope note.** For a central atom with more than four electron domains, the AP course framework asks for the **shape only**. You will not be asked for an $\ce{sp^3d}$ or $\ce{sp^3d^2}$ label, and none appears here.

## Ladder 1: Bond type from electronegativity

Bonding is a continuum, not three boxes. The electronegativity difference $\Delta\text{EN}$ tells you where on it a bond sits: near zero, the pair is shared equally; large, the electron is effectively transferred.

<Worked title="Placing three bonds on the continuum">

Classify $\ce{K-F}$, $\ce{C-O}$ and $\ce{H-H}$ using EN: H 2.2, C 2.6, O 3.4, F 4.0, K 0.8.

**$\ce{K-F}$:**

$$
\Delta\text{EN} = 4.0 - 0.8 = 3.2
$$

Very large: the electron is transferred, giving $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{F-}$. **Ionic.**

**$\ce{C-O}$:**

$$
\Delta\text{EN} = 3.4 - 2.6 = 0.8
$$

Moderate: the pair is shared but pulled toward oxygen, leaving $\delta+$ on C and $\delta-$ on O. **Polar covalent.**

**$\ce{H-H}$:** $\Delta\text{EN} = 0$. Identical atoms share equally. **Nonpolar covalent.**

**Read the boundaries as guides, not laws.** Roughly: up to about 0.4 nonpolar, 0.4 to 1.7 polar, above 1.7 ionic. Two nonmetals stay covalent even past 1.7: H–F (1.8) is a polar covalent bond. A bond at 1.6 is not categorically different from one at 1.8; the character shifts gradually.

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Coulomb's law and lattice energy

Lattice energy is the energy needed to pull the ions of a crystal apart into separate gaseous ions. It is Coulombic, so only two things matter: the **product of the charges** and the **distance** between ion centers.

$$
\text{lattice energy} \;\propto\; \frac{q_1 q_2}{d}
$$

<Worked title="One factor at a time">

Rank $\ce{LiF}$, $\ce{NaF}$ and $\ce{KF}$ by lattice energy, then compare $\ce{NaF}$ with $\ce{MgO}$.

**Within the fluoride series,** every compound pairs a $1+$ with a $1-$, so the charge product is the same and cannot decide anything. *Distance* decides. Cation radii rise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Li+}\ (76) < \ce{Na+}\ (102) \\
&< \ce{K+}\ (138)\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $d$ rises and the attraction falls:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{LiF} > \ce{NaF} > \ce{KF} \\
&(1030 > 910 > 808\ \text{kJ/mol})
\end{aligned}
$$

**$\ce{NaF}$ against $\ce{MgO}$:** now the *charges* change.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{NaF}&: (+1)(-1) = 1 \\
\ce{MgO}&: (+2)(-2) = 4
\end{aligned}
$$

That is four times the charge product. The ions also sit closer together (212 pm between centers, against 235 pm), so $d$ is shorter too. Both factors point the same way, and the gap is enormous: 3795 against 910 kJ/mol.

**The method:** check charge first. If the charges match, distance decides. If they differ, charge almost always dominates.

</Worked>

## Ladder 3: Bond order, length and energy

Within the same pair of atoms, more shared pairs means a shorter, stronger bond. Bond order runs with energy and against length.

<Worked title="A carbon–carbon series">

Rank $\ce{C-C}$, $\ce{C=C}$ and $\ce{C#C}$ by bond length and by bond energy.

Bond energies: 347, 614, 839 kJ/mol.

**Energy rises with bond order:**

$$
\ce{C-C} < \ce{C=C} < \ce{C#C}
$$

Each extra shared pair adds attraction between the two nuclei and the electron density between them.

**Length falls as order rises:**

$$
\ce{C#C} < \ce{C=C} < \ce{C-C}
$$

More shared electron density pulls the nuclei closer together.

**Note the energies are not simple multiples:** 614 is not twice 347, and 839 is not three times it. The second and third pairs are pi bonds, whose side-on overlap is less effective than the first bond's end-on overlap.

</Worked>

<Question number={3} label="Your turn" topic="2.2">

<Part label="a">

Rank $\ce{N-N}$ (163), $\ce{N=N}$ (418), $\ce{N#N}$ (941) by bond length, shortest first.

<Answer>

$\ce{N#N} < \ce{N=N} < \ce{N-N}$

$$
\ce{N#N} < \ce{N=N} < \ce{N-N}
$$

Higher bond order draws the nuclei closer.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The bond energies are $\ce{H-F}$ 567, $\ce{H-Cl}$ 431, $\ce{H-Br}$ 366 and $\ce{H-I}$ 299 kJ/mol. Explain the trend.

<Answer>

Larger halogen, longer bond, weaker attraction.

Down the halogen group the atom gets **larger**, so the $\ce{H-X}$ bond is longer and the shared pair sits farther from both nuclei. The Coulombic attraction holding the bond is correspondingly weaker, so the bond energy falls steadily.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why is $\ce{N2}$ so unreactive?

<Answer>

The triple bond is extremely strong.

Its $\ce{N#N}$ triple bond is one of the strongest known (941 kJ/mol). Any reaction must begin by breaking it, which demands an enormous energy input, so nitrogen is kinetically sluggish even where reaction is thermodynamically favorable.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

In $\ce{O2}$ (495) and $\ce{F2}$ (159), the bond order falls from 2 to 1. Is that the whole explanation for the energy difference?

<Answer>

Largely, but lone-pair repulsion in $\ce{F2}$ also weakens it.

**Largely, but not entirely.** Bond order accounts for most of it. $\ce{F2}$ is also anomalously weak for a single bond because the two small fluorine atoms are close together and their **lone pairs repel** strongly, further destabilizing the bond. That is why $\ce{F-F}$ (159) is weaker even than $\ce{Cl-Cl}$ (243). $\ce{N-N}$ (163) is unusually weak for the same reason.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 4: Properties of ionic solids

Every property of an ionic solid traces back to the same fact: charged particles locked in a rigid lattice, held by strong electrostatic attractions.

<Worked title="Four properties, one cause">

Explain why $\ce{NaCl}$ is hard, brittle, high-melting, and conducts only when molten or dissolved.

**Hard and high-melting:** each ion is attracted to all its oppositely charged neighbors throughout the lattice. Deforming or melting the solid means overcoming many strong Coulombic attractions at once.

**Brittle:** a blow displaces one plane of ions by a single lattice position. Ions of *like* charge are then brought face to face, attraction along that plane becomes strong repulsion, and the crystal splits cleanly.

**Conductivity:** conduction requires mobile charges. In the solid the ions are fixed: charges are present but cannot move. Melting or dissolving frees them, and the liquid or solution conducts.

**The contrast that makes the point:** a metal, struck the same way, bends. Its delocalized electrons flow with the displaced layer and keep binding it, so no like-charge repulsion ever arises.

</Worked>

<Question number={4} label="Your turn" topic="2.3">

<Part label="a">

Why does solid $\ce{KBr}$ not conduct, while molten $\ce{KBr}$ does?

<Answer>

Ions fixed vs mobile.

Conduction needs *mobile* charge carriers. In solid $\ce{KBr}$ the ions sit in fixed lattice positions; melting frees them to migrate, so the melt conducts.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{MgO}$ is harder than $\ce{NaCl}$. Explain Coulombically.

<Answer>

Charge product 4 vs 1.

$\ce{MgO}$ pairs $2+$ with $2-$, a charge product of 4, against $\ce{NaCl}$'s 1; its ions are also smaller, so $d$ is shorter. Much stronger attractions make the lattice far harder to deform.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why does an ionic crystal shatter rather than bend?

<Answer>

Like charges brought adjacent.

A blow shifts one layer by one ion position, bringing like charges adjacent. Attraction along that plane is replaced by strong repulsion, which drives the layers apart and splits the crystal.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A substance dissolves in water and the solution conducts. Does that prove the substance was ionic?

<Answer>

No: a molecular acid also ionizes.

**No.** A molecular substance that *ionizes* in water, HCl for instance, also gives a conducting solution despite being covalent as a pure substance. The stronger evidence for an ionic solid is that it conducts when **molten**, since that requires ions to have been present in the solid itself.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 5: Metals and alloys

A metal is a lattice of cations in a sea of delocalized electrons. Every metallic property follows from those electrons being free to move.

<Worked title="Conductivity, malleability, alloys">

Explain metallic conduction and malleability, and why adding a second element hardens the metal.

**Conduction:** the valence electrons belong to the whole lattice, not to individual atoms. Under a potential difference they drift through the solid, carrying charge.

**Malleability:** when layers of cations slide over one another, the electron sea flows with them and continues to bind the lattice. Nothing brings like charges together, so the metal deforms instead of fracturing.

**Alloying:** atoms of a different size disrupt the regular layers. Layers can no longer slide smoothly past one another, so the alloy resists deformation: it is harder and stronger, though usually less ductile.

</Worked>

<Question number={5} label="Your turn" topic="2.4">

<Part label="a">

Why do metals conduct heat as well as electricity?

<Answer>

Mobile electrons carry kinetic energy.

The same delocalized electrons carry *kinetic* energy as well as charge. Heated at one end, they move faster and transfer that energy rapidly through the lattice by collision.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Brass is copper with zinc. Why is it harder than pure copper?

<Answer>

Zinc atoms disrupt the layers.

Zinc atoms differ in size from copper, disrupting the regular layers so that they cannot slide over one another as easily.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why does a small amount of carbon make iron much harder?

<Answer>

Small carbon atoms wedge into the gaps and pin the layers.

Carbon atoms are much smaller than iron atoms, so they fit into the gaps between them (an interstitial alloy). They disrupt the regular layers and pin them in place, so the layers can no longer slide easily and the metal resists deformation.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Both metals and molten ionic solids conduct. State how the *carriers* differ.

<Answer>

Electrons vs ions.

In a metal the mobile carriers are **electrons**, and the cations stay put. In a molten ionic solid the carriers are the **ions** themselves, which physically migrate through the liquid.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 6: Drawing Lewis structures

Count the valence electrons first, adjusting for charge. Then build a skeleton, complete the outer atoms' octets, and convert lone pairs to multiple bonds only if the central atom is short.

## Ladder 7: Formal charge

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{formal charge} = {}&\text{valence electrons} \\
&- \text{nonbonding electrons} \\
&- \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{bonding electrons})
\end{aligned}
$$

Formal charges must sum to the overall charge on the species. The best structure keeps them small, and puts any negative charge on the most electronegative atom.

<Worked title="Nitrate">

Assign formal charges in $\ce{NO3-}$, drawn with one $\ce{N=O}$ and two $\ce{N-O}$.

**Nitrogen:** 5 valence, no nonbonding electrons, 8 bonding electrons:

$$
5 - 0 - 4 = \mathbf{+1}
$$

**Doubly bonded O:** 6 valence, 4 nonbonding electrons, 4 bonding:

$$
6 - 4 - 2 = \mathbf{0}
$$

**Each singly bonded O:** 6 valence, 6 nonbonding electrons, 2 bonding:

$$
6 - 6 - 1 = \mathbf{-1}
$$

**Check the sum:**

$$
(+1) + 0 + (-1) + (-1) = -1
$$

That matches the ion's charge. ✓ If your formal charges do not sum to the overall charge, the structure is drawn wrong: go back before doing anything else.

</Worked>

## Ladder 8: Resonance

When a double bond can be placed in more than one equivalent position, no single Lewis structure is correct. The real species is the **average**: the electrons are delocalized. It does not flip between structures.

<Worked title="Nitrate's three structures">

Explain why $\ce{NO3-}$ needs three resonance structures, and predict its bond lengths.

**Why three:** the double bond can go to any one of the three oxygens, and the three choices are equivalent.

**What is real:** not one structure, and not rapid switching between them. The extra bonding pair is **delocalized** over all three $\ce{N-O}$ positions simultaneously.

**Bond lengths:** all three are **identical**, and each is intermediate between a normal $\ce{N-O}$ single bond and a normal $\ce{N=O}$ double bond. Every position has the same bond order, about $1\tfrac{1}{3}$.

**The experimental point:** measurement finds three equal bond lengths. A structure with one short and two long bonds would predict otherwise, which is exactly why the single Lewis structure is known to be inadequate.

</Worked>

## Ladder 9: VSEPR: shapes and angles

Count the electron **domains** on the central atom: each bond (single, double or triple) and each lone pair is one domain. Domains repel and spread as far apart as possible. The *shape* is then named from the positions of the **atoms** only.

<Worked title="Same domain count, three different shapes">

Give the shape and approximate bond angle for $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$.

All three have **four** domains, so all three are based on a tetrahedron. What differs is how many domains are lone pairs.

**$\ce{CH4}$** (4 bonds, 0 lone pairs): **tetrahedral**, 109.5°.

**$\ce{NH3}$** (3 bonds, 1 lone pair): **trigonal pyramidal**, about 107°.

**$\ce{H2O}$** (2 bonds, 2 lone pairs): **bent**, about 104.5°.

**Why the angle shrinks:** a lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out closer to the central atom and takes up more angular space than a bonding pair. Each lone pair squeezes the remaining bonds together, so the angle drops with every one added.

</Worked>

</Answer>

<Answer>

A domain is a *region* of electron density, not a count of pairs. All the electrons of a double bond lie between the same two nuclei, so they occupy one region and repel other domains as a single unit. Carbon therefore has two domains and $\ce{CO2}$ is linear.

## Ladder 10: Hybridization and sigma and pi counting

Count the domains on an atom and the hybridization label follows: 2 domains $\ce{sp}$, 3 domains $\ce{sp^2}$, 4 domains $\ce{sp^3}$. Every single bond is one sigma; every double bond is one sigma plus one pi; every triple bond is one sigma plus two pi.

<Worked title="Hybridization and bond count together">

For $\ce{HCN}$, give the hybridization of carbon and count the sigma and pi bonds.

**Domains on carbon:** one bond to H, one triple bond to N. The triple bond is a single domain, so carbon has **two** domains. Hence it is $\ce{sp}$ hybridized, and the molecule is linear.

**Bond count:** $\ce{H-C}$ is one sigma. $\ce{C#N}$ is one sigma plus *two* pi. Total: **2 sigma, 2 pi**.

**The rule that never fails:** the *first* bond between any two atoms is sigma; every additional bond between that same pair is pi.

</Worked>

Why does the $\ce{C=C}$ bond in ethene resist rotation while the $\ce{C-C}$ bond in ethane does not?

<Answer>

Rotation would destroy the pi overlap.

Ethane's $\ce{C-C}$ is a single sigma bond, and sigma overlap is cylindrically symmetric about the bond axis: twisting does not reduce it, so rotation is nearly free. Ethene's double bond includes a pi bond formed by two *parallel* $p$ orbitals; rotating one end turns them out of alignment, and at 90° the overlap is zero. Rotation would mean breaking the pi bond, which costs far too much energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 11: Molecular polarity

A molecule is polar only if it has polar bonds *and* a shape that prevents their dipoles canceling. Shape decides, which is why this ladder comes after VSEPR.

<Worked title="Same bonds, opposite verdicts">

$\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ both contain polar bonds. Explain why only water is polar.

**$\ce{CO2}$ is linear.** The two $\ce{C=O}$ bond dipoles are equal and point in exactly opposite directions, so they cancel completely. Net dipole zero: **nonpolar**, despite strongly polar bonds.

**$\ce{H2O}$ is bent**, because oxygen's two lone pairs force it so. The two $\ce{O-H}$ dipoles no longer oppose each other and add to a net dipole pointing toward the oxygen: **polar**.

**The method:** identify the polar bonds, then ask whether the shape is symmetric enough for them to cancel. Never answer from the bonds alone.

</Worked>

<Question number={11} label="Your turn" topic="2.1, 2.7">

<Part label="a">

Is $\ce{CCl4}$ polar? Justify.

<Answer>

No: tetrahedral, dipoles cancel.

**Nonpolar.** The four $\ce{C-Cl}$ bonds are polar, but the tetrahedral arrangement is perfectly symmetric and the four dipoles cancel.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Is $\ce{CH3Cl}$ polar? Justify.

<Answer>

Yes: asymmetric.

**Polar.** Start from $\ce{CH4}$ and replace one hydrogen with chlorine. The $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is far more polar than the $\ce{C-H}$ bonds, so the symmetry is broken and the dipoles no longer cancel. A net dipole remains, pointing toward the chlorine.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Is $\ce{NH3}$ polar? Justify.

<Answer>

Yes: pyramidal.

**Polar.** Three $\ce{N-H}$ dipoles plus a lone pair on a *pyramidal* nitrogen; the dipoles do not cancel and the net points along the lone-pair axis.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

$\ce{BF3}$ and $\ce{NH3}$ both have three bonds to the central atom, but only one is polar. Explain.

<Answer>

$\ce{BF3}$ is planar and symmetric; $\ce{NH3}$ has a lone pair and is pyramidal.

$\ce{BF3}$ has three domains and *no* lone pair, so it is **trigonal planar**: perfectly symmetric, and the three dipoles cancel. $\ce{NH3}$ has four domains, one being a lone pair, so it is **pyramidal**: unsymmetric, and the dipoles add. Same number of bonds, different domain count, opposite result.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
