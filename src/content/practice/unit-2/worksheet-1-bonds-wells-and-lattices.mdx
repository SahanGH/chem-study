<Question number={2} topic="2.1">

Rank by increasing bond polarity, then state the trend that decides it: $\ce{O-F}$, $\ce{O-H}$, $\ce{O-O}$.

<Answer>

$$
\ce{O-O} < \ce{O-F} < \ce{O-H}
$$

Polarity grows with the electronegativity difference (O and F are neighbors; O and H differ most).

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="2.2">

A potential-energy curve for $\ce{Cl2}$ has its minimum at 199 pm and $-243\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

<Part label="a">

Bond length: <Blank /> Bond energy: <Blank />

<Answer>

The bond length: 199 pm. The bond energy: 243 kJ/mol.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

At distances shorter than 199 pm, the curve rises steeply. What interaction dominates there?

<Answer>

nucleus–nucleus repulsion

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The well for $\ce{Br2}$: 228 pm, $-193\ \text{kJ/mol}$. On shared axes, sketch both curves, labeled.

<Answer>

The $\ce{Br2}$ minimum is farther right (228 pm) and shallower ($-193\ \text{kJ/mol}$). The $\ce{Cl2}$ well is deeper, and its minimum is at a shorter distance.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Which bond is easier to break, and what number says so?

<Answer>

$\ce{Br2}$, with the smaller well depth (193 kJ/mol)

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={4} topic="2.2">

Rank by lattice energy magnitude, largest first: $\ce{KBr}$, $\ce{CaO}$, $\ce{KCl}$, $\ce{SrO}$.

<Answer>

$$
\ce{CaO} > \ce{SrO} \gg \ce{KCl} > \ce{KBr}
$$

Charge products first (4 against 1). Within each pair, the smaller ions win ($\ce{Ca^2+} < \ce{Sr^2+}$; $\ce{Cl-} < \ce{Br-}$).

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="2.2, 2.3">

Solid $\ce{MgO}$ melts at 2852 °C; solid $\ce{NaF}$ melts at 993 °C. Both lattices have similar ion sizes. Explain the gap with Coulomb's law.

<Answer>

$\ce{MgO}$ is $2+/2-$: its charge product is 4, against 1 for $\ce{NaF}$. That is roughly four times the attraction at similar $r$. So far more energy is needed to pull the ions apart, and $\ce{MgO}$ melts much higher.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="2.4">

A jeweler's gold alloy substitutes Cu atoms (similar radius) into the Au lattice. Steel inserts small C atoms into holes in the Fe lattice.

<Part label="a">

Name each alloy type: <Blank />

<Answer>

Au/Cu: substitutional. Steel: interstitial.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Both alloys are harder than the pure metal. Give the particle-level reason.

<Answer>

Foreign atoms disrupt the uniform layers, so planes of atoms can no longer slide freely past each other.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why does the alloy still conduct electricity?

<Answer>

The delocalized electron sea remains mobile.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
