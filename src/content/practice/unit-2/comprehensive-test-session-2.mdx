This is the second of two sittings that together test all of Unit 2. It covers topics 2.5 to 2.7: Lewis diagrams, resonance and formal charge, and the shapes of molecules, with their polarity and hybridization. Session 1 covers bonds and solids.

There are 22 questions worth 113 points. Give yourself about 120 minutes. You may use a calculator. Draw each diagram and write out each answer in full before you open the worked answer and the scoring rubric.

One limit applies. For a central atom with five or six electron domains, you are asked for the shape only, never a hybridization.

## Part E: Lewis diagrams

These six questions are about drawing Lewis diagrams: counting the valence electrons, building the diagram step by step, and the atoms that end up with fewer than eight electrons or more. A drawing is complete only with every bond, every lone pair, and, for an ion, square brackets and the charge.

<Question number={1} title="Counting valence electrons" topic="2.5" points={5}>

Every Lewis diagram starts with a count of the valence electrons.

<Part label="a">

Determine the total number of valence electrons in each species. Show how you got each count.

- hydrazine, $\ce{N2H4}$, a fuel for the small thrusters that steer spacecraft
- the hydronium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$
- the phosphate ion, $\ce{PO4^3-}$

<Answer>

Add the valence electrons of every atom: N has 5, H has 1, O has 6 and P has 5. Then add one electron for each unit of negative charge, or take one away for each unit of positive charge.

$$
\ce{N2H4}: 2(5) + 4(1) = \mathbf{14}
$$

$$
\ce{H3O+}: 6 + 3(1) - 1 = \mathbf{8}
$$

The + charge means one electron fewer.

$$
\ce{PO4^3-}: 5 + 4(6) + 3 = \mathbf{32}
$$

The 3− charge means three electrons more.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Draw the Lewis diagram of the hydronium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$.

<Answer>

Oxygen goes in the center, because hydrogen forms only one bond and is never central. Three O–H bonds use 6 of the 8 electrons, and each hydrogen has its two. The last 2 go on oxygen as one lone pair, so oxygen has eight around it: six in bonds and two in the lone pair. The diagram goes in square brackets, with the + charge outside them at the upper right.

<Question number={2} title="Double and triple bonds" topic="2.5" points={6}>

Some molecules need multiple bonds before every atom has eight electrons around it.

<Part label="a">

Acetone, $\ce{C3H6O}$, is the solvent in many nail polish removers. Its three carbon atoms form a chain, the oxygen atom is bonded to the middle carbon, and three hydrogen atoms are bonded to each end carbon. Determine the number of valence electrons and draw the Lewis diagram.

<Answer>

The count:

$$
3(4) + 6(1) + 6 = \mathbf{24}
$$

Now every carbon and the oxygen have eight, and every hydrogen has two.

The diagram has **a C=O double bond, two lone pairs on oxygen, and every other bond single**.

Cyanogen, $\ce{C2N2}$, is a toxic gas. Its atoms are joined in the order N, C, C, N. Determine the number of valence electrons and draw the Lewis diagram.

<Answer>

The count:

$$
2(4) + 2(5) = \mathbf{18}
$$

Three single bonds (N–C, C–C and C–N) use 6, leaving 12. Each nitrogen gets three lone pairs, which uses all 12. That makes two C≡N triple bonds, and each nitrogen keeps one lone pair.

The diagram is **N≡C–C≡N, with one lone pair on each nitrogen and none on carbon**.

<Part label="c">

In your diagram of cyanogen, determine how many electrons are in bonds and how many are in lone pairs. Show that every atom has eight electrons around it.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{bonds}&: 2(6) + 2 = 14 \\
\text{lone pairs}&: 2(2) = 4 \\
\text{total}&: 14 + 4 = \mathbf{18}
\end{aligned}
$$

That matches the count. Each carbon has 6 electrons from its triple bond and 2 from the C–C bond, **8** in all. Each nitrogen has 6 from its triple bond and 2 in its lone pair, also **8**.

</Answer>

</Part>

State the number of electrons around the boron atom and around each chlorine atom in your diagram. Explain why boron is left with fewer than eight.

<Answer>

Boron has **6** electrons around it, all in its three bonds. Each chlorine has **8**: two in its bond and six in its three lone pairs.

Boron brings only three valence electrons, and all three go into its three bonds. Once each chlorine has eight, all 24 electrons are placed, so none are left for boron. Boron is the atom that is often content with six, so the diagram stops there.

</Answer>

</Part>

The count:

$$
5 + 3(7) = 26
$$

Three N–Cl bonds use 6, and the chlorines' lone pairs use 18, which leaves 2. They go on nitrogen as one lone pair, so nitrogen has eight around it: six in bonds and two in the lone pair.

<Question number={4} title="More than eight around the central atom" topic="2.5" points={5}>

Atoms from the third period down can be drawn with more than eight electrons around them.

<Part label="a">

Chlorine trifluoride, $\ce{ClF3}$, is a very reactive gas used in the computer-chip industry to clean equipment. Its chlorine atom is bonded to three fluorine atoms. Determine the number of valence electrons, draw the Lewis diagram, and state how many electrons surround the chlorine atom.

<Answer>

The count:

$$
7 + 3(7) = \mathbf{28}
$$

Three Cl–F bonds use 6, leaving 22. Each fluorine gets three lone pairs, which uses 18 and leaves 4. Those 4 go on chlorine as two lone pairs.

Chlorine has **10** electrons around it: six in its bonds and four in its lone pairs. Chlorine is in the third period, so it can hold more than eight.

<Part label="b">

Phosphorus pentafluoride, $\ce{PF5}$, is a stable compound, but no molecule of $\ce{NF5}$ is known. Determine how many electrons would surround the central atom in a Lewis diagram of either molecule. Explain why phosphorus can be drawn this way but nitrogen cannot.

<Answer>

The count for $\ce{PF5}$:

$$
5 + 5(7) = 40
$$

Five P–F bonds use 10, and the fluorines' lone pairs use 30, so none are left. The central atom has **10** electrons around it, in five bonds. Nitrogen also has five valence electrons. A diagram of $\ce{NF5}$ drawn the same way would put 10 around nitrogen.

Phosphorus is in the third period, and atoms from the third period down can be drawn with more than eight. **Nitrogen is in the second period, and a second-period atom never holds more than eight**, so nitrogen cannot be drawn with five bonds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={5} title="An odd number of electrons" topic="2.5, 2.6" points={5}>

The hydroxyl radical, $\ce{OH}$, is a neutral particle made of one oxygen atom and one hydrogen atom. Sunlight makes it in the air, where it is so reactive that it breaks down many pollutants.

<Part label="a">

Determine the number of valence electrons in $\ce{OH}$. Explain why no Lewis diagram of $\ce{OH}$ can give oxygen eight electrons and hydrogen two.

<Answer>

The count:

$$
6 + 1 = \mathbf{7}
$$

Electrons in a bond come two at a time, and so do the electrons in a lone pair. A diagram built only from bonds and lone pairs therefore holds an even number of electrons, and 7 is not even. One electron has to stand alone. Hydrogen already has two from its bond to oxygen and can hold no more, so the lone electron cannot go on hydrogen. **It goes on oxygen, which is left with seven**, one short of eight.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Draw the Lewis diagram of $\ce{OH}$.

<Answer>

The O–H bond uses 2 of the 7 electrons and gives hydrogen its two. The other 5 go on oxygen: two lone pairs and one unpaired electron. Oxygen has seven around it: two in the bond, four in its lone pairs and the unpaired one.

<Part label="c">

A student suggests an O=H double bond, so that oxygen reaches eight. Explain why this does not work.

<Answer>

A double bond would put **four electrons around hydrogen**, and hydrogen holds only two. It would not get rid of the lone electron either: the double bond holds 4 of the 7, and the 3 left over still include one that has no partner.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={7} title="The formate ion" topic="2.6" points={6}>

Determine the number of valence electrons in the formate ion, and draw its resonance structures.

Three single bonds from carbon use 6 electrons. Three lone pairs on each oxygen use the other 12. That is all 18, but carbon has only six electrons around it. So one oxygen lone pair becomes a second bond to carbon, and carbon has eight.

Either oxygen can take the double bond, so there are **two equivalent resonance structures**. In each, the singly bonded oxygen has a formal charge of $-1$ and every other atom has 0.

<Part label="a">

Determine the number of valence electrons in $\ce{ClO2}$.

<Answer>

Valence electrons:

$$
7 + 2(6) = \mathbf{19}
$$

The count is odd, so one electron is left unpaired, as both diagrams show.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Determine the formal charges in both diagrams. State which diagram formal charge favors, and whether that diagram is allowed.

<Answer>

The nonbonding electrons on chlorine are its lone pair and its unpaired electron: 3.

Diagram P:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Cl}&: 7 - 3 - 2 = \mathbf{+2} \\
\text{each } \ce{O}&: 6 - 6 - 1 = \mathbf{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Diagram Q:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Cl}&: 7 - 3 - 4 = \mathbf{0} \\
\text{each } \ce{O}&: 6 - 4 - 2 = \mathbf{0}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both sets add up to 0, the charge of a neutral molecule. Formal charge favors **diagram Q**, where every formal charge is zero. In Q, chlorine has 8 bonding electrons and 3 nonbonding electrons around it, 11 in all. That is allowed: chlorine is in the third period, and a central atom from the third period can hold more than eight.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student says: "Diagram Q has every formal charge at zero, so it shows exactly where the unpaired electron is in a real $\ce{ClO2}$ molecule." Explain why this claim goes too far.

<Answer>

Formal charge is bookkeeping, and the rules for choosing a diagram are guides, not laws. They pick the best model the Lewis rules allow, but they cannot locate a real electron. Here they cannot even pick one place: a diagram with one Cl=O double bond, one Cl–O single bond, two lone pairs on chlorine, and the unpaired electron on the singly bonded oxygen also has every formal charge zero.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Cl}&: 7 - 4 - 3 = 0 \\
\ce{O}\ (\ce{Cl=O})&: 6 - 4 - 2 = 0 \\
\ce{O}\ (\text{unpaired})&: 6 - 5 - 1 = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So formal charge cannot tell whether the odd electron is on chlorine or on an oxygen. Even where formal charge does pick one place, it can be wrong. In nitrogen dioxide, formal charge would put its unpaired electron on an oxygen, but the way two $\ce{NO2}$ molecules join through their nitrogen atoms shows it sits mostly on nitrogen. Only evidence from the real molecule can show where the unpaired electron is. A molecule with an odd number of electrons is exactly where the Lewis model reaches its limits.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={15} title="Ammonium, ammonia and amide" topic="2.7" points={4}>

The ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$, ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, and the amide ion, $\ce{NH2-}$, each have nitrogen in the center, bonded only to hydrogen.

<Part label="a">

Rank the three by their H–N–H bond angle, from largest to smallest. Justify your ranking.

<Answer>

First count the valence electrons in each. A positive charge removes one, and a negative charge adds one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{NH4+}&: 5 + 4 - 1 = 8 \\
\ce{NH3}&: 5 + 3 = 8 \\
\ce{NH2-}&: 5 + 2 + 1 = 8
\end{aligned}
$$

Each N–H bond uses 2 electrons, and the rest are lone pairs on nitrogen. So $\ce{NH4+}$ has 4 bonds and no lone pair, $\ce{NH3}$ has 3 bonds and 1 lone pair, and $\ce{NH2-}$ has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs.

**Count the domains.** All three have 4 electron domains around nitrogen, so all three start from the tetrahedral arrangement, with angles of 109.5°.

**Conclude.** $\ce{NH4+}$, with no lone pair, keeps the full 109.5°. $\ce{NH3}$ closes to about 107°. $\ce{NH2-}$, with two lone pairs, closes further, below 107°:

$$
\ce{NH4+} > \ce{NH3} > \ce{NH2-}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={17} title="Polar bonds, polar molecules" topic="2.7" points={6}>

For each molecule, state whether it is polar or nonpolar. Justify your answer from its shape and its bond dipoles. Use these electronegativities where you need them: C 2.6, N 3.0, O 3.4, F 4.0, S 2.6.

<Part label="a">

Arsenic pentafluoride, $\ce{AsF5}$.

<Answer>

Count the valence electrons:

$$
5 + 5(7) = 40
$$

Five As–F bonds use 10 electrons, and the fluorines take 30 as lone pairs. Nothing is left, so arsenic has no lone pair. Arsenic is in period 4 and can hold more than eight. It has 5 domains, all bonds: the shape is **trigonal bipyramidal**.

Each As–F bond is polar, because fluorine is the most electronegative element and arsenic is far less electronegative. But the dipoles cancel. The two axial bonds point in exactly opposite directions. The three equatorial bonds are 120° apart in one plane, like the bonds of $\ce{BF3}$, and they cancel too. $\ce{AsF5}$ is **nonpolar**, even though every bond in it is polar.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Carbonyl sulfide, OCS, with carbon in the center.

<Answer>

Count the valence electrons:

$$
6 + 4 + 6 = 16
$$

The Lewis diagram is S=C=O, with two lone pairs on each end atom and none on carbon. Carbon has 2 domains, the two double bonds, so the molecule is **linear**, like $\ce{CO2}$.

But its two ends are different. The electronegativity differences are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{C=O}&: 3.4 - 2.6 = 0.8 \\
\ce{C=S}&: 2.6 - 2.6 = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The C=O bond is polar and the C=S bond is not, so nothing on the sulfur side balances the C=O dipole. The dipoles do not cancel, and OCS is **polar**. In $\ce{CO2}$, two equal C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Nitrogen trifluoride, $\ce{NF3}$.

<Answer>

Count the valence electrons:

$$
5 + 3(7) = 26
$$

Three N–F bonds use 6 electrons and the fluorines take 18 as lone pairs, which leaves 2: one lone pair on nitrogen. Nitrogen has 4 domains, 3 bonds and 1 lone pair, so the shape is **trigonal pyramidal**.

Each N–F bond is polar:

$$
4.0 - 3.0 = 1.0
$$

The three bonds all point down and away from the nitrogen at the top of the pyramid, so their dipoles point partly the same way and do not cancel. $\ce{NF3}$ is **polar**.

</Answer>

</Part>

State whether each of compounds A, B and C is polar or nonpolar. Justify each answer.

<Answer>

Each carbon has 3 domains and no lone pair, so its bonds are about 120° apart. Compare the bonds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{C-F}&: 4.0 - 2.6 = 1.4 \\
\ce{C-H}&: 2.6 - 2.2 = 0.4
\end{aligned}
$$

Each C–F bond is strongly polar, and the C–H bonds are close to nonpolar. So each answer turns on whether the two C–F dipoles cancel.

**A is polar.** Both fluorines are on the same side of the double bond. Both C–F dipoles point partly toward that side, so they add up.

**B is polar.** Both fluorines are on the same carbon. Both C–F dipoles point partly toward that end of the molecule, so they add up.

**C is nonpolar.** The fluorines are on opposite sides and opposite ends. The two C–F dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Two of the three compounds are geometric isomers of each other. Identify them, and state which is cis and which is trans.

<Answer>

**A and C.** They have the same atoms bonded in the same order: each carbon carries one H and one F. They differ only in where the atoms are held in space. **A is cis**, with both fluorines on the same side, and **C is trans**, with the fluorines on opposite sides.

B is not a geometric isomer of either. Its fluorines are both on one carbon, so its atoms are bonded in a different order.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why the two compounds you named in part (b) cannot turn into each other at room temperature.

<Answer>

To turn A into C, one carbon would have to rotate half a turn about the C=C bond. The double bond is one σ bond and one π bond, and the π bond's overlap lies above and below the line between the carbons. Rotating one carbon pulls that overlap apart, so the π bond would have to break first. At room temperature it does not, so the atoms cannot rotate, and A and C stay separate compounds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={21} title="A carbon with fewer than eight" topic="2.7" points={3}>

The methyl cation, $\ce{CH3+}$, and the methyl anion, $\ce{CH3-}$, each have a carbon atom bonded to three hydrogen atoms.

<Part label="a">

For each ion, determine the number of electron domains around carbon and the molecular shape.

<Answer>

**$\ce{CH3+}$.** Count the valence electrons, removing one for the charge:

$$
4 + 3(1) - 1 = 6
$$

Three C–H bonds use all 6, so carbon has no lone pair and only six electrons around it. No outer atom has a lone pair to share, so no double bond can be made. It has **3 domains**, all bonds: **trigonal planar**.

**$\ce{CH3-}$.** Count the valence electrons, adding one for the charge:

$$
4 + 3(1) + 1 = 8
$$

Three C–H bonds use 6, and the last 2 are a lone pair on carbon. It has **4 domains**, 3 bonds and 1 lone pair: **trigonal pyramidal**, like $\ce{NH3}$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State the hybridization of carbon in each ion.

<Answer>

$\ce{CH3+}$: 3 domains, so **sp²**. $\ce{CH3-}$: 4 domains, so **sp³**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={22} title="Urea from diagram to dipole" topic="2.7" points={7}>

Urea, $\ce{CO(NH2)2}$, is the main form in which the human body gets rid of nitrogen, and it is widely used as a fertilizer. Urea has a central carbon atom with one oxygen atom and two $\ce{NH2}$ groups attached. Electronegativities: H 2.2, C 2.6, N 3.0, O 3.4.

<Part label="a">

Determine the number of valence electrons and draw the best Lewis diagram of urea, using formal charge to choose where any double bond goes.

<Answer>

Count the valence electrons:

$$
4 + 6 + 2(5) + 4(1) = 24
$$

Single bonds for the skeleton (C–O, two C–N and four N–H) use 14 electrons. Oxygen takes three lone pairs and each nitrogen one, which uses the other 10. But carbon then has only six electrons. Turn one lone pair on oxygen into a second bond: C=O. Now every atom other than hydrogen has an octet, and the total is still 24.

Formal charge says the double bond belongs on oxygen. With C=O, every atom has a formal charge of 0. A C=N double bond instead would give that nitrogen $+1$ and oxygen $-1$.

Determine the number of electron domains around carbon, and name the shape around carbon.

<Answer>

Carbon has **3 domains**: the double bond to oxygen (which counts once) and the two bonds to nitrogen. It has no lone pair, so the shape around carbon is **trigonal planar**.

State the hybridization of the carbon atom.

<Answer>

Three domains: **sp²**.

Determine the number of sigma bonds and the number of pi bonds in the molecule.

<Answer>

The four N–H bonds and the two C–N bonds are 6 σ. The C=O bond is 1 σ and 1 π. Total: **7 sigma bonds** and **1 pi bond**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

State whether urea is polar or nonpolar. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

The electronegativity differences of the bonds to carbon are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{C=O}&: 3.4 - 2.6 = 0.8 \\
\ce{C-N}&: 3.0 - 2.6 = 0.4
\end{aligned}
$$

The C=O bond (0.8) is far more polar than the C–N bonds (0.4). In a trigonal planar shape, three bond dipoles cancel only when they are equal, as in $\ce{BF3}$. Nothing balances the C=O dipole, so urea is **polar**.

</Answer>
