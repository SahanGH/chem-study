Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One habit runs through all of them: the electron count comes first, always. Start every structure by writing down the total electron count. A structure without a count cannot be checked, and the count catches most errors before they happen.

<Question number={3} topic="2.6">

Cyanide, $\ce{CN-}$ (10 electrons), could be drawn as C≡N with one lone pair on each atom, with both lone pairs on one atom, or with a C=N double bond instead. The accepted structure gives $\ce{C}$ a formal charge of $-1$ and $\ce{N}$ a formal charge of 0. A student objects: "A negative charge should sit on the **more** electronegative atom, so this structure is wrong." Respond.

<Answer>

The electronegativity rule is a preference among valid candidates, not a veto. It picks among structures that already give C and N an octet; it does not override the octet. With 10 electrons only one structure does that: a triple bond with one lone pair on each atom. Every other arrangement leaves an atom short of eight. The closest rival, a double bond with one lone pair on C and two on N, does put the $-1$ on nitrogen, but it leaves carbon with only six electrons. So the octet comes first, and $-1$ on carbon is the price of giving both atoms an octet.

</Answer>

</Question>

Both O–O bonds measure 128 pm. A true O=O bond is 121 pm; an O–O single bond is 148 pm. Explain how this is evidence for the resonance hybrid.

<Answer>

Both bonds are identical and intermediate, matching a $3/2$-order average, not one single bond plus one double bond.

<Question number={5} topic="2.5">

Each structure below has one error. Find it.

<Part label="a">

$\ce{BF3}$ drawn with a B=F double bond "to complete boron's octet."

<Answer>

The double bond puts $+1$ on F, the most electronegative element. The structure with B's incomplete octet (6 electrons) is the accepted structure. Boron is the exception that may stop at six electrons (topic 2.5), so nothing forces the double bond, and the single-bonded structure has every formal charge at zero.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$\ce{SF4}$ drawn with only 8 electrons on S when the count demands more.

<Answer>

The count:

$$
6 + 4(7) = 34\ \text{electrons}
$$

Four bonds use 8, and the F octets use 24, so the last pair **must** sit on S. Sulfur, in period 3, can hold more than eight.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
