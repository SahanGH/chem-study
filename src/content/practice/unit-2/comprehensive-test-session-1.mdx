## Part A: Types of bonds

This part covers topic 2.1: what electronegativity is, why it changes across the periodic table, and how the difference between two atoms places a bond on the continuum from nonpolar covalent to ionic. Each question gives the electronegativities you need.

<Question number={1} title="Classifying four substances" topic="2.1" points={5}>

Use these electronegativities: Li 1.0, C 2.6, O 3.4, S 2.6.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the electronegativity difference for the Li–O bond in lithium oxide, $\ce{Li2O}$, the S–O bond in sulfur trioxide, $\ce{SO3}$, and the C–S bond in carbon disulfide, $\ce{CS2}$.

<Answer>

Subtract the smaller value from the larger one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Li-O}&: 3.4 - 1.0 = \mathbf{2.4} \\
\ce{S-O}&: 3.4 - 2.6 = \mathbf{0.8} \\
\ce{C-S}&: 2.6 - 2.6 = \mathbf{0}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Classify each of the three bonds in part (a) as nonpolar covalent, polar covalent or ionic. Justify each answer.

<Answer>

**Li–O is ionic.** Lithium is a metal and oxygen is a nonmetal, and the difference of 2.4 is large. Lithium's electron is, in effect, handed over to oxygen, so lithium oxide is made of $\ce{Li+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ ions.

**S–O is polar covalent.** Both atoms are nonmetals, so they share electrons, but oxygen pulls harder (a difference of 0.8). The shared pairs sit nearer oxygen, which is δ−, and sulfur is δ+.

**C–S is nonpolar covalent.** Both atoms are nonmetals with the same electronegativity on this scale, 2.6, so they share the bonding electrons evenly. Two different elements can still form a nonpolar bond: what matters is the difference in pull, not whether the atoms match.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify the type of bonding in a piece of magnesium metal, and describe what its valence electrons are doing.

<Answer>

**Metallic bonding.** Each magnesium atom gives up its two valence electrons to the whole solid. Those electrons are **delocalized**: they belong to no single atom and move freely among a regular array of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ions. The attraction between the positive ions and this sea of electrons holds the metal together. There is no electronegativity difference to calculate, since every atom is the same element.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for all three differences: 2.4, 0.8 and 0.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for each bond classified correctly with a reason: a metal with a nonmetal, or a large difference, for Li–O; two nonmetals with an unequal pull for S–O; equal electronegativities for C–S. A classification with no reason earns 0 points for that bond. "C–S is polar because the atoms are different" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for metallic bonding with the valence electrons delocalized over the whole solid. Describing the electrons as shared by one pair of neighboring atoms earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="A metal chloride on the borderline" topic="2.1" points={5}>

Magnesium chloride and aluminum chloride both pair a metal with chlorine. Electronegativities: Mg 1.3, Al 1.6, Cl 3.2. The table compares the two compounds.

| Property | $\ce{MgCl2}$ | $\ce{AlCl3}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Heated at atmospheric pressure | melts at 714 °C | turns straight to vapor at about 180 °C |
| The liquid | conducts electricity well | conducts electricity very poorly (it forms only under pressure) |

Molten magnesium chloride conducts well enough that passing a current through it is one way magnesium metal is made.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the electronegativity difference for the Mg–Cl bond and for the Al–Cl bond.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Mg-Cl}&: 3.2 - 1.3 = \mathbf{1.9} \\
\ce{Al-Cl}&: 3.2 - 1.6 = \mathbf{1.6}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

One working rule says that a metal bonded to a nonmetal is generally ionic. State the type of bonding this rule predicts for each compound.

<Answer>

**Ionic for both.** Each compound is a metal bonded to a nonmetal, and that rule says a metal with a nonmetal is generally ionic.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain what the properties in the table show about the bonding in aluminum chloride.

<Answer>

Magnesium chloride behaves like an ionic solid. It melts only at a high temperature, and the liquid conducts because its ions are free to move and carry the current.

Aluminum chloride does not. It becomes a vapor at only about 180 °C, so its particles are held to one another weakly. Its liquid barely conducts, so it has almost no ions free to move. That is how a substance made of molecules behaves, not one made of ions; the vapor is in fact made of $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ molecules.

So the **Al–Cl bonds are largely covalent**: the bonding electrons are shared, unevenly, rather than handed over from aluminum to chlorine.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain how the two compounds fit the idea that bonding is a continuum, and state which is the better guide to the type of bonding: that rule or the compound's properties.

<Answer>

Bonds between a metal and a nonmetal are not all equally ionic. Every polar bond has some ionic character, and the bigger the difference, the more it has. The Al–Cl difference (1.6) is smaller than the Mg–Cl difference (1.9), so Al–Cl sits farther toward the covalent end of the scale.

The difference alone, only 0.3 apart, would not have predicted so large a change in behavior, and the rule predicted "ionic" for both. **The properties are the better guide.** Here they overrule the rule.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for both differences, 1.9 and 1.6.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for ionic for both compounds, because each is a metal with a nonmetal.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for linking the low temperature and the poorly conducting liquid to weak attractions between particles and almost no mobile ions; 1 point for concluding that the Al–Cl bonds are largely covalent, with shared electrons. "Aluminum chloride is ionic because aluminum is a metal" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for naming the properties as the better guide, with Al–Cl placed nearer the covalent end of the continuum than Mg–Cl.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Which end is negative?" topic="2.1" points={4}>

Nitrogen forms single bonds to hydrogen in ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, and single bonds to fluorine in nitrogen trifluoride, $\ce{NF3}$. Electronegativities: H 2.2, N 3.0, F 4.0.

<Part label="a">

For an N–H bond, identify which atom is δ+ and which is δ−. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

Nitrogen (3.0) is more electronegative than hydrogen (2.2), so it pulls the shared pair harder and the pair sits nearer nitrogen. **Nitrogen is δ− and hydrogen is δ+.**

$$
\overset{\delta-}{\ce{N}}{-}\overset{\delta+}{\ce{H}}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For an N–F bond, identify which atom is δ+ and which is δ−. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

Fluorine (4.0) is more electronegative than nitrogen (3.0), so this time the shared pair sits nearer fluorine. **Fluorine is δ− and nitrogen is δ+.**

$$
\overset{\delta+}{\ce{N}}{-}\overset{\delta-}{\ce{F}}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student writes: "Nitrogen is very electronegative, so it always carries the partial negative charge in its bonds." Explain what is wrong with this statement.

<Answer>

A partial charge depends on **both** atoms in the bond, not on one atom's electronegativity alone. The δ− end is whichever atom of the pair is more electronegative. Nitrogen out-pulls hydrogen but is out-pulled by fluorine, so nitrogen is δ− in ammonia and δ+ in nitrogen trifluoride.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Calculate the electronegativity difference for each bond, and identify which bond has the larger bond dipole.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{N-H}&: 3.0 - 2.2 = 0.8 \\
\ce{N-F}&: 4.0 - 3.0 = 1.0
\end{aligned}
$$

Both are single bonds, and among single bonds a bigger difference means a bigger bond dipole. **The N–F bond has the larger bond dipole.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for nitrogen δ− and hydrogen δ+, justified by nitrogen's higher electronegativity. Labels with no reason earn 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for fluorine δ− and nitrogen δ+, justified by fluorine's higher electronegativity. Labels with no reason earn 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for explaining that the δ− atom is the more electronegative atom of the pair, so nitrogen's partial charge depends on its partner.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for N–F, with both differences (0.8 and 1.0) and the link from a bigger difference to a bigger dipole.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Ranking bonds to carbon" topic="2.1" points={4}>

Each compound in the table has a single bond from carbon to a different atom. Electronegativities: Li 1.0, C 2.6, O 3.4, F 4.0, Cl 3.2.

| Molecule | Bond |
|---|---|
| Chloromethane, $\ce{CH3Cl}$ | C–Cl |
| Methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$ | C–O |
| Fluoromethane, $\ce{CH3F}$ | C–F |
| Methyllithium, $\ce{CH3Li}$ | C–Li |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the electronegativity difference for each of the four bonds.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{C-Cl}&: 3.2 - 2.6 = \mathbf{0.6} \\
\ce{C-O}&: 3.4 - 2.6 = \mathbf{0.8} \\
\ce{C-F}&: 4.0 - 2.6 = \mathbf{1.4} \\
\ce{C-Li}&: 2.6 - 1.0 = \mathbf{1.6}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Rank the four bonds from least polar to most polar. Justify your ranking.

<Answer>

All four are single bonds, so the bigger the electronegativity difference, the bigger the bond dipole:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C-Cl} < \ce{C-O} \\
&< \ce{C-F} < \ce{C-Li}
\end{aligned}
$$

**C–Cl is the least polar and C–Li the most polar**, with the most ionic character of the four.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify the δ− atom in each bond.

<Answer>

In C–Cl, C–O and C–F, the atom bonded to carbon is more electronegative than carbon, so **Cl, O and F are δ−** and carbon is δ+.

Lithium (1.0) is much less electronegative than carbon (2.6). So in C–Li the shared pair sits nearer carbon: **carbon is δ−** and lithium is δ+. The bond has the largest difference of the four, yet it is the one where carbon is the negative end.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for all four differences: 0.6, 0.8, 1.4 and 1.6.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for the order C–Cl, C–O, C–F, C–Li, justified by the size of the difference. A correct order with no reason earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for Cl, O and F as the δ− atoms in their bonds; 1 point for carbon as the δ− atom in C–Li. Putting δ− on lithium earns 0 points for the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Explaining electronegativity trends" topic="2.1" points={4}>

Electronegativities: N 3.0, O 3.4, S 2.6. Nitrogen and oxygen are neighbors in period 2. Oxygen and sulfur are in group 16, with sulfur directly below oxygen.

<Part label="a">

Explain, in terms of atomic structure and Coulomb's law, why oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen.

<Answer>

Oxygen has 8 protons and nitrogen has 7. In both atoms the valence electrons are in the second shell, shielded from the nucleus by the same 2 inner electrons. The extra electron that oxygen has goes into that same shell, and electrons in the same shell shield one another very little.

So oxygen's valence shell feels more unshielded nuclear charge, and it is drawn in a little closer. By Coulomb's law, the attraction grows with the charge and shrinks with distance. More charge at a slightly shorter distance means **oxygen's nucleus pulls a shared pair of electrons harder than nitrogen's does.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain, in terms of atomic structure and Coulomb's law, why sulfur is less electronegative than oxygen.

<Answer>

Sulfur has 16 protons, twice as many as oxygen, but they are shielded by 10 inner electrons in two inner shells, against oxygen's 2 inner electrons in one. The extra protons are largely offset by the extra shielding.

Sulfur's valence electrons are in the third shell, farther from the nucleus than oxygen's second shell. By Coulomb's law, the attraction weakens as the distance grows. So **sulfur's nucleus pulls a shared pair of electrons less strongly than oxygen's does.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for oxygen's extra proton with the valence electrons in the same shell, so little added shielding; 1 point for linking the greater unshielded charge to a stronger Coulombic attraction for the shared electrons. "Oxygen wants electrons more" or "oxygen is closer to a full octet" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for sulfur's valence electrons in a shell farther from the nucleus, with its extra protons offset by more inner-shell shielding; 1 point for the weaker Coulombic attraction at the greater distance. "Sulfur is bigger" alone earns 0 points; with the farther shell and the extra shielding but no link to the attraction, it earns only the first point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

## Part B: Bond energy and Coulomb's law

Six questions on what holds two atoms or two ions together. Questions 6, 7 and 11 work with a bond's potential-energy curve; questions 8 and 9 explain bond length and bond energy from bond order and atom size; question 10 ranks attractions between ions.

<Question number={6} title="Reading a potential-energy curve" topic="2.2" points={5}>

Two atoms of an element X are brought together from far apart. The table describes the curve of their potential energy against the distance between their nuclei. The values are for illustration.

| Distance between the nuclei (pm) | Potential energy (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|
| 100 | $+237$ |
| about 110 | 0 |
| 145 | $-210$, the lowest point |
| 200 | $-117$ |
| 300 | $-18$ |
| 600 | about 0 |

To the left of 100 pm the curve climbs ever more steeply. Beyond 600 pm it stays at zero.

<Part label="a">

Determine the bond length and the bond energy of $\ce{X2}$. Explain why the bond length is not 110 pm, where the curve crosses zero.

<Answer>

The atoms settle where the potential energy is lowest. That is the bottom of the curve: 145 pm, at $-210\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

The bond length is the distance at the lowest point: **145 pm**.

The bond energy is the depth of the lowest point below zero, the energy of the separate atoms: **210 kJ/mol**. It is a positive number, because pulling the atoms apart takes energy.

At 110 pm the energy only happens to equal that of the separate atoms. There the nuclei are 35 pm closer than the bond length, so the repulsion between them outweighs the attraction and the energy is climbing steeply. Two atoms held that close would be pushed apart, back toward 145 pm. They settle at the lowest point, not at the crossing.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the energy needed to separate the atoms in 0.250 mol of $\ce{X2}$ molecules. State the energy change when 0.250 mol of $\ce{X2}$ forms from separate atoms.

<Answer>

The bond energy is the energy to break one mole of bonds, and each $\ce{X2}$ molecule has one bond:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.250\ \text{mol} \times 210\ \text{kJ/mol} \\
&= \mathbf{52.5}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

So **52.5 kJ must be supplied** to separate the atoms.

Forming the bonds is the same change run backward: the atoms drop from zero to the bottom of the curve. So **52.5 kJ is released** when 0.250 mol of $\ce{X2}$ forms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for 145 pm, read at the lowest point; 1 point for 210 kJ/mol as the depth below zero (a bond energy given as negative earns 0 points for this); 1 point for explaining that at 110 pm repulsion outweighs attraction, so the energy there is not the lowest. "The bond length is 110 pm" earns 0 points for the first award.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for 52.5 kJ needed to separate the atoms; 1 point for 52.5 kJ released when the molecules form (or an energy change of $-52.5\ \text{kJ}$). Saying that forming the bonds absorbs energy earns 0 points for the second award.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="Squeezing and stretching a bond" topic="2.2" points={4}>

The bond length of hydrogen chloride, $\ce{HCl}$, is 127 pm. A bond is not rigid: its nuclei vibrate about the bond length. Imagine the nuclei pushed in to 110 pm apart, and then pulled out to 160 pm apart.

<Part label="a">

For each distance, 110 pm and 160 pm, state whether attraction or repulsion outweighs the other, and identify the particles involved.

<Answer>

**At 110 pm, repulsion outweighs attraction.** The nuclei are closer than the bond length, and the two positive nuclei repel each other strongly. That repulsion grows steeply as the nuclei come closer, so it wins, and it pushes the atoms apart.

**At 160 pm, attraction outweighs repulsion.** The nuclei are farther apart than the bond length. Each nucleus attracts the shared electrons between them, and at this distance that attraction is larger than the repulsion between the nuclei. The net pull draws the atoms back together.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Of the three distances, 110 pm, 127 pm and 160 pm, identify the one at which the potential energy is lowest, and state how the energy at the other two compares with it.

<Answer>

The energy is lowest at **127 pm**, the bond length. At both 110 pm and 160 pm the energy is **higher**. Moving the nuclei closer or farther than the bond length always raises the energy, which is why the nuclei keep returning toward 127 pm as they vibrate.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student writes: "At 127 pm the H and Cl atoms no longer attract each other, which is why they stay at that distance." Explain what is wrong with the statement.

<Answer>

The atoms still attract each other at 127 pm. What changes is the balance: at the bond length, the attraction of each nucleus for the shared electrons is **exactly balanced by the repulsion** between the nuclei. Neither wins, so the energy is at its lowest and there is no net push or pull.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for repulsion between the nuclei outweighing attraction at 110 pm (with or without repulsion between the electrons); 1 point for attraction outweighing repulsion at 160 pm, described as the nuclei attracting the shared electrons or as each nucleus attracting the other atom's electrons. A distance with the right winner but no particles named earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for the lowest energy at 127 pm with both other distances higher.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for stating that attraction is still present at the bond length and is balanced by repulsion.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

The three carbon–oxygen bond lengths are 113 pm, 121 pm and 143 pm. Match each length to its molecule, and justify the match.

</Answer>

Identify the molecule whose carbon–oxygen bond has the largest bond energy. Explain, in terms of the shared electrons and the two nuclei, why a higher bond order makes a bond both shorter and stronger.

<Question number={9} title="Atom size in a refrigerant" topic="2.2" points={5}>

Dichlorodifluoromethane, $\ce{CCl2F2}$, was once widely used as a refrigerant. Its carbon atom is bonded to two chlorine atoms and two fluorine atoms. Each $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is about 175 pm long, and each $\ce{C-F}$ bond about 135 pm.

<Part label="a">

Explain, in terms of atom size, why the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is longer than the $\ce{C-F}$ bond.

<Answer>

Chlorine is below fluorine in group 17. Its valence electrons are in the third shell, while fluorine's are in the second, so **a chlorine atom is larger**.

In a bond, the nuclei can come only so close before they repel. A larger atom keeps its nucleus farther from the carbon nucleus, so the **nuclei sit farther apart** in $\ce{C-Cl}$, and the bond is longer.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

High in the atmosphere, ultraviolet light breaks $\ce{CCl2F2}$ molecules apart, and the bond that breaks is a $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond, not a $\ce{C-F}$ bond. Explain why this fits what the bond lengths suggest about the two bonds' energies.

<Answer>

In the longer $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond, the shared electrons are farther from the chlorine nucleus than they are from the fluorine nucleus in $\ce{C-F}$. Attraction weakens with distance, so the shared pair is held less tightly. **The $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond has the smaller bond energy**, so less energy is needed to break it, and it is the bond that breaks first. The shorter $\ce{C-F}$ bond is the stronger one.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Predict how a $\ce{C-Br}$ bond in $\ce{CBr2F2}$ compares with a $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond in $\ce{CCl2F2}$, in length and in bond energy.

<Answer>

Bromine is below chlorine in the same group, so its atom is larger still. The $\ce{C-Br}$ bond is **longer** and **weaker** than the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={10} title="Charge first, then distance" topic="2.2" points={5}>

The energy of attraction between a cation and an anion in contact is proportional to

$$
\frac{q_1 q_2}{d}
$$

where $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the sizes of the two charges and $d$ is the distance between the ions' centers, the sum of their radii. The table gives the radii for an ion with six neighbors. For each compound, compare one cation with one anion in contact.

| Compound | Ions | Cation radius (pm) | Anion radius (pm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{LiCl}$ | $\ce{Li+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ | 76 | 181 |

State the product of the charges, $q_1 q_2$, for the ion pair in each compound.

<Answer>

\ce{BaS}&: 2 \times 2 = \mathbf{4}
\end{aligned}

</Answer>

Rank the four ion pairs from the strongest attraction to the weakest. Justify the ranking by comparing the charges first and the distances second.

\begin{aligned}

\ce{BaS}&: 135 + 184 = 319\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

The ions in $\ce{MgS}$ get closer, so they attract more strongly. The ranking, strongest first:

\ce{MgS} > \ce{BaS} > \ce{K2S} > \ce{LiCl}

</Answer>

## Part C: Ionic solids

These four questions are about solids built from ions. You will rank lattice energies and melting points with Coulomb's law, explain why a salt shatters and when it conducts, and describe a particle picture of an ionic solid.

<Question number={12} title="Lattice energy from the distance between ions" topic="2.2, 2.3" points={6}>

Lattice energy is the energy needed to pull apart the ions in a mole of an ionic solid. The table gives the radii of four ions. Questions 13 and 15 use it too.

| Ion | Radius (pm) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Na+}$ | 102 |
| $\ce{Cs+}$ | 167 |
| $\ce{F-}$ | 133 |
| $\ce{Cl-}$ | 181 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate the distance between the centers of a neighboring cation and anion in sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$, and in cesium fluoride, $\ce{CsF}$. Treat the ions as spheres that touch.

<Answer>

When two ions touch, the distance between their centers is the cation's radius plus the anion's radius.

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{\ce{NaCl}} &= 102 + 181 \\
&= \mathbf{283}\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{\ce{CsF}} &= 167 + 133 \\
&= \mathbf{300}\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Use Coulomb's law to determine which compound has the larger lattice energy. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

The energy of attraction between the ions is proportional to the product of their charges divided by the distance between their centers, $q_1 q_2 / d$.

**Charges first.** Both compounds are made of 1+ and 1− ions, so the sizes of the charges multiply to $1 \times 1 = 1$ in each. The charges tie, so the distance decides.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{NaCl}&: \frac{1 \times 1}{283} \\
\ce{CsF}&: \frac{1 \times 1}{300}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same charge product sits over a shorter distance in $\ce{NaCl}$, so its energy of attraction is larger. **$\ce{NaCl}$ has the larger lattice energy.**

The difference is small, about 6 percent:

$$
\frac{300}{283} = 1.06
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The two lattice energies are about 788 kJ/mol and 744 kJ/mol. A student gives 788 kJ/mol to $\ce{CsF}$, reasoning that $\ce{F-}$ is the smaller anion, so $\ce{CsF}$ must have its ions closest together. Explain what this reasoning leaves out, and assign each value to its compound.

<Answer>

The distance between the centers depends on both ions, not on the anion alone. Going from $\ce{NaCl}$ to $\ce{CsF}$, the anion gets smaller ($\ce{Cl-}$ to $\ce{F-}$) but the cation gets larger by more ($\ce{Na+}$ to $\ce{Cs+}$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{anion}&: 133 - 181 = -48\ \text{pm} \\
\text{cation}&: 167 - 102 = +65\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

The large $\ce{Cs+}$ ion more than cancels the small $\ce{F-}$ ion, so the distance grows by 17 pm overall, and the energy of attraction is smaller. So **$\ce{NaCl}$: 788 kJ/mol** and **$\ce{CsF}$: 744 kJ/mol**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for each distance, 283 pm and 300 pm, from adding the cation's and the anion's radii. A distance from one radius alone earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for noting that the charge products are equal (1 in each), so the distance decides; 1 point for $\ce{NaCl}$, because its shorter distance gives the larger energy of attraction. "$\ce{NaCl}$" with no reason earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for explaining that the distance depends on both ions and that the larger $\ce{Cs+}$ outweighs the smaller $\ce{F-}$; 1 point for 788 kJ/mol to $\ce{NaCl}$ and 744 kJ/mol to $\ce{CsF}$.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={13} title="Matching melting points: charge before size" topic="2.2, 2.3" points={5}>

Cesium chloride, $\ce{CsCl}$, sodium fluoride, $\ce{NaF}$, and calcium fluoride, $\ce{CaF2}$, are ionic solids. Their melting points, in no particular order, are 646 °C, 993 °C and 1418 °C. A $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ion is very close in size to an $\ce{Na+}$ ion. For the other ions, use the radii in question 12.

<Part label="a">

Match each compound to its melting point. Justify the match with Coulomb's law.

<Answer>

**Charges first.** Multiply the sizes of the charges on the cation and the anion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{CsCl}&: 1 \times 1 = 1 \\
\ce{NaF}&: 1 \times 1 = 1 \\
\ce{CaF2}&: 2 \times 1 = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

$\ce{CaF2}$ has twice the charge product of the other two. Its ions sit about as far apart as those in $\ce{NaF}$, because $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is about the size of $\ce{Na+}$, so its energy of attraction is about twice as large. It has the largest energy of attraction of the three: **$\ce{CaF2}$, 1418 °C**.

**Then distance.** $\ce{CsCl}$ and $\ce{NaF}$ tie on charge, so the distance between the centers decides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{\ce{CsCl}} &= 167 + 181 \\
&= 348\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{\ce{NaF}} &= 102 + 133 \\
&= 235\ \text{pm}
\end{aligned}
$$

The ions in $\ce{NaF}$ are much closer together. By Coulomb's law the energy of attraction is larger by a factor of

$$
\frac{348}{235} = 1.48
$$

So **$\ce{NaF}$, 993 °C** and **$\ce{CsCl}$, 646 °C**.

The order, lowest melting point first:

$$
\ce{CsCl} < \ce{NaF} < \ce{CaF2}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain, in terms of the ions, why a stronger attraction between the ions gives a higher melting point.

<Answer>

In the solid, each ion is held in place by its attractions to all of its oppositely charged neighbors. To melt, the ions must gain enough energy to break out of those fixed positions and move past one another. The stronger the attractions holding them, the more energy that takes, so the solid must be heated to a higher temperature before it melts.

No bonds inside molecules are broken: an ionic solid has no molecules.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for $\ce{CaF2}$ highest because its charge product is 2 against 1; 1 point for $\ce{NaF}$ above $\ce{CsCl}$ because its ions are closer together (235 pm against 348 pm, or smaller ions); 1 point for all three matched correctly ($\ce{CsCl}$ 646 °C, $\ce{NaF}$ 993 °C, $\ce{CaF2}$ 1418 °C). A correct match with no reasons earns only the last point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for melting as the ions gaining enough energy to leave their fixed positions against the attractions of their neighbors; 1 point for linking a stronger attraction to more energy needed, and so a higher temperature. An answer about breaking bonds inside molecules earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={14} title="Why a salt shatters, and when it conducts" topic="2.3" points={5}>

<Part label="a">

A mineral collector sets a chisel on a crystal of fluorite, which is calcium fluoride, and taps it. The crystal does not dent. It splits cleanly along a flat plane. Explain this behavior in terms of the ions.

<Answer>

In the crystal, every ion's nearest neighbors carry the opposite charge. The blow pushes one layer of ions sideways against the layer next to it. A small sideways shift is enough to put $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ions next to $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ions and $\ce{F-}$ ions next to $\ce{F-}$ ions across that plane.

Like charges repel. The two layers push each other apart, and the crystal splits along the plane instead of bending.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student tests calcium chloride, $\ce{CaCl2}$, with a conductivity tester three ways: as the solid, as the molten salt, and dissolved in water. State whether it conducts in each case and explain each answer.

<Answer>

A current needs charged particles that can move.

**Solid: does not conduct.** The solid is full of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, but each ion is locked in place by its neighbors. Charges that cannot move cannot carry a current.

**Molten: conducts.** In the liquid the ions are free to move past one another, so they carry the current.

**Dissolved in water: conducts.** The ions separate and move freely through the solution.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the blow shifting one layer of ions against the next so that like charges meet; 1 point for the repulsion between like charges pushing the layers apart along that plane.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for the solid not conducting because its ions are fixed in place; 1 point for the molten salt conducting because its ions can move; 1 point for the solution conducting because its ions can move. "The solid has no charged particles" earns 0 points for the first point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

You are asked to draw a thin slice through a crystal of cesium fluoride, $\ce{CsF}$, as a grid of circles, each circle one ion. The radii in question 12 apply.

<Part label="a">

## Part D: Metals and alloys

Four questions on metallic solids. Question 16 uses the sea-of-electrons model to explain what a metal does. Questions 17 to 19 decide from atom sizes how a second element fits into a metal, and compare an alloy's hardness, ductility and conductivity with the pure metal's.

<Question number={16} title="Aluminum wire and foil" topic="2.4" points={6}>

Aluminum carries the current in many overhead power lines, and it can be rolled into kitchen foil thinner than a sheet of paper. An aluminum atom has three valence electrons.

<Part label="a">

Describe, in words, a particle diagram of a small region of solid aluminum that shows its metallic bonding. State what particles sit in the array, what charge they carry and where the valence electrons are.

<Answer>

The array is a regular arrangement of aluminum **ions**, all the same size. Each one is an aluminum atom without its three valence electrons, so each carries a charge of **3+**: it is $\ce{Al^3+}$.

The valence electrons are drawn as dots spread through the spaces between all the ions. They are **delocalized**: shared by the whole piece of metal, not held in pairs between two particular atoms, and free to move among the ions.

The attraction between the positive ions and this sea of electrons is the metallic bond. It holds every ion at once.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Your diagram shows 12 aluminum ions. Determine how many electrons the sea must contain for that region to be electrically neutral.

<Answer>

Each ion has given up three valence electrons to the sea, so the sea holds three electrons for every ion:

$$
12 \times 3 = \mathbf{36}\ \text{electrons}
$$

The 36 negative charges of the electrons balance the 36 positive charges of the twelve $\ce{Al^3+}$ ions.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why aluminum wire conducts electricity while it is solid.

<Answer>

A current is moving charge. The delocalized valence electrons are not tied to any one ion, so when the wire is connected to a voltage they move along it. That flow of electrons is the current. Nothing has to melt first: the electrons are free to move in the solid.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain, at the particle level, why aluminum can be rolled into thin foil without cracking.

<Answer>

Rolling pushes layers of aluminum ions to slide past one another. As a layer slides, the electron sea moves with it. After the slide every ion is still surrounded by the electron sea and still attracted to it, so no new repulsion appears. The metal changes shape instead of breaking: it is **malleable**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={17} title="Three additions to stainless steel" topic="2.4" points={6}>

A common grade of stainless steel, used for kitchen sinks, is about 18% chromium and 8% nickel by mass, with less than 0.1% carbon. The rest is mostly iron. The table gives each element's atomic radius.

|---|---|

| Chromium | 128 |
| Nickel | 124 |
| Carbon | 77 |

<Part label="a">

For each of chromium, nickel and carbon, state whether it forms a substitutional or an interstitial alloy with iron. Justify each answer by comparing its radius with iron's.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{Cr}&: \tfrac{128}{126} = \mathbf{1.02} \\

\end{aligned}

A carbon atom is only about 0.6 of an iron atom's size. It is small enough to fit into the gaps between the iron atoms, so carbon is **interstitial**.

One alloy can be both kinds at once: this steel is substitutional for chromium and nickel, and interstitial for carbon.

</Answer>

A complete diagram also shows the sea of delocalized valence electrons spread through the whole region.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

<Answer>
