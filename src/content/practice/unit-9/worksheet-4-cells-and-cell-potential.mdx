Work each question on paper before you open its answer.

One rule runs through this page: scale the half-reaction, never its voltage. To make the electrons cancel you may multiply a half-reaction by a whole number. Its potential stays as the table prints it.

You will need these relationships.

$$
E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}
$$

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ
$$

Use these values. Each half-reaction is written as a reduction, and $E^\circ$ is its standard reduction potential.

| Half-reaction | $E^\circ$ (V) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Ag+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}$ | $+0.80$ |
| $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$ | $+0.34$ |
| $\ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)}$ | $0.00$ |
| $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Pb(s)}$ | $-0.13$ |
| $\ce{Ni^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Ni(s)}$ | $-0.25$ |
| $\ce{Fe^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Fe(s)}$ | $-0.44$ |
| $\ce{Zn^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Zn(s)}$ | $-0.76$ |
| $\ce{Mg^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Mg(s)}$ | $-2.37$ |

Faraday's constant is the charge of one mole of electrons:

$$
F = 96{,}485\ \text{C/mol}
$$

Part 1(d) is about the signs of the electrodes and question 7 uses the names "oxidizing agent" and "reducing agent". The AP exam tests neither the signs nor the names.

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Parts of a galvanic cell" topic="9.8">

These parts are about the electrodes, the wire and the salt bridge of a galvanic cell.

<Part label="a">

Oxidation occurs at the <Blank />; reduction at the <Blank />.

<Answer>

Oxidation occurs at the anode. Reduction occurs at the cathode.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Electrons travel through the wire from <Blank />

<Answer>

From the anode to the cathode.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

State what the salt bridge does and what happens without it.

<Answer>

It carries ions between the half-cells to keep both solutions electrically neutral. Without it, oxidation leaves the anode solution positively charged and reduction leaves the cathode solution negatively charged; the charge separation stops electron flow almost immediately and the cell stops.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

The AP exam does not ask you to label an electrode positive or negative. Why not?

<Answer>

The sign convention reverses between cell types: the anode is negative in a galvanic cell and positive in an electrolytic one. Oxidation occurs at the anode in both, so identifying electrodes by process is unambiguous where identifying them by charge is not.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Galvanic and electrolytic cells" topic="9.8, 9.9">

Galvanic and electrolytic cells, each at standard conditions. Fill in:

<Part label="a">

Galvanic: $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ <Blank />, $\Delta G^\circ$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$E^\circ_\text{cell}$ is positive. $\Delta G^\circ$ is negative.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Electrolytic: $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ <Blank />, $\Delta G^\circ$ <Blank />

<Answer>

$E^\circ_\text{cell}$ is negative. $\Delta G^\circ$ is positive.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Which converts chemical energy to electrical? <Blank />

<Answer>

The galvanic cell.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

An electrolytic cell is which of topic 9.7's two strategies? <Blank />

<Answer>

Energy from outside. Topic 9.7 gives two ways to make an unfavored reaction go, energy from outside or a coupled reaction, and an electrolytic cell is the first: its energy is electrical, from a power supply.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Zinc and copper" topic="9.8, 9.9">

A galvanic cell is built from a zinc electrode in a solution of $\ce{Zn^2+}$ and a copper electrode in a solution of $\ce{Cu^2+}$.

<Part label="a">

Which metal is oxidized, and why?

<Answer>

Zinc. Its reduction potential is the lower of the two, so $\ce{Cu^2+}$ is the ion that is reduced and zinc is oxidized.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Write the balanced overall equation.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Zn^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate $E^\circ_\text{cell}$.

<Answer>

Copper's half-reaction is the reduction, at the cathode, and zinc's is the oxidation, at the anode:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E^\circ_\text{cell} \\
&= 0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.76\ \text{V}) \\
&= \mathbf{+1.10}\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

State what happens to the mass of each electrode as it runs.

<Answer>

The zinc anode loses mass as it is oxidized to $\ce{Zn^2+}$; the copper cathode gains mass as $\ce{Cu^2+}$ is reduced and plates onto it.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Part label="a">

<Question number={5} title="Copper and silver ion" topic="9.9">

For this reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Calculate $E^\circ_\text{cell}$.

<Answer>

Silver ion is reduced, at the cathode, and copper is oxidized, at the anode:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E^\circ_\text{cell} \\
&= 0.80\ \text{V} - 0.34\ \text{V} \\
&= \mathbf{+0.46}\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student writes this line:

$$
2(0.80) - 0.34 = 1.26\ \text{V}
$$

Identify the error and state the principle.

<Answer>

They scaled the silver half-reaction's potential along with its coefficients. A potential is energy per unit of charge (1 V = 1 J/C), so multiplying a half-reaction leaves $E^\circ$ unchanged. The coefficient is counted in $n$, not in $E^\circ$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Give $n$ for this reaction, and say where you read it from.

<Answer>

$n = 2$, from the balanced overall equation.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="Copper in hydrochloric acid" topic="9.9">

Will $\ce{Cu(s)}$ react with 1 M $\ce{HCl}$? Support your answer with a cell potential.

<Answer>

The proposed reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cu(s) + 2H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cu^2+(aq) + H2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The cathode would be $\ce{2H+}$/$\ce{H2}$, at 0.00 V. The anode would be $\ce{Cu^2+}$/$\ce{Cu}$, at $+0.34\ \text{V}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E^\circ_\text{cell} \\
&= 0.00\ \text{V} - 0.34\ \text{V} \\
&= \mathbf{-0.34}\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

Negative, so the reaction is not thermodynamically favored. **No**: copper does not dissolve in hydrochloric acid.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} title="The table as a ranking" topic="9.9">

Read the table of reduction potentials as a ranking.

<Part label="a">

Stronger oxidizing agent, $\ce{Ag+}$ or $\ce{Zn^2+}$? Justify.

<Answer>

$\ce{Ag+}$. A higher reduction potential means the species is more readily reduced, and a species readily reduced is by definition the stronger oxidizing agent.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Stronger reducing agent, $\ce{Mg}$ or $\ce{Cu}$? Justify.

<Answer>

$\ce{Mg}$. Its reduction potential is far lower, so the reverse process, oxidation of the metal, is strongly favored, which is what a good reducing agent does.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Which metal in the table cannot be oxidized by $\ce{Ag+}$? <Blank />

<Answer>

None of the other six. $\ce{Ag+}$/$\ce{Ag}$ has the highest reduction potential in the table, so $\ce{Ag+}$ can oxidize copper, lead, nickel, iron, zinc and magnesium.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="ΔG° from a cell potential" topic="9.9">

These parts use this relationship:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ
$$

<Part label="a">

For the zinc and copper cell of question 3 ($E^\circ = +1.10\ \text{V}$, $n = 2$), calculate $\Delta G^\circ$ in kJ.

<Answer>

Put $n$ in as moles of electrons. In joules first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ \\
&= -(2\ \text{mol})(96{,}485\ \text{C/mol}) \\
&\quad \times (1.10\ \text{V}) \\
&= -212{,}267\ \text{J} \\
&= \mathbf{-212}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For this reaction ($E^\circ = +0.46\ \text{V}$), calculate $\Delta G^\circ$ in kJ:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Answer>

Put $n$ in as moles of electrons. In joules first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ \\
&= -(2\ \text{mol})(96{,}485\ \text{C/mol}) \\
&\quad \times (0.46\ \text{V}) \\
&= -88{,}766\ \text{J} \\
&= \mathbf{-89}\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why does the calculation produce joules rather than kilojoules? <Blank />

<Answer>

$F$ is in C/mol, and 1 V = 1 J/C. Coulombs times joules per coulomb are joules.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Two cells share $E^\circ = +0.50\ \text{V}$ but transfer 1 and 4 electrons. Compare their voltages and their $\Delta G^\circ$ values.

<Answer>

The voltages are identical: potential does not depend on $n$. The free energy changes differ by a factor of four, because four times as much charge moves through the same potential difference, and $\Delta G^\circ$ is an amount of energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={9} title="One fact, three ways" topic="9.9, 9.10">

Complete the three-way correspondence for a cell at standard conditions:

<Part label="a">

$E^\circ > 0$: $\Delta G^\circ$ <Blank />, $K$ <Blank />, cell is <Blank />

<Answer>

$\Delta G^\circ < 0$, $K > 1$, and the cell is galvanic.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

$E^\circ = 0$: $\Delta G^\circ$ <Blank />, $K$ <Blank />, and with every concentration at 1 M the cell gives <Blank />

<Answer>

$\Delta G^\circ = 0$, $K = 1$, and with every concentration at 1 M the cell gives no voltage. A cell with $E^\circ = 0$ can still run when its concentrations differ (Worksheet 5's concentration cell does). A cell that has run down has $E = 0$, not $E^\circ = 0$.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

$E^\circ < 0$: $\Delta G^\circ$ <Blank />, $K$ <Blank />, cell <Blank />

<Answer>

$\Delta G^\circ > 0$, $K < 1$, and at standard conditions the cell runs only if driven from outside, as an electrolytic cell.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A cell has run down and gives no voltage. Has it used up its reactants? Explain.

<Answer>

No. It is at equilibrium: $Q$ has risen to $K$, so $E = 0$. The reactants are still there, in their equilibrium amounts, which are very small when $K$ is large, and nothing is left to push electrons through the wire.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
