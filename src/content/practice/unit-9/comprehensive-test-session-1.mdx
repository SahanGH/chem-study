## Part A: Entropy

Five questions on entropy. In the first two you compare samples of one gas by the room its particles have and by how widely their energy is shared, and you say what those comparisons do not decide. In the last three you work with standard entropies: backward from an entropy change, with a table that has gaps, and for three liquids that vaporize.

<Question number={1} title="Four flasks of krypton" topic="9.1" points={4}>

Four sealed, rigid flasks each hold 1.00 mol of krypton gas. Each flask has settled at the temperature shown.

| Flask | Volume (L) | Temperature (K) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 5.0 | 250 |
| B | 15.0 | 250 |
| C | 5.0 | 350 |
| D | 15.0 | 200 |

No value of entropy is given, and none is needed.

<Part label="a">

Three pairs of flasks differ in only one thing: A and B, A and C, and B and D. For each pair, identify the flask whose krypton has the higher entropy, and justify your choice.

<Answer>

Every flask holds the same amount of the same gas, so only the volume and the temperature can make a difference.

- **A and B: flask B is higher.** Both are at 250 K. Flask B has 15.0 L and flask A has 5.0 L, so each atom in B can be found anywhere in three times the space. More room means more ways to arrange the atoms, and so more entropy.
- **A and C: flask C is higher.** Both have 5.0 L, so the atoms have the same room. Flask C is at 350 K and flask A is at 250 K. At the higher temperature the atoms' kinetic energies range more widely, so there are more ways to share the energy among them.
- **B and D: flask B is higher.** Both have 15.0 L. Flask B is at 250 K and flask D is at 200 K, so the energy in B is shared more widely, for the same reason as in the pair above.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Four flasks make six pairs. Identify the three pairs that part (a) did not compare, and explain why the reasoning of part (a) cannot say which flask of each pair has the higher entropy.

<Answer>

The other three pairs are **A and D**, **B and C**, and **C and D**. In each of them one flask has more room and the other is warmer:

| Pair | More room | Warmer |
|---|---|---|
| A and D | D (15.0 L) | A (250 K) |
| B and C | B (15.0 L) | C (350 K) |
| C and D | D (15.0 L) | C (350 K) |

More room raises the entropy, and so does a higher temperature. In these pairs the two work in opposite directions. The reasoning of part (a) tells you which way each one pushes, but not how large each one is, so it cannot tell you which wins. That takes values of the entropy, or an equation that this course does not use, and neither is given here.

The answers to part (a) do not settle these pairs either. For example, flask B is above both A and D, and that says nothing about A against D.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Using only the comparisons of part (a), identify every flask that they do not rule out as the highest in entropy of the four, and every flask that they do not rule out as the lowest. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

Part (a) gave three results: B is above A, C is above A, and B is above D.

**Highest: flask B or flask C.** Flask A is below B, and flask D is below B, so neither A nor D can be the highest. None of the three results puts any flask above B or above C, and they do not rank B against C, so they leave either as the highest.

**Lowest: flask A or flask D.** Flask B is above A, and flask C is above A, so neither B nor C can be the lowest. None of the three results puts any flask below A or below D, and they do not rank A against D, so they leave either as the lowest.

</Answer>

</Part>

Three right choices with no reason earn 1 point in all.</RubricLine>

The three pairs with no reason, or the reason with a pair missing or wrong, earn 0 points.</RubricLine>

<Question number={2} title="A notebook of kinetic energies" topic="9.1" points={4}>

A sample of a gas is sealed in a rigid container. A student's notebook has three columns, X, Y and Z. Each is meant to show the percent of the sample's molecules whose kinetic energy lies in each of five bands, with the sample steady at one temperature. The temperature is different for each column, and the notebook does not say what it was.

The numbers are made up for this question, and the energies are in made-up units.

| Kinetic energy (units) | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | 46 | 28 | 30 |
| 10 to 20 | 30 | 27 | 30 |
| 20 to 30 | 14 | 19 | 20 |
| 30 to 40 | 6 | 12 | 15 |
| above 40 | 4 | 14 | 15 |

<Part label="a">

One column cannot be a correct record, whatever the temperature was. Identify it, and justify your choice with a calculation.

<Answer>

Every molecule is in exactly one band, so the five percentages in a column must add up to 100. Add each column:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{X: } &46 + 30 + 14 + 6 + 4 \\
&= 100
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Y: } &28 + 27 + 19 + 12 + 14 \\
&= 100
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Z: } &30 + 30 + 20 + 15 + 15 \\
&= 110
\end{aligned}
$$

**Column Z cannot be right.** It accounts for 110 percent of the molecules. At least one of its entries is wrong, so nothing can be read from it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify which of columns X and Y was recorded at the higher temperature. Justify your choice with numbers from the table.

<Answer>

**Column Y was recorded at the higher temperature.**

Compare where the molecules are. In the lowest band, X has 46 percent of the molecules and Y has only 28 percent. In the three bands from 20 units up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{X: } &14 + 6 + 4 = 24 \\
\text{Y: } &19 + 12 + 14 = 45
\end{aligned}
$$

So 45 percent of the molecules in Y have a kinetic energy of 20 units or more, against 24 percent in X. Fewer molecules at low energy and more at high energy means a higher average kinetic energy. The kelvin temperature of a sample measures the average kinetic energy of its particles, so Y is the hotter record.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify which of columns X and Y shows the sample with the higher entropy. Justify your choice from the way the kinetic energies are spread, and state why the room the molecules have plays no part in your choice.

<Answer>

**Column Y shows the higher entropy.**

In X the molecules are crowded into the low bands: the two lowest hold

$$
46 + 30 = 76
$$

percent of them, and the highest band holds only 4 percent. In Y the two lowest bands hold

$$
28 + 27 = 55
$$

percent, and no band holds fewer than 12 percent. The kinetic energies in Y are spread more evenly over a wider range. There is more energy to share and there are more ways to share it among the molecules, and that is a higher entropy.

The room plays no part because it is the same for both columns: the same sample, sealed in the same rigid container, has the same number of molecules in the same volume. Only the way the energy is shared differs.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Later the sample is moved, with no gas added or removed, into a rigid container with three times the volume. It settles at the temperature it had for column Y. Write the five percentages that a correct record would now show. Then rank the entropy of the sample in three states, from lowest to highest: as recorded in column X, as recorded in column Y, and after the move.

<Answer>

**The percentages are those of column Y: 28, 27, 19, 12 and 14.** The percentages show how the kinetic energy is shared among the molecules, and that goes with the temperature. The temperature is the same as it was for column Y, so the record is the same, whatever the volume.

**From lowest to highest: X, Y, after the move.**

- Y is above X by part (c): the same room, and the energy shared more widely.
- After the move the sample is above Y. The energy is shared exactly as before, as the equal percentages show, but each molecule can now be found anywhere in three times the space. More room means more ways to arrange the molecules.

After the move the sample is above Y, and Y is above X, so it is the highest of the three. The two columns alone do not show this last step: the record after the move is column Y again, and only the volume tells the two states apart.

</Answer>

</Part>

<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for column Y, justified with numbers: a smaller share of the molecules in the lowest band (28 percent against 46), or a larger share in the higher bands (45 percent against 24 from 20 units up, or 14 percent against 4 in the highest band), so a higher average kinetic energy. Column Y with no numbers from the table earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for column Y because its kinetic energies are spread more widely or more evenly (more ways to share the energy), with the statement that the volume is the same for both columns (the same sample in the same sealed, rigid container). "Y is hotter, and warming a gas at a fixed volume raises its entropy" also earns the point. A reason that gives the molecules in Y more room earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for both: the percentages of column Y unchanged (28, 27, 19, 12 and 14), because the temperature is the same; and the order X, Y, after the move, with the last place justified by three times the volume at the same temperature. New percentages, or the order with no reason for the last place, earn 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={3} title="A smudged entry in an entropy table" topic="9.2" points={4}>

Natural gas that contains hydrogen sulfide is called sour gas. Sour gas can also carry a little carbonyl sulfide, $\ce{COS}$. Carbonyl sulfide can be burned in a flare (reaction 1), or it can be changed by steam over a catalyst (reaction 2).

Reaction 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2COS(g) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CO2(g) + 2SO2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Reaction 2:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{COS(g) + H2O(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) + H2S(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A printed table of standard entropies at 25 °C has a smudge over the entry for carbonyl sulfide. Use these values.

| Substance | $S^\circ$ in J/(mol·K) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{O2(g)}$ | 205.2 |
| $\ce{CO2(g)}$ | 213.8 |
| $\ce{SO2(g)}$ | 248.2 |
| $\ce{H2O(g)}$ | 188.8 |
| $\ce{H2S(g)}$ | 205.8 |
| $\ce{COS(g)}$ | (smudged) |

For reaction 1 as written, at 25 °C:

$$
\Delta S^\circ = -154.8\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
$$

For reaction 2 as written, at 25 °C:

$$
\Delta S^\circ = -0.8\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
$$

<Part label="a">

Using only the equation of reaction 1, predict the sign of its $\Delta S^\circ$. State whether the value given agrees with your prediction.

<Answer>

Count the moles of gas on each side.

- Before: 2 mol of $\ce{COS}$ and 3 mol of $\ce{O2}$, 5 mol of gas.
- After: 2 mol of $\ce{CO2}$ and 2 mol of $\ce{SO2}$, 4 mol of gas.

Five moles of gas become four, so the matter ends with less room and $\Delta S^\circ$ should be **negative**. The value given, $-154.8$ J/(mol·K), is negative, so it **agrees**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Use reaction 1 to calculate the standard entropy of $\ce{COS(g)}$, the smudged entry.

<Answer>

$\Delta S^\circ$ is the products' total minus the reactants' total, each $S^\circ$ multiplied by its coefficient. Call the missing entry $x$. Every total below is in J/K.

The products' total can be found in full:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(213.8) + 2(248.2) \\
&\quad = 427.6 + 496.4 \\
&\quad = 924.0
\end{aligned}
$$

The reactants' total holds the unknown:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2x + 3(205.2) \\
&\quad = 2x + 615.6
\end{aligned}
$$

Products minus reactants is the value given:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&924.0 - (2x + 615.6) \\
&\quad = -154.8
\end{aligned}
$$

Solve for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x &= 924.0 - 615.6 \\
&\quad + 154.8 \\
&= 463.2 \\
x &= \mathbf{231.6}\ \text{J/(mol·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The standard entropy of $\ce{COS(g)}$ is **231.6 J/(mol·K)**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student says: "Oxygen is an element, so its entry is zero and could have been left out of the sum." Calculate the value of $S^\circ$ for $\ce{COS(g)}$ that the student would get from reaction 1. Then calculate the entry from reaction 2, which has no oxygen gas in it, and state which of the two values for reaction 1 it agrees with: yours or the student's.

<Answer>

With oxygen entered as zero, the student's total for the reactants of reaction 1, in J/K, is

$$
2x + 3(0) = 2x
$$

The products' total is 924.0 J/K, as in part (b):

$$
924.0 - 2x = -154.8
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x &= 924.0 + 154.8 \\
&= 1{,}078.8 \\
x &= \mathbf{539.4}\ \text{J/(mol·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now reaction 2. Every substance in it but carbonyl sulfide has an entry, and none of them is oxygen, so the student's claim changes nothing here. The products' total, in J/K:

$$
213.8 + 205.8 = 419.6
$$

The reactants' total is $x + 188.8$. Products minus reactants is the value given:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&419.6 - (x + 188.8) \\
&\quad = -0.8
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 419.6 - 188.8 + 0.8 \\
&= \mathbf{231.6}\ \text{J/(mol·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Reaction 2 gives 231.6 J/(mol·K). That **agrees with the value of part (b)**, and not with the student's 539.4 J/(mol·K). One substance has one entry, so the two reactions must give the same value, and they do only when oxygen is counted at 205.2 J/(mol·K).

So the entry for oxygen cannot be left out. The zero that an element has is its $\Delta H_f^\circ$, an agreed mark. Its $S^\circ$ is an amount like any other.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for a negative sign predicted from the moles of gas (5 mol before, 4 mol after), with the statement that the given value agrees. "Negative" with no count of the gas earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for a sum set up as products minus reactants with every coefficient and with oxygen at 205.2 (totals of 924.0 J/K for the products and twice the unknown plus 615.6 J/K for the reactants); 1 point for 231.6 J/(mol·K), or 232 J/(mol·K). An answer of 76.8 J/(mol·K), which comes from reactants minus products or from 154.8 entered without its minus sign, earns the first point only, and only if every coefficient and oxygen's 205.2 are in the sum.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for both: the student's value of 539.4 J/(mol·K) from reaction 1, and 231.6 J/(mol·K) from reaction 2, which agrees with the value found with oxygen at 205.2 and not with the student's. The student's value alone, or "elements do not have zero entropy" with no value from reaction 2, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Which values are enough?" topic="9.2" points={4}>

A table of standard entropies at 25 °C has only these nine entries. The entry for bromine is for its vapor, and reaction 3 is written with bromine as a gas. Use these values.

| Substance | $S^\circ$ in J/(mol·K) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{CH4(g)}$ | 186.3 |
| $\ce{CO2(g)}$ | 213.8 |
| $\ce{CO(g)}$ | 197.7 |
| $\ce{H2(g)}$ | 130.7 |
| $\ce{O2(g)}$ | 205.2 |
| $\ce{HCl(g)}$ | 186.9 |
| $\ce{Cl2(g)}$ | 223.1 |
| $\ce{Br2(g)}$ | 245.5 |
| $\ce{KCl(s)}$ | 82.6 |

Three reactions are of interest.

Reaction 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH4(g) + CO2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CO(g) + 2H2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Reaction 2:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2KClO3(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2KCl(s) + 3O2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Reaction 3:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2HBr(g) + Cl2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2HCl(g) + Br2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Part label="a">

Identify each reaction whose $\Delta S^\circ$ can be calculated from this table, and calculate it. For each of the others, state what is missing.

<Answer>

A sum needs an entry for every substance in the equation, elements included.

- Reaction 1 needs $\ce{CH4(g)}$, $\ce{CO2(g)}$, $\ce{CO(g)}$ and $\ce{H2(g)}$. All four are in the table.
- Reaction 2 needs $\ce{KClO3(s)}$, which is **not in the table**.
- Reaction 3 needs $\ce{HBr(g)}$, which is **not in the table**.

So **only reaction 1** can be calculated. The products' total, in J/K:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(197.7) + 2(130.7) \\
&\quad = 395.4 + 261.4 \\
&\quad = 656.8
\end{aligned}
$$

The reactants' total, in J/K:

$$
186.3 + 213.8 = 400.1
$$

Products minus reactants:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta S^\circ &= 656.8 - 400.1 \\
&= \mathbf{+256.7}\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sign fits the equation: 2 mol of gas become 4 mol.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For each reaction whose $\Delta S^\circ$ you could not calculate in part (a), decide whether the equation alone gives its sign. Where it does, give the sign. Where it does not, calculate the value of the missing entry at which $\Delta S^\circ$ would be exactly zero, and state the sign of $\Delta S^\circ$ if the entry is larger than that.

<Answer>

Count the moles of gas on each side.

**Reaction 2: the equation gives the sign, and it is positive.** There is no gas before and 3 mol of $\ce{O2}$ after. Three moles of gas made from a solid is a large gain of room, so $\Delta S^\circ$ is positive, and large. The missing entry would be needed for the number, not for the sign.

**Reaction 3: the equation does not give the sign.** There are 2 + 1 = 3 mol of gas before and 2 + 1 = 3 mol after. The moles of gas do not change, so $\Delta S^\circ$ is small, and its sign hangs on the missing entry. Call that entry $x$. The products' total, in J/K, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(186.9) + 245.5 \\
&\quad = 373.8 + 245.5 \\
&\quad = 619.3
\end{aligned}
$$

and the reactants' total is $2x + 223.1$. $\Delta S^\circ$ is zero when the two totals are equal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x + 223.1 &= 619.3 \\
2x &= 396.2 \\
x &= \mathbf{198.1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The entry is in J/(mol·K). An entry larger than 198.1 J/(mol·K) makes the reactants' total the larger one, so $\Delta S^\circ$ is **negative**. A smaller entry makes it positive. The table as it stands cannot say which.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

One more entry is found: $S^\circ$ of $\ce{HBr(g)}$ is 198.7 J/(mol·K). Calculate $\Delta S^\circ$ for reaction 3.

<Answer>

The products' total, in J/K:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(186.9) + 245.5 \\
&\quad = 373.8 + 245.5 \\
&\quad = 619.3
\end{aligned}
$$

The reactants' total, in J/K:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2(198.7) + 223.1 \\
&\quad = 397.4 + 223.1 \\
&\quad = 620.5
\end{aligned}
$$

Products minus reactants:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta S^\circ &= 619.3 - 620.5 \\
&= \mathbf{-1.2}\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

It is small, as part (b) said it would be: about one two-hundredth of the size of reaction 1's. Its sign is negative, and nothing short of the four values could have shown that.

</Answer>

</Part>

The value with no sign shown still earns the second point; a negative value does not.</RubricLine>

<Question number={5} title="Three liquids that vaporize" topic="9.2" points={4}>

Bromine, methanol and mercury are all liquids at 25 °C. The table gives the standard entropy of each as a liquid and as a gas, at 25 °C, in J/(mol·K). Each gas entry is for the vapor at standard pressure, as tables list it. Use these values.

| Substance | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Br2}$ | 152.2 | 245.5 |
| $\ce{CH3OH}$ | 126.8 | 239.9 |
| $\ce{Hg}$ | 75.9 | 175.0 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate $\Delta S^\circ$ for the vaporizing of 1 mol of each liquid at 25 °C. Give each with its sign.

<Answer>

Vaporizing is a change like any other, so $\Delta S^\circ$ is the final entry minus the initial one: the gas's $S^\circ$ minus the liquid's. For bromine the change is

$$
\ce{Br2(l) -> Br2(g)}
$$

and the other two are written the same way. Each result is in J/(mol·K).

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{bromine:} \\
&\quad 245.5 - 152.2 = \mathbf{+93.3} \\
&\text{methanol:} \\
&\quad 239.9 - 126.8 = \mathbf{+113.1} \\
&\text{mercury:} \\
&\quad 175.0 - 75.9 = \mathbf{+99.1}
\end{aligned}
$$

All three are positive, as they must be when a liquid becomes a gas.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student claims: "The liquid whose vapor has the highest standard entropy has the largest $\Delta S^\circ$ of vaporizing." Use the table and your results to decide whether the three liquids bear the claim out.

<Answer>

**They do not.** The vapor with the highest standard entropy is bromine's, at 245.5 J/(mol·K). If the claim held, bromine would have the largest $\Delta S^\circ$ of vaporizing. It has the **smallest** of the three: $+93.3$ J/(mol·K), against $+99.1$ for mercury and $+113.1$ for methanol.

$\Delta S^\circ$ is a difference, so it depends on where the liquid starts as well as on where the vapor ends. Bromine's vapor has the highest entry, but its liquid has the highest entry too (152.2), and the gap between the two is the smallest.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Write three orders of the three substances, each from lowest to highest: by $S^\circ$ of the liquid, by $S^\circ$ of the gas, and by $\Delta S^\circ$ of vaporizing. State which orders are the same.

<Answer>

From lowest to highest, in J/(mol·K):

- By $S^\circ$ of the liquid: mercury (75.9), methanol (126.8), bromine (152.2).
- By $S^\circ$ of the gas: mercury (175.0), methanol (239.9), bromine (245.5).
- By $\Delta S^\circ$ of vaporizing: bromine ($+93.3$), mercury ($+99.1$), methanol ($+113.1$).

**The order of the liquids and the order of the gases are the same**: mercury, methanol, bromine. **The order of the gains is different from both**: bromine, mercury, methanol. So for these three, neither column of the table shows which liquid has the largest $\Delta S^\circ$ of vaporizing. Only the subtraction does.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={6} title="Urea, read backward from two temperatures" topic="9.3" points={6}>

Fertilizer plants make urea, $\ce{CO(NH2)2}$, from ammonia and carbon dioxide:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2NH3(g) + CO2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO(NH2)2(s) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

No $\Delta H^\circ$ and no $\Delta S^\circ$ is given for this reaction. Take these two values of $\Delta G^\circ$ for the equation as written, and take $\Delta H^\circ$ and $\Delta S^\circ$ as constant.

| Temperature (K) | $\Delta G^\circ$ ($\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$) |
|---|---|
| 300 | $-6.2$ |
| 350 | $+15.1$ |

<Part label="a">

Without calculating, determine the sign of $\Delta S^\circ$ from the two values in the table. Then state whether the balanced equation agrees with that sign, and how you can tell.

<Answer>

**$\Delta S^\circ$ is negative.**

In $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$, the temperature multiplies only the entropy term. $\Delta H^\circ$ is taken as constant, so any change in $\Delta G^\circ$ on heating comes from $-T\Delta S^\circ$.

From 300 K to 350 K, $\Delta G^\circ$ goes up, from $-6.2$ kJ to $+15.1$ kJ. So $-T\Delta S^\circ$ became more positive as $T$ grew. That happens only when $-T\Delta S^\circ$ is a positive term, which means $\Delta S^\circ$ is negative.

**The equation agrees.** On the left there are 3 mol of gas. On the right there is a solid and a liquid, and no gas. Matter that was spread through the whole container as gas ends up in a solid and a liquid, so the entropy falls.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate $\Delta S^\circ$ for the reaction, in $\text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}$.

<Answer>

Write $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$ once for each temperature, and subtract the first from the second. $\Delta H^\circ$ is the same in both, so it drops out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta G^\circ_{350} - \Delta G^\circ_{300} \\
&\quad = -(350 - 300)\,\Delta S^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

The left side, in kJ:

$$
15.1 - (-6.2) = +21.3
$$

The temperatures differ by 50 K, so:

$$
\begin{aligned}
+21.3\ \text{kJ} &= -(50\ \text{K})\,\Delta S^\circ \\
\Delta S^\circ &= \frac{-21.3\ \text{kJ}}{50\ \text{K}} \\
&= -0.426\ \text{kJ/K}
\end{aligned}
$$

In joules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-0.426\ \text{kJ/K} \times 1{,}000 \\
&\quad = -426\ \text{J/K}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta S^\circ = \mathbf{-426}\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
$$

The sign is the one that part (a) predicted.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate $\Delta H^\circ$ for the reaction, in $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$.

<Answer>

Rearrange the equation to $\Delta H^\circ = \Delta G^\circ + T\Delta S^\circ$, and use either row of the table. $\Delta S^\circ$ is already in kJ/K from part (b): $-0.426$ kJ/K.

At 300 K, the product $T\Delta S^\circ$ is:

$$
(300)(-0.426) = -127.8\ \text{kJ}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ &= -6.2 + (-127.8) \\
&= \mathbf{-134.0}\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

The other row is a check. At 350 K:

$$
(350)(-0.426) = -149.1\ \text{kJ}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ &= +15.1 + (-149.1) \\
&= -134.0\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both rows give the same value. The reaction releases energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Answer>

</Answer>

## Part C: Kinetic control, and free energy and equilibrium

Five questions on what thermodynamics settles and what it leaves to the rate, and on the tie between $\Delta G^\circ$ and $K$. You will read tables in which the two pull against each other, find entries that cannot be right, and turn a measured mixture back into a free energy.

<Question number={11} title="A converter's two demands" topic="9.4" points={5}>

A sulfuric acid plant passes sulfur dioxide and oxygen through a converter to make sulfur trioxide:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{2SO3(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The table gives $K_p$ for this equation at four temperatures, and the time a mixture of the two reactants takes to come to equilibrium, with no catalyst and over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst. The $K_p$ values are calculated from tabulated data. The times are made up for illustration.

| $T$ (K) | $K_p$ | No catalyst (h) | Catalyst (h) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 298 | $7 \times 10^{24}$ | over $10^6$ | over $10^6$ |
| 700 | $9 \times 10^{4}$ | 2,000 | 0.5 |
| 900 | 46 | 30 | 0.02 |
| 1,100 | 0.37 | 0.8 | 0.001 |

<Part label="a">

Of the three columns of data, identify the one that thermodynamics fixes and the two that belong to kinetics. Then state which columns would read differently if the plant found a better catalyst.

<Answer>

**The $K_p$ column is thermodynamics.** At a given temperature $K_p$ depends only on the reactants and the products, the two ends of the reaction.

**The two columns of times are kinetics.** How long a mixture takes to reach equilibrium depends on the route between the two ends: on the activation energy, and on how many collisions have that much energy at the temperature.

A better catalyst gives a quicker route between the same two ends. So **only the "Catalyst" column** would change. The $K_p$ column would stay as it is, because the reactants and the products are the same substances. The "No catalyst" column would stay too, because that route has not changed.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Three vessels are filled with the same mixture of the two reactants, sealed, and held at 700 K with no catalyst. One vessel is opened and its gases are measured after 10 hours, one after 1,000 hours and one after 5,000 hours.

- **After 10 hours:** 0.60 atm of sulfur dioxide, 0.30 atm of oxygen and 0.020 atm of sulfur trioxide.
- **After 1,000 hours:** 0.12 atm of sulfur dioxide, 0.060 atm of oxygen and 0.50 atm of sulfur trioxide.
- **After 5,000 hours:** 0.020 atm of sulfur dioxide, 0.010 atm of oxygen and 0.60 atm of sulfur trioxide.

Calculate $Q$ for each sample. Then use the table to decide which sample is at equilibrium, and state the direction in which each of the other two is still changing.

<Answer>

The reaction quotient has the same form as $K_p$:

$$
Q = \frac{(P_{\ce{SO3}})^2}{(P_{\ce{SO2}})^2\,(P_{\ce{O2}})}
$$

After 10 hours:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.020)^2}{(0.60)^2(0.30)} \\
&= \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{0.108} \\
&= \mathbf{3.7 \times 10^{-3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

After 1,000 hours:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.50)^2}{(0.12)^2(0.060)} \\
&= \frac{0.25}{8.64 \times 10^{-4}} \\
&= \mathbf{2.9 \times 10^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

After 5,000 hours:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{(0.60)^2}{(0.020)^2(0.010)} \\
&= \frac{0.36}{4.0 \times 10^{-6}} \\
&= \mathbf{9.0 \times 10^{4}}
\end{aligned}
$$

At 700 K the table gives $K_p = 9 \times 10^{4}$. Only the sample opened **after 5,000 hours** has $Q$ equal to $K_p$, so it is the one **at equilibrium**. The times agree: with no catalyst a mixture at 700 K needs 2,000 hours, and this one had 5,000.

The other two were opened before 2,000 hours had passed, and each has $Q$ below $K_p$: by more than seven powers of ten after 10 hours, and by a factor of about 300 after 1,000 hours. With $Q$ below $K_p$ the **forward reaction** is still running in both, and sulfur trioxide is still forming. What those two vessels hold is set by how fast the reaction runs, not yet by $K_p$.

Over the catalyst all three samples would have read $9.0 \times 10^{4}$, because 0.5 hour is enough there.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={12} title="One flash, and a pair of lenses" topic="9.4" points={4}>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= 2(-95.3) - 0 \\

\end{aligned}

- **The observation.** The reaction ran to the end after the light was off. So the light was not paying for the change. It supplied the energy for the first bonds to break, and from there the favored reaction carried on without it.

</Answer>

Light did not start a reaction here that then ran without it. It has to keep arriving.

</Answer>

Magnesium sulfate is sold in two forms. The anhydrous solid, $\ce{MgSO4}$, is a white powder that chemists use to take traces of water out of other liquids. Epsom salt, sold for bath water, is the hydrate $\ce{MgSO4*7H2O}$: its crystals hold seven water molecules for each magnesium ion.

Both solids dissolve to give the same ions in water.

Equation 1, the anhydrous solid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MgSO4(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Equation 2, Epsom salt:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MgSO4*7H2O(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)} \\
&\quad +\ \ce{7H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

At 25 °C, use $\Delta H^\circ = -91.3\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ for equation 1, and take $\Delta H^\circ = +16.1\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ for equation 2.

<Part label="a">

A spoonful of each solid is stirred into its own cup of water at 25 °C. State which solid warms its water and which cools it. Justify your answer with the values given.

<Answer>

**The anhydrous solid warms the water, and Epsom salt cools it.**

$\Delta H^\circ$ of equation 1 is negative ($-91.3$ kJ/mol): the dissolving releases energy, the water around it takes that energy in, and its temperature rises.

$\Delta H^\circ$ of equation 2 is positive ($+16.1$ kJ/mol): the dissolving takes energy in from the water, and the water's temperature falls.

The two cups end with the same ions in them. The sign of the temperature change depends on the solid that the ions came from.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

When the dry powder takes up liquid water, it becomes Epsom salt, by equation 3:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MgSO4(s) + 7H2O(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{MgSO4*7H2O(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Equation 3 is thermodynamically favored at 25 °C. Take its $\Delta S^\circ$ as $-210$ J/(mol·K). Calculate $T\Delta S^\circ$ for equation 3 at 298 K. Then determine the sign that its $\Delta H^\circ$ must have, and the value that the size of its $\Delta H^\circ$ must be greater than.

<Answer>

**The entropy term is $-62.6\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$, so $\Delta H^\circ$ must be negative and greater in size than 62.6 kJ/mol.**

Change the entropy change to kilojoules first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-210\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)} \\
&\quad = -0.210\ \text{kJ/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The entropy term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T\Delta S^\circ &= (298)(-0.210) \\
&= \mathbf{-62.6}\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reaction is favored, so $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$ is below zero. Subtracting $-62.6$ adds 62.6:

$$
\Delta H^\circ + 62.6 < 0
$$

$$
\Delta H^\circ < -62.6\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

The entropy term works against equation 3: seven moles of liquid water take fixed places in a crystal. So the reaction can be favored only if it releases energy, and more than 62.6 kJ for each mole of the dry salt.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Dissolving a salt can be taken as three parts: (1) the ions leave the crystal; (2) water molecules move apart and rearrange to make room; (3) ions and water molecules attract. In a crystal of Epsom salt, six of the seven water molecules are already arranged around each magnesium ion, each with its oxygen atom turned toward the ion.

Identify the part that has largely taken place inside Epsom salt's crystal before the crystal meets any water, and say how the two values of $\Delta H^\circ$ show it. Then predict which solid's dissolving has the more negative $\Delta S^\circ$, and justify your choice.

<Answer>

**Part 3 has largely taken place already, and the anhydrous solid's dissolving has the more negative $\Delta S^\circ$.**

**The part.** Part 3 is the attraction of ions and water molecules. In Epsom salt each magnesium ion already has six water molecules held around it, so much of that attraction formed when the hydrate was made, not when it dissolves.

**What the values show.** Part 3 is the part that releases energy. The anhydrous solid has all of part 3 still to come, and its dissolving releases 91.3 kJ/mol. Epsom salt has much of it behind it, and its dissolving takes in 16.1 kJ/mol: what is left of part 3 no longer covers the energy taken in by parts 1 and 2. The two values differ by more than 100 kJ/mol. That difference is the energy released when the dry salt takes up its water, and it is well over the 62.6 kJ/mol that part (b) requires.

**The entropy.** Both solids end as the same ions, each holding water molecules turned toward it. Water held around an ion has fewer ways to be arranged than free liquid water, and that is the loss of entropy in dissolving.

- For the anhydrous solid, every water molecule that ends up held around an ion was free liquid water before. The whole loss takes place during the dissolving.
- For Epsom salt, six water molecules for each magnesium ion were already held in fixed places in the crystal. Dissolving costs them no freedom that they still had.

In both, the ions gain room as they leave the crystal. So the same gain is set against a much larger loss for the anhydrous solid, and its $\Delta S^\circ$ of dissolving is the more negative of the two.

</Answer>

</Part>

Both solids named with no reference to a sign or a value earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for the entropy term, $-62.6$ kJ/mol, from the entropy change in kJ/(mol·K) multiplied by 298 K, with the statement that the enthalpy change must be negative and greater in size than 62.6 kJ/mol (below $-62.6$ kJ/mol). <Question number={18} title="One donor, how many ATP" topic="9.7" points={4}>

A living cell remakes ATP from ADP and phosphate, a reaction that is thermodynamically unfavored when it is taken alone. One of the compounds that pays for it is phosphoenolpyruvate, which is made near the end of the breakdown of glucose. In words, the two reactions are these.

**Reaction 1:** phosphoenolpyruvate + H₂O → pyruvate + phosphate, with $\Delta G^\circ = -61.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$

**Reaction 2:** ADP + phosphate → ATP + H₂O, with $\Delta G^\circ = +30.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$

Every substance is dissolved in water, and the water is the liquid. Both values are for a neutral solution at 25 °C, the conditions biochemists' tables use. Use these values, with $T$ = 298 K and $R$ = 8.314 J/(mol·K).

<Part label="a">

Suppose one phosphoenolpyruvate were asked to pay for $n$ ATP: reaction 1, taken once, is added to reaction 2, taken $n$ times. Calculate $\Delta G^\circ$ of the overall reaction for $n$ = 1, 2 and 3. Identify the largest $n$ for which the overall reaction is thermodynamically favored.

<Answer>

**The largest is $n$ = 2.**

$\Delta G^\circ$ belongs to an equation as written. Reaction 2 taken $n$ times has $n$ times its value, and when equations are added, their values are added, signs included.

**$n$ = 1.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -61.9 + (+30.5) \\
&= \mathbf{-31.4}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**$n$ = 2.**

$$
2(+30.5) = +61.0\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -61.9 + (+61.0) \\
&= \mathbf{-0.9}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**$n$ = 3.**

$$
3(+30.5) = +91.5\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -61.9 + (+91.5) \\
&= \mathbf{+29.6}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sums for $n$ = 1 and $n$ = 2 are below zero, so those overall reactions are thermodynamically favored. The sum for $n$ = 3 is above zero, so that one is unfavored. The largest $n$ with a favored sum is 2, and its sum is below zero by less than 1 kJ/mol.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Reaction 1 makes phosphate and reaction 2 uses it. Write the overall reaction in words for $n$ = 1 and for $n$ = 2. Identify the one in which phosphate does not cancel, and state how many moles of phosphate are left in it and on which side.

<Answer>

**In the overall reaction for $n$ = 2, one mole of phosphate is left, among the reactants.**

**$n$ = 1.** Reaction 1 makes one phosphate and reaction 2 uses one. Water goes the other way: used by reaction 1 and made by reaction 2. Both cancel:

**phosphoenolpyruvate + ADP → pyruvate + ATP**

**$n$ = 2.** Taken twice, reaction 2 is 2ADP + 2 phosphate → 2ATP + 2H₂O. Reaction 1 makes one phosphate, and the doubled reaction 2 uses two, so one cancels and one is left as a reactant. In the same way one H₂O is left as a product:

**phosphoenolpyruvate + 2ADP + phosphate → pyruvate + 2ATP + H₂O**

The phosphate left among the reactants shows what the sum of part (a) hides. Phosphoenolpyruvate has one phosphate group to give. The first ATP takes that group. The second ATP shares nothing with reaction 1 and would have to take its phosphate from the solution. A sum below zero is one of the two conditions for coupling, and a shared substance is the other.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate $K$ at 298 K for the overall reaction with $n$ = 1 and for the overall reaction with $n$ = 2. Use the two values of $K$ to decide which of them, at equilibrium, has turned nearly all of the phosphoenolpyruvate into pyruvate.

<Answer>

Use $K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ/RT}$, with $\Delta G^\circ$ in joules.

**One ATP, $n$ = 1.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-31.4\ \text{kJ/mol} \\
&\quad = -31{,}400\ \text{J/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= \frac{31{,}400}{(8.314)(298)} \\
&= 12.67 \\
K &= e^{12.67} \\
&= \mathbf{3.2 \times 10^{5}}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Two ATP, $n$ = 2.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-0.9\ \text{kJ/mol} \\
&\quad = -900\ \text{J/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= \frac{900}{(8.314)(298)} \\
&= 0.363 \\
K &= e^{0.363} \\
&= \mathbf{1.4}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The one-ATP reaction is the one that turns nearly all of the phosphoenolpyruvate into pyruvate.** Its $K$ is hundreds of thousands of times larger than 1, so at equilibrium the products are far ahead of the reactants.

The two-ATP reaction has a $K$ of 1.4, which is close to 1. At equilibrium neither side is far ahead, and a large share of the phosphoenolpyruvate is still there.

"Favored" says only that $K$ is above 1. The size of $\Delta G^\circ$ beside $RT$, which is about 2.5 kJ/mol at 298 K, says how far above. Here 31.4 kJ/mol is many times $RT$, and 0.9 kJ/mol is less than half of it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={19} title="An antacid as a coupled pair" topic="9.7" points={4}>

Milk of magnesia is solid magnesium hydroxide stirred into water. Very little of the solid dissolves in the water of the bottle. In the acid of a stomach it dissolves readily. Three equations describe this, with values at 25 °C.

\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + 2OH^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}

Equation 2, acid with hydroxide ion:

\begin{aligned}

&\quad \ce{H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Equation 3, the solid in acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) + 2H^+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Use these values. Each $\Delta G^\circ$ was found from its $K$ with $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ at 298 K.

| Equation | $K$ | $\Delta G^\circ$ ($\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $5.61 \times 10^{-12}$ | $+64.2$ |
| 2 | $1.0 \times 10^{14}$ | $-79.9$ |

<Part label="a">

Identify the substance that equation 1 and equation 2 share. Determine how many times equation 2 must be taken for the two to add up to equation 3, and show the addition.

<Answer>

**They share the hydroxide ion, and equation 2 must be taken twice.**

Hydroxide ion is a product of equation 1 and a reactant of equation 2. Equation 1 makes two moles of it for each mole of solid, and equation 3 has none in it, so the favored partner has to use up exactly two moles. Equation 2 uses one mole each time it is written, so it is taken twice:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Add this to equation 1. The two moles of $\ce{OH^-(aq)}$ made by equation 1 are the two moles used here, so they cancel:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) + 2H^+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is equation 3. Check it: 1 Mg, 2 O and 4 H on each side, and a charge of 2+ on each side.

</Answer>

<Part label="b">

Calculate $K$ for equation 3 from the two values of $K$ in the table. Calculate $Q$ for equation 3, and decide whether more solid dissolves or solid forms.

<Answer>

**$K = 5.6 \times 10^{16}$ and $Q = 1.0 \times 10^{3}$, so more solid dissolves.**

When equations are added, their $K$ values are multiplied. An equation taken twice has its $K$ squared, because doubling the coefficients doubles every exponent in its $K$ expression. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_3 &= K_1 \times (K_2)^2 \\
&= (5.61 \times 10^{-12}) \\
&\quad \times (1.0 \times 10^{14})^2 \\
&= (5.61 \times 10^{-12}) \\
&\quad \times (1.0 \times 10^{28}) \\
&= \mathbf{5.6 \times 10^{16}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The answer has two significant figures, as $1.0 \times 10^{14}$ has.

$Q$ has the form of $K$ for equation 3. The solid and the liquid water are left out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{[\ce{Mg^2+}]}{[\ce{H^+}]^2} \\
&= \frac{0.10}{(1.0 \times 10^{-2})^2} \\
&= \frac{0.10}{1.0 \times 10^{-4}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.0 \times 10^{3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$Q$ is far below $K$, so equation 3 goes forward: **more solid dissolves.** With $[\ce{H^+}]$ held there, it dissolves until the solid is gone.

</Answer>
