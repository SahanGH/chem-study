## Part E: Galvanic and electrolytic cells

Four questions on how a cell is put together and what each part of it does. You will catch two reports of mass changes that cannot be true, choose the salt for a salt bridge and count the ions that leave it, read a refining tank from what is seen in it, and find the parts of a cell on a ship's hull, where nothing is drawn as a cell.

<Question number={1} title="Three reports, one cell" topic="9.8" points={5}>

A chromium strip stands in a solution of chromium(III) nitrate, and a silver strip stands in a solution of silver nitrate. A wire joins the two strips, and a salt bridge of potassium nitrate solution joins the two solutions. Nothing else is connected.

The reaction of chromium metal with silver ions, which gives $\ce{Cr^3+}$ ions and silver metal, is thermodynamically favored. The reverse reaction is thermodynamically unfavored.

The strips are weighed, the cell runs, and the strips are dried and weighed again. Three students each report the change in mass of each strip. Only one report can be true. The reports are made up for this question.

| Report | Chromium strip (g) | Silver strip (g) |
|---|---|---|
| A | $-0.520$ | $+3.236$ |
| B | $-0.520$ | $+1.079$ |
| C | $+0.520$ | $-3.236$ |

Use these values: the molar mass of chromium is 52.00 g/mol, and the molar mass of silver is 107.87 g/mol.

<Part label="a">

Write the half-reaction at each strip of this galvanic cell. Then calculate the mass of silver that forms while 0.520 g of chromium is oxidized, and identify the report that agrees.

<Answer>

A galvanic cell runs the thermodynamically favored reaction, so chromium atoms are oxidized and silver ions are reduced.

At the chromium strip:

$$
\ce{Cr(s) -> Cr^3+(aq) + 3e^-}
$$

At the silver strip:

$$
\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e^- -> Ag(s)}
$$

Moles of chromium oxidized, from grams and grams per mole:

$$
\frac{0.520}{52.00} = 0.0100\ \text{mol}
$$

Each chromium atom gives up 3 electrons. Moles of electrons:

$$
0.0100 \times 3 = 0.0300\ \text{mol}
$$

Every one of those electrons goes through the wire and is taken by a silver ion, and each silver ion takes 1. So 0.0300 mol of silver forms. Its mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.0300 \times 107.87 \\
&= \mathbf{3.24}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

The chromium strip loses 0.520 g and the silver strip gains 3.24 g. That is **report A**: its $+3.236$ g is 3.24 g to the three figures that 0.520 g allows.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the moles of silver that report B claims. Then explain, by counting electrons, why report B cannot be true.

<Answer>

Moles of silver in report B:

$$
\frac{1.079}{107.87} = 0.0100\ \text{mol}
$$

Report B has the same number of moles of each metal: 0.0100 mol of chromium lost and 0.0100 mol of silver gained. But the two metals do not trade electrons one for one.

The chromium, 0.0100 mol, releases 0.0300 mol of electrons. Silver ions that form 0.0100 mol of silver take only 0.0100 mol of electrons:

$$
0.0100 \times 1 = 0.0100\ \text{mol}
$$

That leaves 0.0200 mol of electrons released at one strip and taken by nothing at the other. Electrons cannot pile up in a wire: every electron the anode releases is used at the cathode. **Report B matches the moles of metal where it should match the moles of electrons**, so it cannot be true.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Show that the two masses in report C do pass the count of electrons. Then state why report C still cannot be true for this cell.

<Answer>

Report C has the silver strip losing mass and the chromium strip gaining mass, so it describes silver atoms being oxidized and $\ce{Cr^3+}$ ions being reduced.

Moles of silver lost:

$$
\frac{3.236}{107.87} = 0.0300\ \text{mol}
$$

Each silver atom gives up 1 electron, so 0.0300 mol of electrons is released.

Moles of chromium gained:

$$
\frac{0.520}{52.00} = 0.0100\ \text{mol}
$$

Each $\ce{Cr^3+}$ ion takes 3 electrons, so 0.0300 mol of electrons is taken. The count balances: 0.0300 mol released and 0.0300 mol taken.

The fault is the direction. Report C is the reverse reaction, which is thermodynamically unfavored. A galvanic cell runs only the favored direction, and this cell has a wire and no power supply, so nothing is there to drive the reverse. **Report C counts its electrons correctly but runs the cell backward.**

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

For the run of part (a), each half-cell held 250.0 mL of solution, and each solution began at 0.200 M in its metal ion. Take the volumes as unchanged. Calculate the concentration of $\ce{Cr^3+}$ and the concentration of $\ce{Ag^+}$ at the end of the run. Then identify the ion whose concentration changed by more, and state how many times more.

<Answer>

Each solution began with the same moles of its metal ion, from liters and moles per liter:

$$
0.2500 \times 0.200 = 0.0500\ \text{mol}
$$

**The chromium half-cell.** The chromium strip is the anode, and oxidation there adds ions to the solution: 0.0100 mol of $\ce{Cr^3+}$, from part (a).

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.0500 + 0.0100 \\
&= 0.0600\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{0.0600}{0.2500} = \mathbf{0.240}\ \text{M}
$$

**The silver half-cell.** The electrons reach the silver strip through the wire, and reduction there takes ions out of the solution: 0.0300 mol of $\ce{Ag^+}$, from part (a).

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.0500 - 0.0300 \\
&= 0.0200\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{0.0200}{0.2500} = \mathbf{0.0800}\ \text{M}
$$

The concentration of $\ce{Cr^3+}$ rose:

$$
0.240 - 0.200 = 0.040\ \text{M}
$$

The concentration of $\ce{Ag^+}$ fell:

$$
0.200 - 0.0800 = 0.120\ \text{M}
$$

$$
\frac{0.120}{0.040} = 3.0
$$

**The concentration of the silver ion changed by more, 3 times as much.** The 3 electrons that one chromium atom gives up are taken by 3 silver ions, so 3 silver ions leave one solution for each chromium ion that enters the other.

</Answer>

</Part>

Report A named with no calculation does not earn the second point. State symbols are not required for the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for 0.0100 mol of silver together with the reason: that much silver takes 0.0100 mol of electrons while the chromium releases 0.0300 mol (or silver must be three times the moles of chromium, 0.0300 mol, and report B has them equal). "The masses are wrong" with no count of moles or electrons earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for both: the count balances (0.0300 mol of electrons released by the silver and 0.0300 mol taken by 0.0100 mol of chromium), and the report describes the thermodynamically unfavored direction, which a galvanic cell (a cell with no power supply) does not run. Either half alone earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for all three: 0.240 M for $\ce{Cr^3+}$; 0.0800 M for $\ce{Ag^+}$ (0.080 M earns it); and the silver ion named as changing by 3 times as much (0.120 M against 0.040 M). Both concentrations moved the same way, or both changed by the same amount, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Choosing the salt for the bridge" topic="9.8" points={5}>

A galvanic cell has a cadmium strip in a solution of cadmium nitrate and a lead strip in a solution of lead(II) nitrate. A wire joins the strips. In this cell the cadmium strip is the anode.

The salt bridge has still to be filled. Each end of the bridge will stand in one of the two solutions, so the solution in the bridge touches both of them. Five salts are on the shelf:

- potassium nitrate, $\ce{KNO3}$
- ammonium nitrate, $\ce{NH4NO3}$
- magnesium nitrate, $\ce{Mg(NO3)2}$
- sodium sulfate, $\ce{Na2SO4}$
- potassium sulfide, $\ce{K2S}$

Use these facts about solubility in water.

- Every nitrate is soluble. So is every salt of potassium, of sodium and of ammonium.
- Lead(II) sulfate, lead(II) sulfide and cadmium sulfide are insoluble.
- Cadmium sulfate is soluble.

<Part label="a">

Identify the salts on the shelf that can fill the bridge. Justify your choice with the solubility facts.

<Answer>

The bridge's ions must carry charge and react with nothing in the cell. Each end of the bridge touches a solution: one holds $\ce{Cd^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3^-}$ ions, the other $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3^-}$ ions. So no ion of the bridge may form an insoluble compound with $\ce{Cd^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ or $\ce{NO3^-}$.

**Potassium nitrate, ammonium nitrate and magnesium nitrate can fill the bridge.** Their anion is nitrate, and every nitrate is soluble, so it forms no solid with $\ce{Cd^2+}$ or $\ce{Pb^2+}$. Their cations, $\ce{K^+}$, $\ce{NH4^+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$, meet only nitrate ions, and every nitrate is soluble.

The other two salts each bring an anion that forms a solid with a metal ion of the cell. Part (b) finds where.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For each salt that cannot fill the bridge, identify every solid that forms and the end of the bridge where it forms.

<Answer>

**Sodium sulfate: lead(II) sulfate forms at the end in the lead solution.** At that end the solution in the bridge touches the lead solution, and where two solutions touch, their ions mix. Sulfate ions meet $\ce{Pb^2+}$ ions there as soon as the bridge is lowered in, before any electron has gone through the wire:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbSO4(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

No solid forms at the end in the cadmium solution, because cadmium sulfate is soluble. One bad end is enough to rule the salt out.

Running the cell does not save this salt. While the cell runs, sulfate ions are driven toward the anode, which is away from the lead end. But the two solutions still touch at that end, their ions still mix there, and the solid forms all the same.

**Potassium sulfide: a solid forms at both ends.** Cadmium sulfide forms at the end in the cadmium solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cd^2+(aq) + S^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CdS(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Lead(II) sulfide forms at the end in the lead solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + S^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{PbS(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sodium and potassium ions form no solid anywhere. In each case it is the anion that rules the salt out.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The bridge is filled with magnesium nitrate solution, and the cell runs until 0.020 mol of electrons has gone through the wire. Calculate the moles of $\ce{Cd^2+}$ ions made and the moles of $\ce{Pb^2+}$ ions used up.

<Answer>

Cadmium is the anode, so cadmium atoms are oxidized:

$$
\ce{Cd(s) -> Cd^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

Lead is then the cathode, where lead(II) ions are reduced:

$$
\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Pb(s)}
$$

Each half-reaction has 2 electrons for 1 ion, so the moles of each ion are half the moles of electrons:

$$
\frac{0.020}{2} = 0.010\ \text{mol}
$$

**0.010 mol** of $\ce{Cd^2+}$ is made in the cadmium half-cell, and **0.010 mol** of $\ce{Pb^2+}$ is used up in the lead half-cell.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Suppose that the only ions that cross between the bridge and a solution are the bridge's own ions leaving it. For the run in part (c), determine which ion enters each half-cell from the bridge, and how many moles of it. Then determine the moles of magnesium nitrate that the bridge has given up.

<Answer>

Each solution has to stay neutral. What has to balance is charge, not a count of ions.

**The cadmium half-cell** gained 0.010 mol of $\ce{Cd^2+}$. Each ion carries a charge of 2+, so the positive charge added is

$$
0.010 \times 2 = 0.020\ \text{mol}
$$

A nitrate ion carries a charge of 1−, so it takes **0.020 mol** of $\ce{NO3^-}$ from the bridge to balance it. Anions move toward the anode.

**The lead half-cell** lost 0.010 mol of $\ce{Pb^2+}$, which is 0.020 mol of positive charge, and the nitrate ions those lead ions were balancing are still in the solution. A magnesium ion carries a charge of 2+, so the moles of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ that replace that charge are

$$
\frac{0.020}{2} = 0.010\ \text{mol}
$$

So **0.010 mol** of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ enters from the bridge. Cations move toward the cathode.

The two amounts of ions are not equal: 0.020 mol at one end and 0.010 mol at the other. The charge is equal: 0.020 mol at each end, the same as the moles of electrons that went through the wire.

**The bridge has given up 0.010 mol of magnesium nitrate.** It lost 0.020 mol of $\ce{NO3^-}$ and 0.010 mol of $\ce{Mg^2+}$, which is 2 nitrate ions for each magnesium ion, the ratio in $\ce{Mg(NO3)2}$. So the solution left in the bridge is still neutral.

</Answer>

</Part>

The three salts named with no reason earn 0 points. Sodium sulfate accepted because sulfate ions move toward the cadmium half-cell earns 0 points: the bridge's solution touches the lead solution whichever way the ions are driven.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for sodium sulfate: lead(II) sulfate, at the lead end only. 1 point for potassium sulfide: cadmium sulfide at the cadmium end and lead(II) sulfide at the lead end. Equations are not required. A solid of sodium or potassium, or cadmium sulfate named as a solid, does not earn that salt's point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for 0.010 mol of each ion, with $\ce{Cd^2+}$ made and $\ce{Pb^2+}$ used up, from 2 electrons for each ion. An answer of 0.020 mol or 0.040 mol of each earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for all three: 0.020 mol of nitrate ions into the cadmium half-cell; 0.010 mol of magnesium ions into the lead half-cell; and 0.010 mol of magnesium nitrate given up by the bridge. Amounts that follow correctly from the student's own answer to (c) earn the point. An answer of 0.020 mol of magnesium ions, the charge of 2+ on each not counted, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="One tank, one half-reaction, two directions" topic="9.8" points={4}>

Copper for electrical wire has to be very pure. To purify it, a thick slab of impure copper and a thin sheet of pure copper are hung in one tank of copper(II) sulfate solution, and each is joined by a cable to a power supply. The slab is nearly all copper, with a little zinc and a little silver mixed through it. There is no salt bridge.

While the supply is on, these things are observed.

- The thin sheet grows thicker, and what is added to it is copper.
- The slab shrinks.
- The solution is blue, the color of $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ions in water, and the blue does not visibly change.
- Zinc ions, which are colorless, slowly build up in the solution.
- A sludge collects on the floor of the tank under the slab. It contains silver metal.

<Part label="a">

Write the half-reaction that copper undergoes at the slab and the one it undergoes at the sheet. Identify the anode and the cathode, and identify the electrode into which the power supply pushes electrons.

<Answer>

The slab shrinks: copper atoms leave it for the solution, as ions.

$$
\ce{Cu(s) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

That is oxidation, so **the slab is the anode.**

The sheet grows: copper(II) ions come out of the solution onto it, as atoms.

$$
\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Cu(s)}
$$

That is reduction, so **the sheet is the cathode.**

It is one half-reaction, running in opposite directions at the two electrodes.

**The supply pushes electrons into the thin sheet.** At the sheet, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ions gain electrons. Those electrons have to arrive through the cable, so the sheet is the electrode the supply pushes electrons into. At the slab, copper atoms leave their electrons behind in the metal, and the supply pulls them out through the other cable.

In the cables the electrons go from the slab, the anode, through the supply to the sheet, the cathode.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

In one run the slab loses 96.6 g in all, and the sheet gains 95.3 g of copper. Take the molar mass of copper as 63.55 g/mol, and take every electron that the supply moves as used to turn one kind of copper particle into the other. Calculate the moles of electrons that the supply moved to add 95.3 g of copper to the sheet. Then calculate the moles of electrons that would go with 96.6 g of copper, and decide whether everything the slab lost can have been copper changing to or from $\ce{Cu^2+}$.

<Answer>

Moles of copper added to the sheet, from grams and grams per mole:

$$
\frac{95.3}{63.55} = 1.50\ \text{mol}
$$

Each $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ion takes 2 electrons. Moles of electrons that went into the sheet:

$$
1.50 \times 2 = \mathbf{3.00}\ \text{mol}
$$

The supply pulled those same electrons out of the slab. The cables and the supply store none, so the atoms of the slab left behind 3.00 mol of electrons in this run.

Now suppose all 96.6 g were copper oxidized to $\ce{Cu^2+}$:

$$
\frac{96.6}{63.55} = 1.52\ \text{mol}
$$

$$
1.52 \times 2 = \mathbf{3.04}\ \text{mol}
$$

That is more than the 3.00 mol that passed:

$$
3.04 - 3.00 = 0.04\ \text{mol}
$$

**No.** Not everything the slab lost was copper oxidized to $\ce{Cu^2+}$. The slab left behind 0.04 mol fewer electrons than 96.6 g of copper would. Some of the mass it lost left fewer electrons behind than the same mass of copper does. Part (d) finds what that was.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Suppose for this part that copper is the only metal in the slab that reacts. For every 2.0 mol of electrons the supply moves, determine the moles of $\ce{Cu^2+}$ that enter the solution, the moles that leave it, and the net change.

<Answer>

Each half-reaction of part (a) has 2 electrons for 1 copper(II) ion.

At the slab, 2.0 mol of electrons are left behind by

$$
\frac{2.0}{2} = 1.0\ \text{mol}
$$

of copper atoms, so **1.0 mol** of $\ce{Cu^2+}$ enters the solution.

At the sheet, the same 2.0 mol of electrons are taken by 1.0 mol of copper(II) ions, so **1.0 mol** of $\ce{Cu^2+}$ leaves the solution.

The net change:

$$
1.0 - 1.0 = \mathbf{0}\ \text{mol}
$$

There is **no net change.** This agrees with what is seen: the blue does not change. The copper is only carried from the slab to the sheet.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Decide from the observations which impurity, zinc or silver, was oxidized and which was not. Give the observation that decides each.

<Answer>

**Zinc was oxidized.** Zinc ions build up in the solution, and the only zinc in the tank began as atoms in the slab. An atom that becomes an ion has lost electrons:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

**Silver was not oxidized.** The sludge contains silver metal, which is silver atoms. As the copper around them went into solution, the silver atoms came loose from the slab and fell, still as metal. They never lost electrons.

The observations also say what does not happen at the sheet. Zinc ions build up in the solution, so they are not being reduced there, and the stem says that what is added to the sheet is copper. Zinc stays in the solution, silver stays on the floor of the tank, and only copper reaches the sheet.

One more thing follows. Each zinc atom oxidized at the slab leaves 2 electrons that remove one $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ion at the sheet, and no $\ce{Cu^2+}$ ion enters to replace it. So the count of part (c) is not quite exact: the amount of $\ce{Cu^2+}$ falls very slowly, too slowly to see, because the slab holds only a little zinc.

</Answer>

</Part>

State symbols are not required for the point. A plus or minus sign given to an electrode in place of its name earns 0 points.</RubricLine>

"No" with no count, or "the two masses differ" alone, earns 0 points.</RubricLine>

</Rubric>

<Question number={4} title="A cell with no beakers" topic="9.8" points={4}>

A block of zinc is bolted to the steel hull of a ship, below the waterline. Steel is mostly iron. The bolt is steel too, so metal touches metal all the way from the block to the hull. Seawater is a solution of ions, mostly $\ce{Na^+}$ and $\ce{Cl^-}$.

Over one season these things are observed.

- The zinc block slowly gets smaller, and $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ions enter the seawater around it.
- The steel does not corrode.
- At the surface of the steel, oxygen dissolved in the seawater is reduced:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{O2(aq) + 2H2O(l) + 4e^- ->} \\
&\quad \ce{4OH^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The block and the hull form a galvanic cell, though nothing here looks like two beakers, a wire and a salt bridge.

<Part label="a">

Identify the anode of this cell and write the half-reaction that happens there.

<Answer>

**The zinc block is the anode.** It gets smaller because zinc atoms leave it as ions, and an atom that becomes a positive ion has lost electrons. That is oxidation, and the electrode where oxidation happens is the anode.

$$
\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A galvanic cell on a lab bench has a cathode, a wire and a salt bridge. Identify what does the job of each on the hull.

<Answer>

**The cathode is the surface of the steel.** Reduction happens there: oxygen molecules take electrons.

**The wire is the metal itself:** the zinc block, the bolt and the steel of the hull, touching one another. Electrons left in the zinc travel through the metal to the steel surface, from the anode to the cathode, as they would through a wire.

**The salt bridge is the seawater.** Around the zinc, positive $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ions are being added. At the steel, negative $\ce{OH^-}$ ions are being made. The ions of the seawater move between the two places and keep every part of the water neutral: anions such as $\ce{Cl^-}$ toward the zinc, the anode, and cations such as $\ce{Na^+}$ toward the steel, the cathode. Here one solution touches both electrodes, so it carries the ions itself.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

In the season the block loses 6.54 g of zinc. Take the molar mass of zinc as 65.38 g/mol, and suppose that every electron the zinc gives up is taken by oxygen at the steel and that every ion formed stays dissolved. Calculate the moles of oxygen reduced. Then state the change in the mass of the steel that this cell causes, with a reason.

<Answer>

Moles of zinc oxidized:

$$
\frac{6.54}{65.38} = 0.100\ \text{mol}
$$

Each zinc atom gives up 2 electrons. Moles of electrons:

$$
0.100 \times 2 = 0.200\ \text{mol}
$$

The half-reaction at the steel has 4 electrons for 1 oxygen molecule. Moles of oxygen:

$$
\frac{0.200}{4} = \mathbf{0.0500}\ \text{mol}
$$

**The mass of the steel changes by zero.** The steel is an electrode that only hands electrons over. The species reduced at it is oxygen from the water, and the product is hydroxide ions, which stay dissolved. No iron atom leaves the steel and, in this picture, where every ion stays dissolved, nothing is deposited on it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

On another ship a block of copper is bolted to the hull in place of the zinc. The copper block does not change. The steel near it corrodes, faster than steel with no block at all, and $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ions enter the seawater. Oxygen is still reduced. Identify the metal that is the anode now, write its half-reaction, and identify the surface that only hands electrons over.

<Answer>

**The steel is the anode now.** Its iron atoms are the ones leaving as ions, which is oxidation:

$$
\ce{Fe(s) -> Fe^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

**The copper block is now the surface that only hands electrons over.** It does not change, so no copper atom is oxidized and nothing is deposited on it. Electrons left in the steel pass through the bolt to the copper, where dissolved oxygen takes them. The copper is a cathode.

The steel was a cathode beside zinc and is an anode beside copper. A metal has no fixed role: what happens at its surface names it.

</Answer>

</Part>

| 3 | Z | 0.16 | gained mass |

\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\ce{M^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> M(s)}
$$

The standard cell potential is the cathode's reduction potential minus the anode's, each as a table would print it. So the first job is to find which strip is the cathode, and the mass of the cobalt strip tells you.

$$
\begin{aligned}

E^\circ_\text{X} &= -0.28 - 0.48 \\
&= \mathbf{-0.76}\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}

\begin{aligned}
0.15 &= E^\circ_\text{Y} - (-0.28) \\
E^\circ_\text{Y} &= 0.15 - 0.28 \\
&= \mathbf{-0.13}\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}

\begin{aligned}
0.16 &= -0.28 - E^\circ_\text{Z} \\
E^\circ_\text{Z} &= -0.28 - 0.16 \\
&= \mathbf{-0.44}\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}

|---|---|

Use these values. $R$ = 8.314 J/(mol·K), and $F$ = 96,485 C per mole of electrons.

$$
\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}

<Answer>

</Answer>

</Part>

<Answer>

\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}

\end{aligned}

\end{aligned}

Tin's half-reaction stands just below lead's, by a hundredth of a volt.

</Answer>

<Answer>

The solution that gains ions is the more dilute one, as in part (a), so Y is more concentrated than Z.

</Answer>

<Question number={17} title="A cell that runs until something is gone" topic="9.11" points={5}>

A galvanic cell is built from two half-cells joined by a wire and a salt bridge.

- A zinc strip with a mass of 5.00 g stands in a solution of zinc nitrate.
- A cadmium strip stands in 0.100 L of 0.500 M cadmium nitrate.

The thermodynamically favored reaction of the cell is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn(s) + Cd^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Zn^2+(aq) + Cd(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Oxidation happens at the zinc strip, the anode:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

Reduction happens at the cadmium strip, the cathode:

$$
\ce{Cd^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Cd(s)}
$$

The equilibrium constant of the cell reaction is very large. Take the cell as running until one reactant is used up, and take its current as a steady 0.150 A for as long as it runs.

Use these values: the molar mass of zinc is 65.38 g/mol and of cadmium is 112.41 g/mol; one mole of electrons carries 96,485 C.

<Part label="a">

Determine which reactant is used up first, the zinc of the strip or the cadmium ions of the solution.

<Answer>

The moles of zinc in the strip:

$$
\frac{5.00}{65.38} = 0.0765\ \text{mol}
$$

The moles of cadmium ions in the solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.100)(0.500) \\
&= 0.0500\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The equation uses one zinc atom for each cadmium ion. There are fewer moles of cadmium ions, 0.0500 mol against 0.0765 mol, so **the cadmium ions are used up first**. Zinc is left over.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the total charge the cell can deliver, and the time, in hours, for which it can run at 0.150 A.

<Answer>

The charge is set by the reactant that runs out. Each cadmium ion takes 2 electrons, so going from ions to electrons the 2 multiplies:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0500)(2) \\
&= 0.100\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.100 mol of electrons. Multiply by Faraday's constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= (0.100)(96{,}485) \\
&= 9{,}650\ \text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

The product is 9,648.5 C, which is **9,650 C** to three significant figures.

Solve $I = q/t$ for the time:

$$
\begin{aligned}
t &= \frac{q}{I} \\
&= \frac{9{,}650}{0.150} \\
&= 64{,}300\ \text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

An hour is 3,600 s:

$$
\frac{64{,}300}{3{,}600} = \mathbf{17.9}\ \text{h}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the mass of zinc left on the zinc strip when the cell stops, and the mass that the cadmium strip has gained. Then calculate, each with its sign, the change in the combined mass of the two strips and the change in the combined mass of zinc and cadmium dissolved in the two solutions. Leave out the ions of the salt bridge.

<Answer>

The 0.100 mol of electrons passes both strips, and both half-reactions have 2 electrons for each atom. So 0.0500 mol of zinc leaves the zinc strip and 0.0500 mol of cadmium is deposited on the cadmium strip.

The zinc strip loses

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0500)(65.38) \\
&= 3.27\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

so the zinc left is

$$
5.00 - 3.27 = \mathbf{1.73}\ \text{g}
$$

The cadmium strip gains

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(0.0500)(112.41) \\
&= \mathbf{5.62}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

For the two strips together, the loss counts as negative and the gain as positive:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-3.27 + 5.62 \\
&= \mathbf{+2.35}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

The combined mass of the strips rises by 2.35 g.

Now the solutions. Every zinc atom that leaves the zinc strip goes into the zinc nitrate solution as an ion, so that solution gains 3.27 g of dissolved zinc. Every cadmium atom deposited came out of the cadmium nitrate solution, so that solution loses 5.62 g of dissolved cadmium:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&+3.27 - 5.62 \\
&= \mathbf{-2.35}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

The combined mass of dissolved zinc and cadmium falls by 2.35 g. The two changes add up to zero. No zinc or cadmium is made or lost in the cell: 2.35 g more of it has left the solutions for the strips than has left the strips for the solutions. The mass of the electrons that moved is far too small to show in these figures.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={18} title="Where did the charge go?" topic="9.11" points={5}>

Manganese metal is made by electrolysis of a solution of manganese(II) sulfate. At the cathode, the power supply pushes in electrons and manganese is deposited:

$$
\ce{Mn^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Mn(s)}
$$

Not every electron does this job. Some of them make hydrogen gas at the same cathode:

$$
\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2e^- -> H2(g)}
$$

Take these two half-reactions as the only ones at the cathode.

A cell runs at a steady 265 A for 2.50 h. Its cathode is weighed before and after the run and has gained 445 g, all of it manganese. The run's numbers are made up for this question.

Use these values: the molar mass of manganese is 54.94 g/mol, and one mole of electrons carries 96,485 C.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the moles of electrons that passed, and the mass of manganese that would have been deposited if every one of them had reduced a manganese ion.

<Answer>

The time in seconds is 2.50 h × 3,600 s/h = 9,000 s.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= It \\
&= (265)(9{,}000) \\
&= 2.385 \times 10^{6}\ \text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by Faraday's constant:

$$
\frac{2.385 \times 10^{6}}{96{,}485} = \mathbf{24.72}\ \text{mol}
$$

That is 24.72 mol of electrons. If all of them made manganese, 2 for each atom, the moles of manganese would be

$$
\frac{24.72}{2} = 12.36\ \text{mol}
$$

and the mass would be

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(12.36)(54.94) \\
&= \mathbf{679}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the percent of the charge that deposited manganese.

<Answer>

Run the chain backward from the mass the cathode gained. The moles of manganese:

$$
\frac{445}{54.94} = 8.100\ \text{mol}
$$

Each atom took 2 electrons:

$$
(8.100)(2) = 16.20\ \text{mol}
$$

So 16.20 mol of the 24.72 mol of electrons deposited manganese:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{16.20}{24.72} \times 100 \\
&= \mathbf{65.5}\ \text{percent}
\end{aligned}
$$

The masses give the same share: 445 g is 65.5 percent of 679 g.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the moles of electrons that did not deposit manganese, and the moles of hydrogen gas they made.

<Answer>

The electrons that did not deposit manganese:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&24.72 - 16.20 \\
&= \mathbf{8.52}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

By the stem, these made hydrogen. The hydrogen half-reaction takes 2 electrons for each $\ce{H2}$, so there are half as many moles of hydrogen as of these electrons:

$$
\frac{8.52}{2} = \mathbf{4.26}\ \text{mol}
$$

The run made **4.26 mol** of hydrogen gas alongside 8.100 mol of manganese. The chain still holds for each half-reaction. What changed is that the electrons had to be shared out between the two before either chain could start.

</Answer>

</Part>
