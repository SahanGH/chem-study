<Part label="c">

A student converts 300 K to 27 °C and uses 27 in the ratio for part (a). Explain the effect of the error.

<Answer>

Ratios require absolute temperature. With 27 for the starting temperature and 600 K left as it is, the ratio is

$$
600/27 \approx 22
$$

so the student predicts a 22-fold volume change, about 130 L, instead of doubling to 12 L. Celsius has an arbitrary zero, so a ratio of Celsius temperatures means nothing.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={5} topic="3.5">

$\ce{He}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ are held at the same temperature.

<Part label="a">

Compare their average kinetic energies.

<Answer>

Equal. Average kinetic energy depends only on the kelvin temperature.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Compare their average speeds, with reasoning.

<Answer>

Helium atoms move much faster. At the same temperature both gases have the same average kinetic energy. A helium atom is much lighter: the molar mass of $\ce{CO2}$, 44.01 g/mol, is about 11 times that of $\ce{He}$, 4.003 g/mol. So a helium atom must move faster to carry the same energy. Speed goes as the square root of the mass ratio:

$$
\sqrt{11} \approx 3.3
$$

So a helium atom moves about 3.3 times as fast.

**Beyond the exam:** the root-mean-square speed is

$$
u_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}
$$

Here $R$ is $8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$ and $M$ is the molar mass in kg/mol.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

On paper, sketch both Maxwell–Boltzmann curves on one set of axes and label them.

<Answer>

The helium curve is broad and flat, with its peak far to the right. The $\ce{CO2}$ curve is narrow and tall, with its peak to the left. The two areas are equal.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="3.6">

Under which conditions does a real gas behave most ideally, and why? Name both failed assumptions in your answer.

<Answer>

High temperature and low pressure. The two assumptions that fail for a real gas are that the particles have no volume of their own and that they do not attract each other. At low pressure the particles are far apart, so their own volume is negligible compared with the container and their attractions are weak. At high temperature they move fast enough that attractions barely affect their collisions.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={7} topic="3.6">

For $\ce{CH4}$ at 200 K, a plot of $PV/nRT$ against $P$ first dips below 1, then rises above 1.

<Part label="a">

Cause of the dip: <Blank />

<Answer>

Attractions between the molecules soften their collisions with the walls, so the pressure is lower than $PV = nRT$ predicts.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Cause of the rise: <Blank />

<Answer>

At very high pressure the molecules' own volume becomes a real share of the container.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Would $\ce{He}$ show a larger or smaller dip? Why?

<Answer>

Smaller. A helium atom has only 2 electrons (methane has 10), so its London dispersion forces are extremely weak, and attractions barely reduce the measured pressure.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>
