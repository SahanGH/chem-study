Work each question on paper before you open its answer. One idea runs through all of them: every ranking needs its reason.

Name the force **and** the structural feature that produces it. "It has stronger intermolecular forces" only restates the question. Say *which* force, and *why* it is present.

<Question number={2} topic="3.1">

$\ce{HF}$ boils at 19.5 °C, while $\ce{HCl}$ boils at $-85$ °C, even though $\ce{HCl}$ has the greater molar mass. Explain.

<Answer>

$\ce{HF}$ hydrogen bonds: H is attached directly to F, one of only three elements that permit it. That attraction far exceeds the dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces in $\ce{HCl}$, more than compensating for the larger electron count of $\ce{HCl}$.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={3} topic="3.1">

These are the measured boiling points: $\ce{BI3}$, 209.5 °C (London dispersion forces only); $\ce{PCl3}$, 76.1 °C (London dispersion and dipole–dipole forces); $\ce{NH3}$, $-33.3$ °C (London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding).

<Part label="a">

This ordering appears to contradict "hydrogen bonding is strongest." Resolve the apparent contradiction.

<Answer>

Force type alone does not set the ranking. Magnitude does. $\ce{BI3}$ has an enormous, highly polarizable electron cloud, so its London dispersion forces add up to more than all the attractions between $\ce{NH3}$ molecules, hydrogen bonds included. $\ce{NH3}$ is also very small, with few electrons.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

State the rule this illustrates in one sentence.

<Answer>

Compare sizes before naming forces: when sizes differ greatly, the London dispersion forces of a large molecule can outweigh the hydrogen bonds of a small one.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

<Question number={5} topic="3.2">

A student claims that melting ice breaks the O–H bonds in water. Evaluate this claim and give the correct account.

<Answer>

False. Melting breaks some of the hydrogen bonds between molecules and rearranges the rest, so the molecules can move past one another. The covalent O–H bonds within each molecule are untouched. Breaking O–H bonds would decompose water into different substances and requires far more energy.

</Answer>

</Question>

<Question number={6} topic="3.2, 3.3">

Rank these by vapor pressure at 25 °C, highest first, with a one-line reason: $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CH3OH}$ and $\ce{C5H12}$ (pentane).

<Answer>

$$
\ce{C5H12} > \ce{CH3OH} > \ce{H2O}
$$

Pentane has only London dispersion forces. Even with 42 electrons to the 18 of methanol, they hold its molecules less strongly than the hydrogen bonds of methanol do. Methanol hydrogen-bonds through one O–H group per molecule. Water hydrogen-bonds through two O–H groups per molecule. Stronger intermolecular forces hold more molecules in the liquid, which lowers the vapor pressure.

</Answer>

</Question>
