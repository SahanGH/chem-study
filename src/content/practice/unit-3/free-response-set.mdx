This set covers most of Unit 3: topics 3.1 to 3.6 and 3.8 to 3.13. Unit 3 is worth 18 to 22 percent of the AP exam, more than any other unit.

There are 5 questions worth 32 points. Give yourself about 50 minutes. You may use a calculator. Write out each answer in full before you open the worked answer and the scoring rubric.

Use $R$ = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K). The vapor pressure of water at 25 °C is 23.8 torr.

Reading phase diagrams, colligative properties, and calculations of molality or percent by mass are not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for them. Concentration here always means molarity.

## Questions

<Question number={1} title="Gas stoichiometry, collection over water, and kinetic molecular theory" topic="3.4, 3.5" points={10}>

Zinc reacts with excess hydrochloric acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A 3.27 g sample of zinc reacts completely and the hydrogen is collected over water at 25 °C. The total pressure in the collection tube is 745 torr.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the moles of hydrogen produced.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\ce{Zn}) &= \frac{3.27}{65.38} \\
&= 0.0500\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The ratio is $1:1$, so:

$$
n(\ce{H2}) = \mathbf{0.0500}\ \text{mol}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Determine the partial pressure of the dry hydrogen, in atm.

<Answer>

The collected gas is saturated with water vapor, which contributes its own partial pressure:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\ce{H2}) &= 745 - 23.8 \\
&= 721\ \text{torr} \\
&= \frac{721.2}{760} \\
&= \mathbf{0.949}\ \text{atm}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the volume the hydrogen occupies.

<Answer>

$$
T = 25 + 273 = 298\ \text{K}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{nRT}{P} \\
&= \frac{(0.05002)(0.08206)(298)}{0.94895} \\
&= \mathbf{1.29}\ \text{L}
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the *total* pressure instead of the dry partial pressure gives 1.25 L. The water vapor must be subtracted first.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

The tube is warmed. Explain, using kinetic molecular theory, what happens to the pressure exerted by the hydrogen if the volume is held fixed.

<Answer>

The pressure **rises**. Raising the temperature raises the average kinetic energy, and therefore the average speed, of the molecules.

They then strike the walls both *more often* and *with greater force*, and pressure is exactly the force of those collisions per unit area. (The vapor pressure of the water also rises, so the total pressure climbs faster still.)

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="e">

Helium is added to the tube at constant volume and temperature. State the effect on the partial pressure of the hydrogen and on the total pressure.

<Answer>

The partial pressure of hydrogen is **unchanged**. At fixed volume and temperature, it depends only on the moles of hydrogen, which have not changed. The **total** pressure rises by the partial pressure of the added helium.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the moles of zinc; 1 point for the $1:1$ ratio.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for subtracting the water vapor pressure; 1 point for converting to 0.949 atm.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={3}>1 point for the kelvin temperature; 1 point for using the *dry* pressure; 1 point for 1.29 L. Using 745 torr loses only the dry-pressure point: 1.25 L with the kelvin temperature earns 2.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for greater average kinetic energy and speed; 1 point for collisions that are more frequent and more forceful, so the pressure rises.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="e" points={1}>Hydrogen's partial pressure unchanged, total pressure up.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Forces, phases and dissolving" topic="3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.10" points={10}>

<Part label="a">

Rank $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{CH3OH}$ and $\ce{CH3F}$ by boiling point, lowest first, and name the strongest intermolecular force in each.

<Answer>

$$
\ce{CH4} < \ce{CH3F} < \ce{CH3OH}
$$

$\ce{CH4}$: London dispersion forces only. It is nonpolar.

$\ce{CH3F}$: dipole–dipole forces (polar, but all its hydrogens are on carbon, so no hydrogen bonding).

$\ce{CH3OH}$: hydrogen bonding, with an H bonded directly to oxygen.

$\ce{CH3F}$ and $\ce{CH3OH}$ have 18 electrons each, so their London dispersion forces are about equal and the stronger kind of force decides. $\ce{CH4}$, with 10 electrons and no dipole, attracts its neighbors least.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why $\ce{CH3F}$ boils lower than $\ce{CH3OH}$ even though fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen.

<Answer>

Hydrogen bonding requires a hydrogen bonded *directly* to N, O or F. In $\ce{CH3F}$ the fluorine is bonded to carbon and all three hydrogens sit on carbon, so no hydrogen bond can form however electronegative the fluorine is. Its molecules attract one another only by dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces.

In $\ce{CH3OH}$ the hydrogen on the oxygen is left nearly bare of electron density and can approach a lone pair on a neighboring oxygen very closely, giving a much stronger attraction.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Methanol and water mix in all proportions; methane is almost insoluble in water. Account for the difference.

<Answer>

The O–H group of methanol hydrogen-bonds with water, so the solute–solvent attractions formed are comparable in strength to the water–water attractions they replace. Methane is nonpolar and can attract water only weakly, through London dispersion and dipole–induced dipole forces, which cannot make up for breaking the hydrogen bonds of water, so it stays essentially undissolved.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

During boiling, energy is supplied but the temperature does not rise. Explain.

<Answer>

The energy goes into overcoming the intermolecular attractions and separating the molecules, which raises the potential energy of the system. Temperature measures average *kinetic* energy, which is unchanged, so it stays constant until the last of the liquid has vaporized. The energy taken in or given out in a phase change is the subject of topic 6.5.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for the correct order; 2 points for all three forces correctly named (1 point for two).</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={3}>1 point for saying that hydrogen bonding requires H bonded directly to N, O or F; 1 point for saying that all the hydrogens in $\ce{CH3F}$ are on carbon; 1 point for saying that $\ce{CH3OH}$ does hydrogen bond. Arguing from electronegativity alone predicts the wrong order and earns **0**.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for saying that methanol hydrogen-bonds with water; 1 point for saying that methane can attract water only weakly (London dispersion and dipole–induced dipole forces).</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={2}>1 point for energy overcoming intermolecular forces (or raising potential energy); 1 point for temperature tracking kinetic energy, which is unchanged.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Deviation from ideal behavior" topic="3.6" points={4}>

<Part label="a">

State the two assumptions of the ideal gas model that fail in a real gas.

<Answer>

The model assumes that the molecules have **negligible volume** of their own, and that there are **no attractive forces** between them.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

At high pressure the measured pressure of many real gases falls below the ideal prediction, but at very high pressure it rises above it. Explain both.

<Answer>

**Below, at high pressure:** the molecules are close enough to attract one another. A molecule heading for the wall is pulled back by those behind it, so it strikes with less force and the measured pressure is lower than ideal.

**Above, at very high pressure:** the molecules are packed so closely that their own volume is no longer negligible against the container. The free space available is smaller than $V$, so the particles strike the walls more often than the ideal law assumes, and the pressure runs higher.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for each assumption.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for attractions reducing the force of collisions with the walls; 1 point for molecular volume dominating at very high pressure.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Spectroscopy and the Beer–Lambert law" topic="3.11, 3.12, 3.13" points={4}>

A colored solution has an absorbance of 0.435 in a 1.00 cm cell. The molar absorptivity of the absorbing species is $1.25 \times 10^{3}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the concentration.

<Answer>

$$
A = \varepsilon b c
$$

So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{0.435}{(1.25 \times 10^{3})(1.00)} \\
&= \mathbf{3.48 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student dilutes the sample to half its concentration. State the new absorbance and explain.

<Answer>

The new absorbance is 0.218. Absorbance is directly proportional to concentration at fixed path length and wavelength, so halving the concentration halves the absorbance.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why a molecule absorbs only certain wavelengths of light.

<Answer>

It can have only certain energies (its energy levels are quantized). A photon is absorbed only when its energy, $E = hc/\lambda$, matches exactly the gap between two allowed levels, so only certain wavelengths are taken up and the rest pass through.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the Beer–Lambert relationship; 1 point for $3.48 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}$.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>The value 0.218 (0.2175 also earns it), with the proportionality.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>Quantized levels, with the photon's energy matching the gap.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="Representations of solutions and separation" topic="3.8, 3.9" points={4}>

<Part label="a">

A student draws a particulate diagram of aqueous $\ce{K2SO4}$ showing intact $\ce{K2SO4}$ units. Identify the error and describe the correct drawing.

<Answer>

$\ce{K2SO4}$ is a soluble ionic compound and dissociates completely in water. The drawing should show *separate* ions, each surrounded by water molecules (oxygen toward $\ce{K+}$, hydrogens toward $\ce{SO4^2-}$), **two** $\ce{K+}$ for every one $\ce{SO4^2-}$, with no intact $\ce{K2SO4}$ units. The sulfate should be drawn as one polyatomic unit, since the $\ce{S-O}$ bonds do not break.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Name a technique that separates a dissolved salt from its solvent, and state the property it exploits.

<Answer>

**Distillation** (or simple evaporation). It exploits the large difference in **volatility**: the solvent has a much higher vapor pressure, so it escapes as vapor. The salt stays behind, because its ions are held by strong attractions.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for identifying that it dissociates into ions; 1 point for the correct $2:1$ ion ratio with sulfate kept intact.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for a valid technique; 1 point for the property it exploits.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>
