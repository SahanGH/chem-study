## Part E: Solutions and how to picture them

This part is about molarity: making a solution, diluting one, and finding the concentration of each ion. Then it asks you to describe solutions particle by particle, and to find what a wrong drawing gets wrong.

<Question number={1} title="A standard solution of potassium iodate" topic="3.7" points={5}>

Potassium iodate, $\ce{KIO3}$ (molar mass 214.00 g/mol), is pure and stable enough to be weighed out exactly, so chemists use it to make solutions whose concentration is known to four significant figures. It is also the compound added to table salt to iodize it in many countries.

A chemist needs 250.0 mL of 0.02000 M $\ce{KIO3}$.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the mass of $\ce{KIO3}$ the chemist needs.

<Answer>

First the moles of solute. The volume goes in liters: 250.0 mL is 0.2500 L.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= M \times V \\
&= (0.02000\ \text{mol/L}) \\
&\quad \times (0.2500\ \text{L}) \\
&= 0.005000\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then the mass, from the molar mass.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= 0.005000\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad \times 214.00\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= \mathbf{1.070}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

Four significant figures, from 0.02000 M and 250.0 mL.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Describe how the chemist makes the solution, naming the glassware.

<Answer>

Weigh **1.070 g** of $\ce{KIO3}$ on a balance. Put it in a **250-mL volumetric flask** and add water to fill the flask only partway, about halfway. Swirl until all of the solid has dissolved. Then add water until the **bottom of the meniscus sits on the line** etched on the neck, looking at it at eye level. Stopper the flask and turn it over several times to mix.

Dissolving the solid in a beaker first also works, as long as the solution and every rinse of the beaker go into the flask before it is filled to the line.

The order matters because molarity counts liters of solution, not liters of water. The line marks 250.0 mL of the finished solution. Stirring the solid into 250.0 mL of water would give a volume that is not exactly 250.0 mL.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

While filling the flask, the chemist adds too much water, and the bottom of the meniscus ends up above the line. State whether the concentration of the solution is higher than, lower than, or equal to 0.02000 M, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

**Lower.** The flask still holds 0.005000 mol of $\ce{KIO3}$, but now in more than 0.2500 L of solution. The same moles in a larger volume give a smaller molarity.

Pouring off liquid down to the line does not fix it, because that liquid carries dissolved $\ce{KIO3}$ away with it. The chemist has to make the solution again.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the moles, 0.005000 mol; 1 point for 1.070 g (1.07 g also earns it). Leaving the volume in milliliters gives 1070 g and earns 1 point, for multiplying moles by the molar mass.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for dissolving the solid in a 250-mL volumetric flask filled only partway with water (dissolving it in a beaker and rinsing all of it into the flask also earns it); 1 point for then adding water until the bottom of the meniscus is on the line, and mixing. Adding the solid to 250.0 mL of measured water earns 0 points for this part.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for lower, because the same moles of solute are in a larger volume of solution. "Lower" with no reason earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Ammonium sulfate for a nutrient solution" topic="3.7" points={5}>

Ammonium sulfate, $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$ (molar mass 132.14 g/mol), is a fertilizer that gives plants both nitrogen and sulfur. A greenhouse technician dissolves 8.26 g of it in water in a 500.0-mL volumetric flask and fills the flask to the line.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the molarity of the ammonium sulfate in the solution.

<Answer>

Moles of ammonium sulfate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{8.26\ \text{g}}{132.14\ \text{g/mol}} \\
&= 0.0625\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by the volume of solution in liters. 500.0 mL is 0.5000 L.

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \frac{0.0625\ \text{mol}}{0.5000\ \text{L}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.125}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Three significant figures, from the 8.26 g.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the concentration of ammonium ion, the concentration of sulfate ion, and the total concentration of ions in the solution.

<Answer>

Ammonium sulfate is ionic, and its ions separate when it dissolves. Ammonium and sulfate are both polyatomic ions, so each stays in one piece:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{(NH4)2SO4(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2NH4+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each formula unit gives two $\ce{NH4+}$ and one $\ce{SO4^2-}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{NH4+}] &= 2 \times 0.125\ \text{M} \\
&= \mathbf{0.250}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
[\ce{SO4^2-}] = \mathbf{0.125}\ \text{M}
$$

Each formula unit gives three ions in all, so the total is three times the ammonium sulfate concentration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.250\ \text{M} + 0.125\ \text{M} \\
&= \mathbf{0.375}\ \text{M of ions}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the volume of this solution, in milliliters, that contains 0.0100 mol of ammonium ion.

<Answer>

Use the ammonium ion concentration from part (b), not the ammonium sulfate concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{n}{M} \\
&= \frac{0.0100\ \text{mol}}{0.250\ \text{mol/L}} \\
&= 0.0400\ \text{L} \\
&= \mathbf{40.0}\ \text{mL}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the moles, 0.0625 mol; 1 point for 0.125 M. Dividing by 500.0 instead of 0.5000 L earns only the moles point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for 0.250 M ammonium ion and 0.125 M sulfate ion; 1 point for 0.375 M of ions in all. A total from ammonium or sulfate split into atoms earns 0 points for the total. Ion concentrations carried correctly from a wrong (a) earn both points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for 40.0 mL, from the ammonium ion concentration. Using 0.125 M gives 80.0 mL and earns 0 points. A volume carried correctly from a wrong (b) earns the point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Two dilutions in a row" topic="3.7" points={4}>

A laboratory that measures chromium in water samples makes very dilute solutions of known concentration from a stock of 0.360 M chromium(III) nitrate, $\ce{Cr(NO3)3}$.

First, a technician delivers 25.00 mL of the stock with a volumetric pipet into a 250.0-mL volumetric flask and fills it to the line. Call this solution B.

Then 20.00 mL of solution B goes by volumetric pipet into another 250.0-mL volumetric flask, which is filled to the line. Call this solution C.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the concentration of chromium(III) nitrate in solution B.

<Answer>

Dilution keeps the moles of solute and changes only the volume, so $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_2 &= \frac{M_1V_1}{V_2} \\
&= \frac{(0.360\ \text{M})(25.00\ \text{mL})}{250.0\ \text{mL}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.0360}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Working through moles gives the same: 0.02500 L of 0.360 M holds 0.00900 mol, and 0.00900 mol in 0.2500 L is 0.0360 M.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the concentration of chromium(III) nitrate in solution C.

<Answer>

Solution B is now the solution being diluted.

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \frac{(0.0360\ \text{M})(20.00\ \text{mL})}{250.0\ \text{mL}} \\
&= \mathbf{2.88 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step diluted the solution 10 times and the second 12.5 times, so the stock is 125 times as concentrated as C.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the concentration of $\ce{Cr^3+}$ and the concentration of $\ce{NO3-}$ in solution C.

<Answer>

Each formula unit of chromium(III) nitrate gives one $\ce{Cr^3+}$ and three $\ce{NO3-}$. Nitrate is a polyatomic ion and stays whole.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr(NO3)3(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cr^3+(aq) + 3NO3-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So:

$$
[\ce{Cr^3+}] = \mathbf{2.88 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{NO3-}] &= 3 \times [\ce{Cr^3+}] \\
&= 3 \times (2.88 \times 10^{-3}) \\
&= \mathbf{8.64 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for 0.0360 M, by the dilution equation or through moles (either earns it).</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for $2.88 \times 10^{-3}$ M, diluting solution B (not the stock). Diluting the stock with 20.00 mL and 250.0 mL gives 0.0288 M and earns 0 points. Diluting a wrong value from (a) correctly earns the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for the chromium(III) ion at the same concentration as the salt; 1 point for the nitrate ion at three times it, $8.64 \times 10^{-3}$ M. Ion concentrations carried correctly from a wrong (b) earn both points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Two fertilizers, drawn particle by particle" topic="3.8" points={5}>

Urea, $\ce{CO(NH2)2}$, and ammonium chloride, $\ce{NH4Cl}$, are both nitrogen fertilizers, and both dissolve well in water. Urea is a molecular compound: its carbon atom is double-bonded to an oxygen atom and single-bonded to two $\ce{NH2}$ groups. Ammonium chloride is ionic.

A student makes a 0.60 M urea solution and a 0.30 M ammonium chloride solution and draws, on paper, the same small volume of each. The urea drawing shows 6 urea molecules.

<Part label="a">

Describe what the urea drawing must show: the solute particles, and the attraction between urea and water that the drawing marks with dashed lines, naming the atoms at each end of a dashed line.

<Answer>

**Six whole $\ce{CO(NH2)2}$ molecules**, each with all eight of its atoms joined (one C, one O, two N and four H), apart from one another among the water molecules. No ions and no loose atoms: dissolving moves the urea molecules apart and leaves every covalent bond inside them in place.

The dashed lines are **hydrogen bonds** between urea and water:

- from a hydrogen atom on one of urea's nitrogen atoms to the oxygen atom of a water molecule;
- from a hydrogen atom of a water molecule to urea's oxygen atom.

Urea and water also attract each other by dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces. The dashed lines mark the hydrogen bonds.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Describe what the ammonium chloride drawing must show: the solute particles, and how the water molecules next to each kind of ion are turned.

<Answer>

The urea drawing shows 6 molecules for 0.60 M, so in the same volume 0.30 M ammonium chloride is 3 formula units. Each comes apart into its ions: **3 $\ce{NH4+}$ ions and 3 $\ce{Cl-}$ ions**, all apart from one another. No $\ce{NH4Cl}$ pairs.

Each $\ce{NH4+}$ is drawn whole, a nitrogen atom with four hydrogen atoms, because the bonds inside a polyatomic ion are covalent and dissolving does not break them. The charges add to zero:

$$
3(+1) + 3(-1) = 0
$$

Water is polar. Next to each $\ce{NH4+}$, the water molecules turn their **partly negative oxygen atoms toward the ion**. Next to each $\ce{Cl-}$, each water molecule points **one of its partly positive hydrogen atoms at the ion**. These attractions are ion–dipole forces.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A classmate says the ammonium chloride drawing should show half as many solute particles as the urea drawing, because its concentration is half as large. Explain why the classmate is wrong, and state the number of solute particles in each drawing.

<Answer>

The two concentrations count what was dissolved: urea molecules in one solution, formula units of ammonium chloride in the other. Urea stays as whole molecules, so its drawing shows **6 particles**. Each formula unit of ammonium chloride comes apart into two ions, so its drawing shows **6 particles** too, 3 $\ce{NH4+}$ and 3 $\ce{Cl-}$: **the same number**.

In concentrations: the 0.30 M ammonium chloride solution is 0.30 M in $\ce{NH4+}$ and 0.30 M in $\ce{Cl-}$, 0.60 M of ions in all, equal to the 0.60 M of urea molecules.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for six whole urea molecules, not broken into atoms or ions; 1 point for hydrogen bonds between urea and water with the atoms at the ends named correctly (urea's N–H hydrogen to water's oxygen, or water's hydrogen to urea's oxygen; a water hydrogen to urea's nitrogen also earns it).</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for 3 ammonium ions and 3 chloride ions, apart, each ammonium ion whole; 1 point for water's oxygen toward the ammonium ions and a water hydrogen toward the chloride ions. Ammonium chloride pairs, or separate N and H atoms, earn 0 points for the first point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for 6 urea molecules and 6 ions from the ammonium chloride, the same number, because each formula unit gives two ions while urea stays whole.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="What a drawing of barium chloride solution gets wrong" topic="3.8" points={4}>

A solution of barium chloride, $\ce{BaCl2}$, is used to test water samples for sulfate ions. A student draws a small volume of barium chloride solution, leaving out most of the water. The drawing shows:

- three $\ce{Ba^2+}$ ions, apart from one another;
- three $\ce{Cl2}$ molecules, each drawn as two chlorine atoms joined together;
- four water molecules around each $\ce{Ba^2+}$ ion, each with one of its hydrogen atoms pointed at the ion.

<Part label="a">

Identify two errors in the drawing, and explain why each is wrong.

<Answer>

**The chlorine is drawn as $\ce{Cl2}$ molecules.** When barium chloride dissolves, its ions separate, and the chlorine is there only as separate chloride ions, $\ce{Cl-}$, each with a charge of −1. Dissolving does not join chloride ions into molecules. $\ce{Cl2}$ is a different substance, the element chlorine, and it carries no charge.

**The water is turned the wrong way around the $\ce{Ba^2+}$ ions.** A barium ion is positive, so each water molecule next to it turns its partly negative oxygen atom toward it, not a partly positive hydrogen atom.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Use the charges of the particles in the drawing to show that the drawing cannot be correct. Then describe the corrected drawing, including how a water molecule next to a chloride ion is turned.

<Answer>

The three barium ions carry a total charge of +6, and the $\ce{Cl2}$ molecules carry none:

$$
3(+2) + 3(0) = +6
$$

A solution has no overall charge, so this drawing cannot be right.

The corrected drawing shows **three $\ce{Ba^2+}$ ions and six separate $\ce{Cl-}$ ions**, two chloride ions for each barium ion, as the formula says. Now the charges add to zero:

$$
3(+2) + 6(-1) = 0
$$

Water molecules next to each $\ce{Ba^2+}$ turn their oxygen atoms toward it. Each water molecule next to a $\ce{Cl-}$ points **one of its hydrogen atoms at the chloride ion**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for each error explained: chlorine drawn as Cl₂ molecules instead of separate chloride ions; water turned with a hydrogen toward the barium ion instead of the oxygen. Naming the charge imbalance in place of the Cl₂ molecules also earns the first point. The $\ce{Cl2}$ molecules and the charge imbalance are one error and earn one point between them.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for a total charge of +6, not zero, so the drawing cannot be right; 1 point for three barium ions and six separate chloride ions, with a water hydrogen toward each chloride ion.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="A metal sulfate known only from its drawing" topic="3.7, 3.8" points={4}>

A student is given a solution of the sulfate of a metal, M, but is not told its formula. A correct drawing of a small volume of the solution, with the water left out, shows 4 ions of the metal and 6 sulfate ions, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, all apart from one another, with each sulfate ion drawn as one particle.

<Part label="a">

Determine the charge on the metal ion and the formula of the salt. Justify your answer using the charges in the drawing.

<Answer>

The six sulfate ions carry a total charge of

$$
6(-2) = -12
$$

A solution has no overall charge, so the four metal ions together carry +12, and each one carries **+3**: the metal ion is $\ce{M^3+}$.

The drawing has the ions in the ratio 4 to 6, which is 2 to 3, so the formula is **$\ce{M2(SO4)3}$**. Its charges add to zero:

$$
2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

The solution was made by dissolving 0.0300 mol of the salt in water to make 250.0 mL of solution. Calculate the concentration of the metal ion and the concentration of the sulfate ion.

<Answer>

The concentration of the salt, with 250.0 mL as 0.2500 L:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \frac{0.0300\ \text{mol}}{0.2500\ \text{L}} \\
&= 0.120\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each formula unit gives two $\ce{M^3+}$ and three $\ce{SO4^2-}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{M2(SO4)3(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2M^3+(aq) + 3SO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{M^3+}] &= 2 \times 0.120\ \text{M} \\
&= \mathbf{0.240}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{SO4^2-}] &= 3 \times 0.120\ \text{M} \\
&= \mathbf{0.360}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two concentrations are in the ratio 2 to 3, the same as the counts in the drawing.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for a charge of +3, from the charges adding to zero; 1 point for the formula with two metal ions for every three sulfate ions. A formula with no reasoning from the charges earns only the formula point. The formula is written in its lowest whole-number ratio, $\ce{M2(SO4)3}$; $\ce{M4(SO4)6}$ earns 0 points for the formula.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for 0.120 M of the salt; 1 point for 0.240 M metal ion and 0.360 M sulfate ion. A formula from part (a), even a wrong one, used correctly earns the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={7} title="Cleaning a dry-cleaning solvent" topic="3.9" points={4}>

Dry cleaning washes clothes in tetrachloroethylene, $\ce{C2Cl4}$, instead of water. Tetrachloroethylene is a nonpolar liquid that boils at 121.3 °C. After a wash, the used solvent carries two things away from the clothes: lint, which is loose fibers that do not dissolve, and oily soil, which has dissolved in the solvent. The oily soil is made of large molecules that are mostly long hydrocarbon chains, with several times as many electrons as a tetrachloroethylene molecule. The machine cleans the used solvent in two steps so that it can be used again.

<Part label="a">

In the first step, the used solvent is pumped through a filter. State what the filter removes and what passes through it. Explain at the level of particles, and state the property of the substances that this step uses.

<Answer>

The filter **removes the lint**. The **dissolved oily soil passes through** with the tetrachloroethylene.

A filter holds back only particles too big for its pores. Lint fibers are large, undissolved pieces of solid, so they are caught. The oily soil is dissolved: it is spread through the solvent as single molecules, far smaller than any pore, so it goes straight through with the solvent.

The property this step uses is **particle size**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

In the second step, the filtered solvent is distilled. Identify what collects as the distillate and what stays behind in the boiling flask. Explain in terms of intermolecular forces, and state the property of the substances that this step uses.

<Answer>

The **distillate is tetrachloroethylene**. The **oily soil stays behind** in the flask.

Compare sizes first. A tetrachloroethylene molecule has 80 electrons. An oil molecule is mostly long hydrocarbon chains and has several times as many electrons, with long chains that lie against their neighbors over a large area. So the London dispersion forces between oil molecules are far stronger than the attractions between tetrachloroethylene molecules.

Molecules held by weaker attractions escape into the vapor more easily. At the temperature of the boiling flask, tetrachloroethylene has a much higher vapor pressure than the oil, and the oil has so little vapor pressure that practically none of it comes over. The tetrachloroethylene vapor is led to the condenser and turned back into liquid, and the oil is left in the flask.

The property this step uses is the **difference in the strength of the intermolecular forces**, which shows up as a difference in vapor pressure and boiling point.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the lint removed and the dissolved oily soil passing through; 1 point for the reason, that the dissolved oil is spread out as single molecules far smaller than the pores while the lint is too big for them (particle size). Saying that the filter also catches the oil earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for tetrachloroethylene as the distillate and the oil left in the flask, with a difference in boiling point, vapor pressure (volatility) or the strength of the intermolecular forces as the property; 1 point for the explanation, that the much larger oil molecules, with far more electrons, have stronger London dispersion forces and so a much lower vapor pressure. Saying that the oil distills over first earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="Dyes in a black marker" topic="3.9" points={6}>

The ink of a black marker is a mixture of dyes. A student puts a spot of the ink on the start line of a silica plate (plate 1) and runs the plate with a less polar solvent. The solvent front ends 6.0 cm above the start line, and the ink separates into three spots. Her lab partner records only the $R_f$ value of each spot.

Plate 1, the ink:

| Spot | $R_f$ |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.85 |
| 2 | 0.50 |
| 3 | 0.40 |

On a second silica plate (plate 2), run with the same solvent, the student spots three dyes that might be in the ink, labeled J, K and L. Every distance on plate 2 is measured from the start line, to the center of a spot or to the solvent front. (The dyes are made up.)

Plate 2, the three known dyes:

| Measured to | Distance (cm) |
|---|---|
| solvent front | 4.0 |
| dye J | 3.4 |
| dye K | 2.4 |
| dye L | 2.0 |

<Part label="a">

Calculate how far each of the three ink spots moved from the start line on plate 1.

<Answer>

$R_f$ is the distance the spot moved divided by the distance the solvent moved, so the spot's distance is its $R_f$ times the solvent's distance, 6.0 cm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Spot 1: } d &= 0.85 \times 6.0\ \text{cm} \\
&= \mathbf{5.1}\ \text{cm} \\[0.4em]
\text{Spot 2: } d &= 0.50 \times 6.0\ \text{cm} \\
&= \mathbf{3.0}\ \text{cm} \\[0.4em]
\text{Spot 3: } d &= 0.40 \times 6.0\ \text{cm} \\
&= \mathbf{2.4}\ \text{cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Identify which of the dyes J, K and L are in the ink. Explain why comparing the distances the dyes moved on plate 2 with the distances you found in part (a) would lead to a wrong identification.

<Answer>

Find the $R_f$ of each known dye, dividing by plate 2's solvent front, 4.0 cm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{J: } R_f &= \frac{3.4\ \text{cm}}{4.0\ \text{cm}} = 0.85 \\[0.4em]
\text{K: } R_f &= \frac{2.4\ \text{cm}}{4.0\ \text{cm}} = 0.60 \\[0.4em]
\text{L: } R_f &= \frac{2.0\ \text{cm}}{4.0\ \text{cm}} = 0.50
\end{aligned}
$$

Both plates are silica run with the same solvent, so a dye should give about the same $R_f$ on either plate. Spot 1 matches J (0.85) and spot 2 matches L (0.50). So the ink **probably contains dyes J and L**.

**Comparing distances would wrongly match dye K with spot 3.** Both moved 2.4 cm. But the solvent moved 6.0 cm on plate 1 and only 4.0 cm on plate 2, so a distance on one plate cannot be compared with a distance on the other. Dye K moved 2.4 cm out of 4.0 cm, an $R_f$ of 0.60. Spot 3 moved 2.4 cm out of 6.0 cm, an $R_f$ of 0.40. Their $R_f$ values differ, so they are different dyes. Spot 3 is a dye that is not among the three known dyes.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Take one ink spot and the known dye whose $R_f$ value matches it in part (b). State what the match shows and what it does not show. Then describe a further test, using chromatography, that would make the identification more certain.

<Answer>

Take spot 1 and dye J, which both have an $R_f$ of 0.85. (Spot 2 and dye L work the same way.)

**What it shows:** the dye in spot 1 is held by the silica and carried by the solvent in the same balance as dye J. That is what dye J would do, so spot 1 is **probably** dye J.

**What it does not show:** that the two are the same substance. Two different dyes can happen to give the same $R_f$ with one solvent on one kind of plate. A match is evidence, not proof.

**A further test:** run the ink and dye J side by side on one plate with a **different solvent**, for example a more polar one. A different solvent changes the balance of attractions, and two different dyes are unlikely to change in exactly the same way. If spot 1's dye and dye J still move the same distance, the identification is much more certain. If they now separate, they are different dyes.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

To collect the dyes separately, the student washes the ink down a column packed with silica, using the same less polar solvent. <Answer>

**First the dye of spot 1, then the dye of spot 2, and last the dye of spot 3, which is the most polar.**

A column works the same way as the plate. Silica is polar: its surface is covered with O–H groups. A polar dye is held to those groups by dipole–dipole forces, and often by hydrogen bonds as well, while the less polar solvent attracts it only weakly. The more strongly a dye is held by the silica, the more slowly it travels. On plate 1, spot 3 moved the smallest fraction of the solvent's distance ($R_f$ = 0.40), so it is held most strongly: it is the most polar and leaves the column last. Spot 1 ($R_f$ = 0.85) is held least strongly and leaves first.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={10} title="Glycerol and two solvents" topic="3.10" points={4}>

Glycerol, $\ce{C3H5(OH)3}$, is a colorless liquid that pours slowly at room temperature. Its molecule is a chain of three carbon atoms with one O–H group on each carbon. Dodecane, $\ce{C12H26}$, is a nonpolar hydrocarbon found in kerosene.

<Part label="a">

Predict whether glycerol mixes with water. Explain by naming the attractions that must be broken and the attractions that form.

<Answer>

Glycerol **mixes with water**. In fact the two are miscible: they mix in any proportion.

**Broken:** the attractions between glycerol molecules, which are hydrogen bonds from their O–H groups, along with dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces; and some of the attractions between water molecules, which are also hydrogen bonds, with dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces.

**Formed:** attractions between glycerol and water molecules. Each of glycerol's three O–H groups can hydrogen-bond with water molecules, and dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces act between them as well.

The hydrogen bonds that form are about as strong as the ones that are broken, so the trade costs little and the two liquids mix.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Predict whether glycerol mixes with dodecane. Explain by naming the attractions that must be broken and every intermolecular force that would act between a glycerol molecule and a dodecane molecule.

<Answer>

Glycerol **does not mix with dodecane**.

**Broken:** the hydrogen bonds between glycerol molecules, with their dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces, and the London dispersion forces between dodecane molecules.

**Formed:** a glycerol molecule and a dodecane molecule would attract each other by **London dispersion forces and dipole–induced dipole forces**: glycerol's dipoles distort the electron clouds of the nonpolar dodecane. Dodecane has no hydrogen bonded to N, O or F and no lone pairs, so it cannot hydrogen-bond with glycerol.

These attractions are much weaker than the hydrogen bonds glycerol would have to give up. So the glycerol molecules stay together, and the two liquids form separate layers.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Answer>

<Part label="b">

The orange color of compound X ends up in one layer only. Predict which layer, and explain by naming the attractions that are broken and the attractions that form.

<Answer>

The orange color is in the **top layer, the cyclohexane**.

**Not in water:** to take in X, water would have to give up hydrogen bonds between its molecules, and X could attract water molecules only by London dispersion and dipole–induced dipole forces. Those are much weaker than the hydrogen bonds lost, so the water molecules stay together and X stays out.

</Answer>

The attractions that would form are real but much weaker than the hydrogen bonds lost, so the water molecules stay together and the cyclohexane stays in a layer of its own.

</Answer>

<Question number={12} title="Water vapor, ozone and a lamp that disinfects air" topic="3.11, 3.12" points={5}>

Molecules in three settings absorb three kinds of light:

- Weather instruments called radiometers measure the water vapor in the air from light of frequency 22.235 GHz, which water molecules absorb and give out.
- Ozone, $\ce{O3}$, in the atmosphere absorbs light of wavelength 9.6 µm given off by Earth's warm surface.
- A lamp filled with krypton and chlorine gives out light of wavelength 222 nm. It is used to disinfect the air of a room, because the proteins and genetic material of bacteria and viruses absorb the light and are damaged by it.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the wavelength of the 22.235 GHz light, in centimeters.

<Answer>

Giga means $10^{9}$, so the frequency is $2.2235 \times 10^{10}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Rearrange $c = \lambda\nu$ for the wavelength:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= \frac{c}{\nu} \\
&= \frac{2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}{2.2235 \times 10^{10}\ \text{s}^{-1}} \\
&= 1.348 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then convert to centimeters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1.348 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{m} \times \frac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}} \\
&= \mathbf{1.348}\ \text{cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

The answer has four significant figures, because $c$ has four and the frequency has five.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

For each of the three kinds of light, identify the region of the spectrum and the change it causes in a molecule that absorbs it.

<Answer>

**The water vapor's light, 1.348 cm.** That is $1.348 \times 10^{-2}$ m, between 1 m and 1 mm, so it is **microwave** light. A water molecule that absorbs it **rotates faster**: it moves up to a higher rotational level.

**Ozone's light, 9.6 µm.** That is 9600 nm, between 1 mm and 700 nm, so it is **infrared** light. An ozone molecule that absorbs it **vibrates more**: its bonds stretch or bend with more energy, at a higher vibrational level.

**The lamp's light, 222 nm.** That lies between 400 nm and 10 nm, so it is **ultraviolet** light. A molecule that absorbs it has **an electron moved to a higher energy level**, an electronic transition.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain why a 9.6 µm photon cannot move one of ozone's electrons to a higher energy level.

<Answer>

A molecule absorbs a photon only when the photon's energy matches exactly the gap between the level the molecule is on and a higher one.

Electronic levels are the farthest apart of the three kinds, much farther apart than vibrational levels. A 9.6 µm photon carries **far too little energy to match the gap up to a higher electronic level**, so it cannot move an electron. Its energy matches a gap between vibrational levels instead, so it makes ozone's bonds vibrate more.

</Answer>

</Part>

<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for saying that the 9.6 µm photon's energy is far smaller than the gap to a higher electronic level (a photon is absorbed only when its energy matches a gap). "Infrared changes vibrations" with no reason in terms of energy earns 0.</RubricLine>

<Question number={14} title="Two disc lasers" topic="3.11, 3.12" points={4}>

<Question number={15} title="One jump and three photons" topic="3.11, 3.12" points={3}>

\end{aligned}

<Part label="d">

An atom of J at level 1 is struck by a photon carrying $3.80 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Will the atom absorb the photon? Explain.

<Answer>

**No.** An atom absorbs a photon only when the photon's energy matches the gap from the level it is on to a higher one. From level 1, those gaps are $3.00 \times 10^{-19}$ J (to level 2) and $6.80 \times 10^{-19}$ J (to level 3). No level lies $3.80 \times 10^{-19}$ J above level 1.

That energy does match the gap from level 2 to level 3, but this atom is at level 1, not level 2. An atom already at level 2 could absorb it.

</Answer>

</Part>

<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for choosing the smallest gap, level 2 to level 1, $3.00 \times 10^{-19}$ J; 1 point for converting the chosen gap to a wavelength in nanometers with $\lambda = hc/E$: 662 nm for the smallest gap. Using the level 3 to level 2 gap gives 523 nm and earns only the second point.</RubricLine>

## Part H: The Beer–Lambert law

Five questions on $A = \varepsilon b c$: finding each of its quantities from the others, reading a table of standards, choosing the wavelength, and working out what a mistake in the lab does to the result. In this part $c$ is the concentration, not the speed of light, and 1 µM is $10^{-6}$ M.

<Question number={17} title="Measuring a protein's concentration" topic="3.13" points={5}>

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is a protein from cow blood that laboratories use as a standard. Its molar mass is about 66 400 g/mol. At 280 nm its molar absorptivity is $4.38 \times 10^{4}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$.

A biochemist dissolves some BSA in water. She blanks the spectrophotometer with water, then measures the BSA solution at 280 nm in a 1.00 cm cuvette. Its absorbance is 0.512. The solution is colorless.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the molar concentration of BSA in the solution.

<Answer>

Rearrange $A = \varepsilon b c$ for $c$, with $b$ in cm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{A}{\varepsilon b} \\
&= \frac{0.512}{(4.38 \times 10^{4})(1.00)} \\
&= \mathbf{1.17 \times 10^{-5}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Express the concentration in milligrams of BSA per milliliter of solution.

<Answer>

Multiply the moles per liter by the molar mass to get grams per liter:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(1.169 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L}) \\
&\quad \times (66\,400\ \text{g/mol}) \\
&= 0.776\ \text{g/L}
\end{aligned}
$$

A gram per liter is the same as a milligram per milliliter, since both the mass and the volume are divided by 1000. So the solution holds **0.776 mg/mL**. Using the rounded $1.17 \times 10^{-5}$ M gives 0.777 mg/mL.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain how a colorless solution can have an absorbance of 0.512, and why the measurement is made at 280 nm rather than at a wavelength of visible light.

<Answer>

A wavelength of 280 nm is in the **ultraviolet**, which the eye cannot see. BSA absorbs ultraviolet light at 280 nm, but it absorbs almost no visible light. A solution looks the color of the light it lets through, and this one lets every color of visible light through, so it looks colorless.

At a visible wavelength, the molar absorptivity of BSA is close to zero. The absorbance would then be close to zero whatever the concentration, so it could not be used to tell one concentration from another. At 280 nm, $\varepsilon$ is large, and the absorbance rises clearly as the concentration rises.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for dividing the absorbance by the molar absorptivity times the path length, with the values substituted; 1 point for $1.17 \times 10^{-5}$ M. Multiplying the absorbance by $\varepsilon$ earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for 0.776 or 0.777 mg/mL, from the molarity times the molar mass; 0.776 g/L earns the point when it is stated to equal 0.776 mg/mL.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for saying that 280 nm is ultraviolet and that BSA absorbs almost no visible light, so it lets all visible light through and looks colorless; 1 point for saying that at a visible wavelength $\varepsilon$, and so the absorbance, is close to zero whatever the concentration, while at 280 nm the absorbance changes with concentration. "A colorless solution absorbs no light" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={18} title="Iron in a supplement tablet" topic="3.13" points={6}>

The label on an iron supplement tablet says it contains 65 mg of iron. To check it, a student dissolves one tablet in acid and makes the solution up to 500.0 mL in a volumetric flask. She pipets 1.00 mL of this solution into a 100.0 mL volumetric flask, adds reagents that turn every iron ion into an orange-red complex ion with one iron in each, and fills the flask to the mark with water.

She makes five standards of the same complex in the same way. After blanking the spectrophotometer, she measures each standard and then her solution at 508 nm, where the complex absorbs most strongly, all in the same 1.00 cm cuvette. The molar mass of iron is 55.85 g/mol.

| Iron concentration (µM) | Absorbance |
|---|---|
| 10.0 | 0.111 |
| 15.0 | 0.167 |
| 20.0 | 0.222 |
| 25.0 | 0.248 |
| 30.0 | 0.332 |

Her solution made from the tablet reads 0.258.

<Part label="a">

One of the five standards was made up wrongly. Identify it from the data, and state whether it holds more or less of the complex than its label says. Justify your answer.

<Answer>

With the cuvette and the wavelength fixed, absorbance is proportional to concentration. So for every correctly made standard, the absorbance divided by the concentration comes out the same: it is $\varepsilon b$.

| Standard (µM) | $A/c$ ($\mu\text{M}^{-1}$) |
|---|---|
| 10.0 | 0.0111 |
| 15.0 | 0.0111 |
| 20.0 | 0.0111 |
| 25.0 | 0.00992 |
| 30.0 | 0.0111 |

Four standards agree. The **25.0 µM standard** does not: it was made up wrongly.

A correct 25.0 µM standard would read about 0.28. This one reads only 0.248, so it holds **less** of the complex than its label says:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{0.248}{0.0111\ \mu\text{M}^{-1}} \\
&= 22.3\ \mu\text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Leave out that standard. Determine the slope of the calibration line, with its unit, and then the concentration of iron in the solution she measured, in µM.

<Answer>

Each of the four correct standards gave $0.0111\ \mu\text{M}^{-1}$ in part (a), and a best-fit line through the origin and those four points has the same slope:

$$
\text{slope} = \mathbf{0.0111}\ \mu\text{M}^{-1}
$$

The concentration is the absorbance divided by the slope:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{0.258}{0.0111\ \mu\text{M}^{-1}} \\
&= \mathbf{23.2}\ \mu\text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Calculate the mass of iron in the tablet, in milligrams, and state whether it agrees with the label.

<Answer>

The measured solution was made by diluting 1.00 mL to 100.0 mL, a factor of 100. So the 500.0 mL flask held 100 times the concentration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= 100 \times 23.24\ \mu\text{M} \\
&= 2324\ \mu\text{M} \\
&= 2.324 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

That flask held all the iron from the tablet:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= (2.324 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}) \\
&\quad \times (0.5000\ \text{L}) \\
&= 1.162 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= (1.162 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}) \\
&\quad \times (55.85\ \text{g/mol}) \\
&= 0.0649\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

The tablet holds **64.9 mg** of iron, which **agrees with the label's 65 mg**. Carrying the rounded 23.2 µM through gives 64.8 mg.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain why she diluted the tablet's solution a hundredfold before measuring it, instead of measuring the solution in the 500.0 mL flask.

<Answer>

The solution in the 500.0 mL flask is about 2320 µM in iron. If the line held that far, its absorbance would be about 26, far above the highest standard's 0.332.

The line has only been measured for readings up to 0.332, so it cannot be trusted for a reading far outside that range. Diluting by 100 brings the reading down to 0.258, inside the range of the standards.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for identifying the 25.0 µM standard, with evidence from the data: its absorbance divided by its concentration (about 0.0099) differs from the other four (0.0111), or it lies off the straight line through the origin and the other four points; 1 point for less complex than its label, because it reads lower than a correct 25.0 µM standard would. "More" earns 0 for the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for 23.0 to 23.5 µM, the absorbance divided by a slope of 0.0110 to $0.0112\ \mu\text{M}^{-1}$ with its unit, from a best-fit line through the origin and the four correct standards, from the origin and any one correct standard, or from any two correct standards. A best-fit slope that keeps the 25.0 µM standard (about $0.0108\ \mu\text{M}^{-1}$) gives about 24.0 µM and earns 0 points, and so does multiplying by the slope.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for multiplying by 100 for the dilution, or for finding the moles in the 100.0 mL flask and scaling them by 500.0 mL / 1.00 mL; 1 point for the moles in 0.5000 L times 55.85 g/mol, carried to a mass in mg and compared with the label (64 to 66 mg, which agrees). Leaving out the dilution gives 0.649 mg; with the mass worked out correctly from that and compared with the label, it earns the second point only.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for saying that the undiluted solution would read far above the highest standard (about 26 against 0.332), outside the range the line was measured over. Saying the absorbance would be too high to measure reliably, because almost no light gets through, also earns the point.</RubricLine>

<Question number={19} title="Following an enzyme with ultraviolet light" topic="3.13" points={5}>

NADH is a molecule that many enzymes use. It absorbs ultraviolet light at 340 nm, with a molar absorptivity of $6.22 \times 10^{3}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$. When an enzyme uses up NADH, each NADH becomes one $\ce{NAD+}$, which does not absorb at 340 nm. Nothing else in the mixture described below absorbs at 340 nm.

A student mixes an enzyme, NADH and the enzyme's other reactant in a 1.00 cm cuvette, 3.00 mL in all, and reads the absorbance at 340 nm. The first reading is 0.746. Some minutes later, it has fallen to 0.497.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the concentration of NADH at the first reading.

<Answer>

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{A}{\varepsilon b} \\
&= \frac{0.746}{(6.22 \times 10^{3})(1.00)} \\
&= \mathbf{1.20 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the amount of NADH, in moles, used up in the cuvette between the two readings.

<Answer>

Only NADH absorbs at 340 nm, so the fall in absorbance comes from the NADH used up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta A &= 0.746 - 0.497 \\
&= 0.249
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta c &= \frac{0.249}{(6.22 \times 10^{3})(1.00)} \\
&= 4.00 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply by the volume in liters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= (4.00 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}) \\
&\quad \times (3.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{L}) \\
&= \mathbf{1.20 \times 10^{-7}}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finding both concentrations first, $1.20 \times 10^{-4}$ M and $7.99 \times 10^{-5}$ M, and subtracting gives the same result.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A classmate suggests following the reaction at a wavelength where NADH and $\ce{NAD+}$ both absorb. Explain why the absorbance at such a wavelength could not be turned into the NADH concentration using the molar absorptivity of NADH alone.

<Answer>

The instrument counts all the light absorbed by the dissolved particles in the beam. At such a wavelength, the $\ce{NAD+}$ particles absorb too, so the reading includes their share as well as the NADH's.

As the enzyme works, NADH disappears and $\ce{NAD+}$ builds up, so the $\ce{NAD+}$ share grows. The absorbance is then not proportional to the NADH concentration alone, and dividing it by $\varepsilon b$ for NADH would not give the NADH concentration. At 340 nm only NADH absorbs, so the reading measures NADH alone.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

A second batch of the mixture is made without the enzyme, so its NADH concentration stays at the value from part (a). It is put into a well of a plate reader, where the light passes straight up through the liquid, so the path length is the depth of the liquid. After blanking the reader with a well of water, the well reads 0.418 at 340 nm. Calculate the depth of the liquid.

<Answer>

Rearrange $A = \varepsilon b c$ for $b$. First the product of $\varepsilon$ and $c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon c &= (6.22 \times 10^{3}) \\
&\quad \times (1.199 \times 10^{-4}) \\
&= 0.7458\ \text{cm}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= \frac{A}{\varepsilon c} \\
&= \frac{0.418}{0.7458\ \text{cm}^{-1}} \\
&= \mathbf{0.560}\ \text{cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

Or compare with the 1.00 cm cuvette, since $\varepsilon$ and $c$ are the same and $A$ is proportional to $b$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= \frac{0.418}{0.746} \times 1.00\ \text{cm} \\
&= 0.560\ \text{cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the concentration of NADH used up, $4.00 \times 10^{-5}$ M, from the fall in absorbance or from the difference of the two concentrations (either earns the point); 1 point for $1.20 \times 10^{-7}$ mol, using 3.00 mL as 0.00300 L. Multiplying by 3.00 instead of 0.00300 earns the first point only.</RubricLine>

<RubricLine part="d" points={1}>1 point for 0.560 cm, from the Beer–Lambert law with the concentration from part (a) or from the ratio of the two absorbances (either route earns the point).</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={20} title="Choosing a wavelength for a blue dye" topic="3.13" points={5}>

A company colors its window cleaner with a blue dye. The dye and its values are made up for this question. Before making a calibration line, a chemist records the absorption spectrum of one solution of the dye in a 1.00 cm cuvette:

| Wavelength (nm) | Absorbance |
|---|---|
| 450 | 0.021 |
| 505 | 0.052 |
| 535 | 0.197 |
| 565 | 0.455 |
| 580 | 0.621 |
| 590 | 0.648 |
| 600 | 0.619 |
| 625 | 0.398 |
| 655 | 0.194 |
| 695 | 0.037 |

At 590 nm the molar absorptivity of the dye is $3.60 \times 10^{4}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$.

<Part label="a">

State which wavelength in the table the chemist should use for the calibration line, and justify your choice.

<Answer>

**590 nm**, the wavelength of maximum absorbance in the table.

The slope of the calibration line is $\varepsilon b$. With the same cuvette, the line is steepest where $\varepsilon$ is largest, and for one solution that is where its absorbance is highest. On the steepest line a small change in concentration gives the largest change in absorbance, so the measurement is the most sensitive.

The dye looks blue because it lets blue light through: at 450 nm it absorbs very little. Blue light is therefore the worst choice here, not the best.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Calculate the concentration of the dye in this solution. Then calculate the molar absorptivity of the dye at 655 nm.

<Answer>

At 590 nm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{A}{\varepsilon b} \\
&= \frac{0.648}{(3.60 \times 10^{4})(1.00)} \\
&= \mathbf{1.80 \times 10^{-5}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

At 655 nm the solution and the cuvette are the same, so:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon &= \frac{A}{bc} \\
&= \frac{0.194}{(1.00)(1.80 \times 10^{-5})} \\
&= \mathbf{1.08 \times 10^{4}}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Or, since $b$ and $c$ are unchanged, $\varepsilon$ is proportional to $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon &= 3.60 \times 10^{4} \times \frac{0.194}{0.648} \\
&= 1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The wavelength setting of the instrument can be off by a few nanometers. Use the table to explain why such an error changes the reading less at 590 nm than it would at 565 nm.

<Answer>

At 590 nm the spectrum is at its top, where it is nearly flat. Ten nanometers to either side, at 580 nm and 600 nm, the readings are 0.621 and 0.619, within 0.029 of the peak's 0.648.

At 565 nm the spectrum is climbing steeply. The absorbance rises by 0.258 from 535 nm to 565 nm, and by 0.166 from 565 nm to 580 nm. Per nanometer, that is about three to four times the change near 590 nm.

So a setting a few nanometers off changes the reading only a little at 590 nm, but changes it noticeably at 565 nm.

</Answer>
