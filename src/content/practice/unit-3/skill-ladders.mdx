There are fourteen ladders here, one for each skill. Each ladder is a worked example, then four questions of the same kind: same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four before you check any of them.

**Unit 3 is worth 18 to 22 percent of the AP exam, more than any other unit**, roughly one question in five. Do not try to do all fourteen ladders in one sitting. Work a few at a time.

**Two habits that decide this unit.** Temperature in any gas calculation is **always kelvin**. And when you explain why one substance boils higher, melts higher or dissolves better than another, name the **intermolecular force** and say why it is stronger. Never just assert that it is.

**One scope note.** Reading phase diagrams, colligative properties, and calculations of molality or percent by mass are not on the AP exam, and nothing here asks for them. Concentration here always means molarity.

## Ladder 1: Identifying intermolecular forces

Three forces, which for molecules of similar size rank in increasing strength. **London dispersion forces** act in every substance. **Dipole–dipole** adds for polar molecules. **Hydrogen bonding** requires H bonded *directly* to N, O or F. A fourth, **ion–dipole**, appears when an ion is dissolved.

<Worked title="Naming the strongest force present">

Identify the strongest intermolecular force in $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{CH2Cl2}$, $\ce{NH3}$ and a solution of $\ce{NaCl}$ in water.

**$\ce{CH4}$:** nonpolar, symmetric. Only **London dispersion forces**.

**$\ce{CH2Cl2}$:** polar (the shape does not cancel the $\ce{C-Cl}$ dipoles), but its hydrogens are on carbon. **Dipole–dipole**.

**$\ce{NH3}$:** hydrogen bonded directly to nitrogen. **Hydrogen bonding**.

**$\ce{NaCl}$(aq):** ions surrounded by polar water molecules. **Ion–dipole**, the strongest of the set.

**The trap:** calling any molecule containing hydrogen a hydrogen-bonder. The hydrogen must be attached to N, O or F. Carbon does not count, however many hydrogens there are.

</Worked>

## Ladder 2: Ranking boiling points

Boiling means separating molecules completely, so the boiling point tracks the strength of the intermolecular forces. Compare sizes (electron count) first. For molecules of similar size, the force type decides; a much larger molecule can out-boil a smaller one that has a stronger kind of force.

<Worked title="Two comparisons, two different reasons">

Rank $\ce{Ar}$ ($-186$ °C), $\ce{HCl}$ ($-85$ °C) and $\ce{H2O}$ ($+100$ °C), then explain why $\ce{Br2}$ boils higher than $\ce{Cl2}$.

**The three-way ranking is by force type.** $\ce{Ar}$ and $\ce{HCl}$ both have 18 electrons, so their London dispersion forces are about equal; $\ce{HCl}$ is polar and adds dipole–dipole forces. $\ce{H2O}$ has only 10 electrons, yet it hydrogen-bonds. So, lowest to highest:

$$
\ce{Ar} < \ce{HCl} < \ce{H2O}
$$

**$\ce{Br2}$ against $\ce{Cl2}$ is a different comparison.** Both are nonpolar with dispersion only, so type cannot decide. $\ce{Br2}$ has far more electrons, so its electron cloud is more **polarizable**: it distorts more easily, giving larger instantaneous dipoles and stronger dispersion forces. Hence the higher boiling point.

**The method:** count electrons first. If the sizes are similar, the force type decides. If one molecule is much larger, its London dispersion forces can outweigh a stronger kind of force.

</Worked>

<Answer>

**$\ce{C4H10}$**. Both are nonpolar with dispersion only, so electron count decides; butane has far more electrons and a more polarizable cloud.

$\ce{H2O}$: hydrogen bonding.

**$\ce{H2O}$**. Sulfur is too large and too weakly electronegative for $\ce{H2S}$ to hydrogen bond, so its strongest force is dipole–dipole; water hydrogen bonds. $\ce{H2S}$ has more electrons (18 against 10), so its London dispersion forces are stronger, but here the hydrogen bonds of water outweigh them.

</Answer>

Which boils higher, $\ce{CH3OCH3}$ or $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$? Both are $\ce{C2H6O}$.

<Answer>

Ethanol: one of its H atoms is on O.

**Ethanol**. Same formula and the same number of electrons, so dispersion is comparable. But one of ethanol's hydrogens sits on the *oxygen* and can hydrogen-bond, while all six of the ether's hydrogens are on carbon. The whole difference is hydrogen bonding.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

$\ce{HF}$ has stronger hydrogen bonds than $\ce{H2O}$, yet water boils higher. Explain.

<Answer>

Water forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule.

Because the *number* of hydrogen bonds matters more here than the strength of any one. Water has two hydrogens and two lone pairs, so it can form up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule; $\ce{HF}$ has one hydrogen and three lone pairs, so it is limited to about two.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Ladder 3: Classifying solids

Four types, each with properties that follow from what is holding the particles together.

<Worked title="Classify and predict">

Classify $\ce{SiO2}$, $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{Cu}$ and solid $\ce{CO2}$, and give one property of each that follows.

**$\ce{SiO2}$: covalent network.** A continuous lattice of strong covalent bonds. Melting means breaking covalent bonds, so the melting point is very high and it is hard and non-conducting.

**$\ce{NaCl}$: ionic.** Strong electrostatic attractions between ions: high-melting and brittle, conducting only when molten or dissolved.

**$\ce{Cu}$: metallic.** Cations in a delocalized electron sea: conducts as a solid, and is malleable.

**Solid $\ce{CO2}$: molecular.** Only weak dispersion forces between whole $\ce{CO2}$ molecules. Very low melting point (it sublimes at $-78$ °C), and it does not conduct.

**The distinction that matters most:** in a molecular solid the covalent bonds *within* each molecule are strong, but melting only has to overcome the weak forces *between* molecules. That is why dry ice sublimes so easily despite containing strong $\ce{C=O}$ bonds.

</Worked>

<Question number={3} label="Your turn" topic="3.2">

<Part label="a">

Classify diamond and give its expected melting point behavior.

<Answer>

Covalent network, very high.

**Covalent network**. Melting requires breaking a continuous lattice of strong $\ce{C-C}$ covalent bonds, so the melting point is extremely high (above 3500 °C).

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Classify solid $\ce{I2}$: <Blank />

<Answer>

Molecular.

**Molecular**: discrete $\ce{I2}$ molecules held by dispersion forces only, so it is soft and low-melting.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A solid is hard, high-melting, and conducts only when molten. Which type?

<Answer>

Ionic.

**Ionic**. Hardness and a high melting point indicate strong attractions; conducting only when molten indicates charged particles that are fixed in the solid and mobile in the melt.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not, though both are carbon.

<Answer>

Graphite has delocalized electrons; diamond's are all in sigma bonds.

In **diamond** every carbon forms four single bonds, so all four valence electrons are localized in sigma bonds and none is free to move. In **graphite** each carbon bonds to only three others, leaving one electron per atom **delocalized** over the layer. Those mobile electrons carry current along the sheets.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 4: Phase changes and heating curves

On a heating curve, sloped sections are temperature rising (kinetic energy increasing) and flat sections are phase changes (potential energy increasing while temperature holds constant). Heating curves, and the energy taken in or given out in a phase change, are the subject of topic 6.5; this ladder previews them.

<Worked title="Reading the flat sections">

A heating curve for water runs from ice below 0 °C to steam above 100 °C. Explain the two flat regions, and why the second is longer.

**The flats are phase changes.** At 0 °C the ice melts; at 100 °C the water boils. Throughout each, energy is being supplied but the temperature does not move.

**Why the temperature holds:** the energy goes into overcoming intermolecular attractions, raising *potential* energy, not into speeding the molecules up. Temperature measures average *kinetic* energy, which is unchanged.

**Why the boiling flat is longer:** melting only loosens the lattice. Molecules leave fixed positions but stay in contact, and most hydrogen bonds survive. Vaporizing must separate them completely, overcoming essentially all the attractions. For water that is 2260 J/g against 334 J/g, nearly seven times as much.

</Worked>

<Question number={4} label="Your turn" topic="3.3">

<Part label="a">

On a heating curve, what is happening on a *sloped* section?

<Answer>

Temperature rising, kinetic energy increasing.

The substance is in a single phase and its temperature is rising: the energy supplied increases the average *kinetic* energy of the particles.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Why does temperature stay constant during melting?

<Answer>

Energy goes to potential, not kinetic.

The energy supplied is used to overcome the intermolecular attractions holding the lattice, raising the **potential** energy. Since temperature reflects average kinetic energy, and that is not changing, the temperature holds steady.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Which requires more energy per gram for water, melting or vaporizing, and why?

<Answer>

Vaporizing: it separates molecules completely.

**Vaporizing**, by a factor of nearly seven (2260 against 334 J/g). Melting only loosens the structure; vaporizing separates the molecules completely.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Steam at 100 °C causes worse burns than water at 100 °C. Explain.

<Answer>

It releases its heat of condensation as well.

Steam must first *condense* on the skin, releasing its full heat of vaporization, about 2260 J/g, before it even begins to cool. Liquid water at the same temperature delivers only the energy from cooling. The condensation step is the extra damage.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 5: The ideal gas law

$PV = nRT$, with $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K). That choice of $R$ fixes every other unit: pressure in atm, volume in liters, temperature in **kelvin**.

<Worked title="Solving for volume">

What volume does 0.250 mol of gas occupy at 2.00 atm and 300 K?

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{nRT}{P} \\
&= \frac{(0.250)(0.08206)(300)}{2.00} \\
&= \mathbf{3.08}\ \text{L}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sense check:** one mole at 1 atm and 273 K is 22.4 L. A quarter of a mole at double the pressure should be roughly:

$$
22.4/4/2 \approx 2.8\ \text{L}
$$

A slightly higher temperature nudges it up, so 3.08 L is right.

**The error that costs whole questions:** leaving the temperature in °C. At 27 °C you must use 300 K, and using 27 gives an answer wrong by a factor of eleven.

</Worked>

A rigid container is heated from 300 K to 600 K. What happens to the pressure, and why?

<Answer>

It doubles: $P \propto T$ at fixed $n$ and $V$.

The pressure **doubles**. With $n$ and $V$ fixed, $P \propto T$ in kelvin, and the temperature doubled. In kinetic terms the molecules move faster and strike the walls both more often and harder.

## Ladder 6: Gas stoichiometry

Moles are the bridge. Convert to moles, use the balanced equation, then convert the moles of gas to a volume with $PV = nRT$. Use 22.4 L/mol *only* at STP.

<Worked title="Solid to gas volume">

A 5.00 g sample of $\ce{NaHCO3}$ ($M = 84.01$ g/mol) reacts with excess hydrochloric acid, releasing $\ce{CO2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{NaHCO3 + HCl} \\
&\qquad \ce{-> NaCl + H2O + CO2}
\end{aligned}
$$

What volume does the gas occupy at 1.00 atm and 298 K?

**Moles of solid:**

$$
5.00/84.01 = 0.0595\ \text{mol}
$$

**Through the equation:** one $\ce{CO2}$ forms for each $\ce{NaHCO3}$, so:

$$
n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.0595\ \text{mol}
$$

**To a volume:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{nRT}{P} \\
&= \frac{(0.05952)(0.08206)(298)}{1.00} \\
&= \mathbf{1.46}\ \text{L}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why not just use 22.4?** Because 22.4 L/mol holds only at STP (273 K, 1 atm). At 298 K and 1.00 atm the molar volume is 24.5 L/mol, and using 22.4 would give 1.33 L, about 8 percent low.

</Worked>

<Part label="a">

What volume would that same $\ce{CO2}$ occupy at STP?

<Answer>

1.33 L

At STP the molar volume is 22.4 L/mol:

$$
0.05952 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{1.33}\ \text{L}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

0.200 mol of $\ce{O2}$ at 2.00 atm and 273 K: volume?

<Answer>

$$

\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Why is 22.4 L/mol wrong at 298 K?

<Answer>

Molar volume depends on $T$ and $P$.

Because molar volume is $RT/P$, which changes with both. At 298 K and 1 atm it is 24.5 L/mol, not 22.4.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Equal volumes of $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ at the same $T$ and $P$ contain equal what?

<Answer>

Equal moles (equal numbers of molecules).

Equal numbers of **moles**, hence equal numbers of molecules: Avogadro's principle. Note they do *not* contain equal masses, since the molar masses differ.

</Answer>

## Ladder 7: Partial pressures

Each gas in a mixture exerts the pressure it would exert alone. The total is their sum, and each one's share is set by its **mole fraction**, not by its molar mass.

<Worked title="Mole fractions to partial pressures">

A vessel holds 0.400 mol $\ce{N2}$, 0.200 mol $\ce{O2}$ and 0.400 mol $\ce{He}$ at a total pressure of 3.00 atm. Find each partial pressure.

**Total moles:**

$$
0.400 + 0.200 + 0.400 = 1.000
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\ce{N2}} &= \frac{0.400}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{1.20}\ \text{atm} \\
P_{\ce{O2}} &= \frac{0.200}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{0.600}\ \text{atm} \\
P_{\ce{He}} &= \frac{0.400}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{1.20}\ \text{atm}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Check:**

$$
1.20 + 0.600 + 1.20 = 3.00\ \checkmark
$$

**Note what did not matter:** helium is by far the lightest gas here and nitrogen is seven times heavier, yet they exert identical partial pressures because they are present in identical amounts. Pressure counts particles, not mass.

</Worked>

<Answer>

$$

<Answer>

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Adding helium to a rigid vessel: what happens to the partial pressure of the gas already there?

<Answer>

Unchanged.

**Unchanged.** Its partial pressure depends only on how many moles of *it* are present and the volume and temperature, none of which changed. Only the *total* pressure rises.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why does a heavier gas not exert a larger partial pressure at equal moles?

<Answer>

Pressure depends on particle count, not mass.

Because pressure arises from the momentum delivered by collisions with the walls, and at a given temperature all gases have the same average *kinetic energy*. A heavier molecule moves more slowly in exactly the compensating proportion, so equal numbers of particles exert equal pressure.

</Answer>

## Ladder 8: Kinetic molecular theory and effusion

At a given temperature all gases have the same average *kinetic energy*. They do not have the same average *speed*: because $\text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, the lighter molecule must move faster.

<Worked title="Energy, speed, and rate">

Helium and argon are at the same temperature. Compare their average kinetic energies and speeds, and (beyond the exam) find the ratio of their effusion rates.

**Average kinetic energy: equal.** It depends only on absolute temperature.

**Speed: helium is faster.** With $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ the same for both, the smaller mass must carry the larger speed.

**Beyond the exam:** effusion is a gas escaping through a tiny hole, and its rate goes as the inverse square root of molar mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\text{rate}_{\ce{He}}}{\text{rate}_{\ce{Ar}}} &= \sqrt{\frac{39.95}{4.003}} \\
&= \sqrt{9.98} = \mathbf{3.16}
\end{aligned}
$$

Helium effuses about three times faster.

**Watch the direction of the ratio** (also beyond the exam). The *lighter* gas is faster, so the heavier mass goes on top. Getting it upside down gives 0.32; a ratio below 1 for the lighter gas is a sure sign of that mistake.

</Worked>

## Ladder 9: Deviation from ideal behavior

The ideal model assumes molecules have no volume and no attractions. Both assumptions fail, and they fail in opposite directions.

<Worked title="Two regimes, two causes">

Explain why a real gas shows a lower pressure than predicted at moderately high pressure, but a higher pressure at very high pressure.

**Moderately high pressure: attractions dominate.** Molecules are close enough to attract one another. One heading for the wall is pulled back by those behind it and strikes with less force, so the measured pressure falls *below* the ideal prediction.

**Very high pressure: molecular volume dominates.** Now the molecules are packed so tightly that the space they themselves occupy is no longer negligible. The space the particles actually move in is less than $V$, so they strike the walls more often than the law assumes, and the pressure runs *above* the prediction.

**Where the model works best:** **high temperature** (kinetic energy swamps the attractions) and **low pressure** (molecules far apart, own volume negligible).

</Worked>

<Question number={9} label="Your turn" topic="3.6">

<Part label="a">

State the two conditions for most nearly ideal behavior.

<Answer>

High $T$, low $P$.

**High temperature** and **low pressure**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Which behaves more ideally at the same $T$ and $P$, $\ce{He}$ or $\ce{H2O}$ vapor? Why?

<Answer>

$\ce{He}$: weakest attractions and smallest particles.

**Helium.** It is a tiny, nonpolar atom with only very weak dispersion forces, so the no-attraction assumption nearly holds. Water vapor hydrogen bonds strongly, so it deviates far more. Its molecules are also larger than helium atoms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Which assumption fails when a gas liquefies on cooling?

<Answer>

The no-attractions assumption.

The assumption of **no intermolecular attractions**. Liquefaction happens precisely because attractions exist and become able to hold molecules together once they slow down.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="d">

Why does raising the temperature reduce the deviation caused by attractions?

<Answer>

Kinetic energy overwhelms the attraction.

At higher temperature the molecules have far more kinetic energy relative to the strength of the intermolecular attractions. A passing attraction barely alters a fast molecule's path, so the gas behaves as though no forces acted.

</Answer>

</Part>

</Question>

## Ladder 10: Molarity and dilution

Molarity is moles of solute per liter of *solution*, not of solvent. On dilution the moles of solute are unchanged; only the volume grows.

<Worked title="A preparation and a dilution">

(i) 0.0250 mol of solute is made up to 500.0 mL. Find the molarity. (ii) Then 50.0 mL of 2.00 M stock is diluted to 500.0 mL.

**(i)**

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= n/V = 0.0250/0.500 \\
&= \mathbf{0.0500}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**(ii)** Moles of solute do not change, so $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_2 &= \frac{(2.00)(50.0)}{500.0} \\
&= \mathbf{0.200}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why "of solution" matters:** to make the first solution you dissolve the solid in *less* than 500 mL and then make up to the mark. Adding solid to 500 mL of water gives a final volume above 500 mL and a concentration below target.

</Worked>

## Ladder 11: Solubility and "like dissolves like"

A solute dissolves when the solute–solvent attractions formed are comparable to the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions broken.

<Worked title="Two solutes, one solvent">

Explain why $\ce{NaCl}$ dissolves readily in water but not in hexane, and why $\ce{I2}$ does the reverse.

**$\ce{NaCl}$ in water:** water is polar and surrounds each ion with strong **ion–dipole** attractions: oxygen ends toward $\ce{Na+}$, hydrogen ends toward $\ce{Cl-}$. The energy released repays the cost of pulling the lattice apart.

**$\ce{NaCl}$ in hexane:** hexane is nonpolar and offers only weak dispersion forces, which cannot compensate for $\ce{NaCl}$'s very large lattice energy. It does not dissolve.

**$\ce{I2}$ reversed:** iodine is nonpolar. In hexane, dispersion forces between solute and solvent are comparable to those being replaced, so it dissolves. In water, dissolving would mean disrupting water's hydrogen-bond network with nothing comparable offered in return, so it barely dissolves.

</Worked>

## Ladder 13: Photons and the spectrum

$E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$. Energy is *inversely* proportional to wavelength: short wavelength means high energy.

<Worked title="Wavelength to energy per mole">

Find the energy of one photon of 400 nm light, and the energy per mole.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{hc}{\lambda} \\
&= \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(2.998 \times 10^{8})}{400 \times 10^{-9}} \\
&= \mathbf{4.97 \times 10^{-19}}\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Per mole:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(4.97 \times 10^{-19})(6.022 \times 10^{23}) \\
&= 2.99 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/mol} \\
&= \mathbf{299}\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**The unit trap:** the wavelength must be in **meters**. Leaving it in nanometers makes the answer wrong by a factor of $10^{9}$.

**Sense check:** 400 nm is violet, the high-energy end of the visible range. Red light at 700 nm should come out lower; question (a) checks that.

</Worked>

## Ladder 14: The Beer–Lambert law

$A = \varepsilon b c$: absorbance is proportional to concentration at fixed path length. That proportionality is what makes it an analytical tool: measure $A$, get $c$.

<Worked title="Absorbance to concentration">

A solution has $A = 0.620$ in a 1.00 cm cell, with $\varepsilon = 2.00 \times 10^{3}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$. Find the concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{A}{\varepsilon b} \\
&= \frac{0.620}{(2.00 \times 10^{3})(1.00)} \\
&= \mathbf{3.10 \times 10^{-4}}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why a calibration curve is used in practice:** you prepare standards of known concentration, measure each one's absorbance, and plot $A$ against $c$. The result is a straight line through the origin, and an unknown's concentration is read off it. That avoids needing $\varepsilon$ at all.

</Worked>
