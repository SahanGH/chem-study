## Part A: Intermolecular forces

Six questions on the attractions between particles. You will name every force in a pure substance and in a mixture, weigh the size of molecules against the kind of force they add, and explain what polarizability, shape and the charge of an ion do to those attractions.

<Question number={1} title="The forces in a refrigerant blend" topic="3.1" points={4}>

Many newer air conditioners run on the refrigerant R-32, which is difluoromethane, $\ce{CH2F2}$. Its carbon atom is bonded to two hydrogen atoms and two fluorine atoms, which point to the corners of a tetrahedron.

R-410A, the refrigerant in many home air conditioners of recent decades, is a blend of equal masses of $\ce{CH2F2}$ and pentafluoroethane, $\ce{CHF2CF3}$. In pentafluoroethane the single hydrogen atom is bonded to a carbon atom, and the molecule is polar.

<Part label="a">

Identify every intermolecular force between $\ce{CH2F2}$ molecules in the pure liquid. Justify each force you name other than London dispersion forces.

<Answer>

$\ce{CH2F2}$ is polar. Each C–F bond is strongly polar, with the δ− end on fluorine, while the C–H bonds are treated as nonpolar. In a tetrahedron the two C–F bond dipoles do not point in opposite directions, so they do not cancel: the molecule has a δ− side, where the fluorines are, and a δ+ side.

So two forces act between $\ce{CH2F2}$ molecules:

- **London dispersion forces**, which act between all molecules;
- **dipole–dipole forces**, because the molecules are polar.

There are no hydrogen bonds. Both hydrogens are bonded to carbon, not directly to N, O or F.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

In the blend, a molecule can be next to a molecule of the same kind or of the other kind, so there are three kinds of neighboring pair: two $\ce{CH2F2}$ molecules, two $\ce{CHF2CF3}$ molecules, or one of each. Identify the pair with the strongest London dispersion forces. Compare the number of electrons in the two kinds of molecule as part of your answer.

<Answer>

Count the electrons: carbon has 6, hydrogen 1 and fluorine 9.

$$
\ce{CH2F2}:\ 6 + 2(1) + 2(9) = 26
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{CHF2CF3}&: \\
2(6) + 1 + 5(9) &= 58
\end{aligned}
$$

The strongest London dispersion forces act between **two $\ce{CHF2CF3}$ molecules**. A $\ce{CHF2CF3}$ molecule has more than twice as many electrons as a $\ce{CH2F2}$ molecule, so its electron cloud is larger and more polarizable, and it is the larger molecule, with more surface to touch its neighbor. A pair of two such molecules has the most polarizable clouds on both sides.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify every intermolecular force between a $\ce{CH2F2}$ molecule and a $\ce{CHF2CF3}$ molecule in the blend.

<Answer>

**London dispersion forces** and **dipole–dipole forces**. London forces act between any two molecules. Both molecules are polar, so the δ+ side of one is attracted to the δ− side of the other.

There are no hydrogen bonds between them. The fluorine atoms do have lone pairs, but every hydrogen in both molecules is bonded to carbon, so no hydrogen is bonded directly to N, O or F.

</Answer>

</Part>

A list that leaves out London dispersion forces, or adds hydrogen bonding, loses the list point. Adding dipole–induced dipole forces as well does not lose the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for two $\ce{CHF2CF3}$ molecules, with the reason that $\ce{CHF2CF3}$ has more electrons (58 against 26, or a clear statement that it has far more) and so a more polarizable electron cloud. The pair named without the electron comparison earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for London dispersion and dipole–dipole forces, with no hydrogen bonding. Adding dipole–induced dipole forces as well does not lose the point; naming them instead of dipole–dipole forces earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={2} title="Carbon with four halogens" topic="3.1" points={5}>

A carbon atom can bond to four atoms of fluorine, of chlorine or of bromine, giving tetrafluoromethane, $\ce{CF4}$; carbon tetrachloride, $\ce{CCl4}$; and carbon tetrabromide, $\ce{CBr4}$. Each molecule is a tetrahedron with four identical carbon–halogen bonds.

<Part label="a">

Identify every intermolecular force between the molecules of each of the three substances, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

In each molecule the four carbon–halogen bonds are polar, but they are identical and point to the four corners of a tetrahedron, so their dipoles cancel. All three molecules are nonpolar.

So between the molecules of each substance there are **London dispersion forces only**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Rank the three substances by normal boiling point, lowest first. Explain your ranking in terms of the electrons in each molecule and polarizability.

<Answer>

Count the electrons: carbon has 6, fluorine 9, chlorine 17 and bromine 35.

| Molecule | Count | Electrons |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CF4}$ | $6 + 4(9)$ | 42 |
| $\ce{CCl4}$ | $6 + 4(17)$ | 74 |
| $\ce{CBr4}$ | $6 + 4(35)$ | 146 |

$$
\ce{CF4} < \ce{CCl4} < \ce{CBr4}
$$

From fluorine to chlorine to bromine, each halogen atom has more electrons, and its outer electrons sit farther from the nucleus, where they are held less tightly. So the electron cloud of the whole molecule is distorted more easily: it is more **polarizable**. A more polarizable cloud forms larger temporary dipoles and has larger dipoles induced in it, so the London dispersion forces between the molecules are stronger. More energy is then needed to separate the molecules, and the boiling point is higher.

The data agree: $\ce{CF4}$ boils at $-127.8$ °C, $\ce{CCl4}$ at 76.8 °C and $\ce{CBr4}$ at 189.5 °C, a spread of 317.3 °C with only one kind of force.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A polar molecule that comes close to a $\ce{CF4}$ molecule or a $\ce{CBr4}$ molecule attracts it. Name the force, other than London dispersion forces, by which the polar molecule attracts each of them. Explain why the attraction is stronger for $\ce{CBr4}$ than for $\ce{CF4}$ when the same polar molecule is involved.

<Answer>

The force is the **dipole–induced dipole force**. The permanent dipole of the polar molecule distorts the electron cloud of its nonpolar neighbor, giving the neighbor a dipole of its own, lined up so that the two attract.

The attraction is stronger when the nonpolar molecule is more polarizable. $\ce{CBr4}$, with 146 electrons and loosely held outer electrons on its bromine atoms, is far more polarizable than $\ce{CF4}$, with 42. So the same permanent dipole induces a **larger dipole** in $\ce{CBr4}$, and the attraction is stronger.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for London dispersion forces only, with the reason that the four identical bond dipoles cancel in the tetrahedron, so each molecule is nonpolar.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the order $\ce{CF4}$, then $\ce{CCl4}$, then $\ce{CBr4}$; 1 point for linking more electrons (or a larger electron cloud held less tightly) to greater polarizability and stronger London dispersion forces. An explanation by molar mass alone, or by stronger carbon–halogen bonds, earns only the order point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for naming dipole–induced dipole forces; 1 point for saying that $\ce{CBr4}$ is more polarizable (more electrons), so a larger dipole is induced in it. Calling the force dipole–dipole earns 0 points for the name, but a correct polarizability reason still earns its point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={3} title="Vinegar's acid, its isomer and a metal chloride" topic="3.1" points={6}>

Acetic acid is the acid in vinegar. Methyl formate is a volatile liquid with the same formula, $\ce{C2H4O2}$, but its atoms are joined differently:

- in **methyl formate**, $\ce{HCOOCH3}$, a carbon is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to one hydrogen and to a second oxygen, which carries a $\ce{CH3}$ group;
- in **acetic acid**, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, a carbon is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to a $\ce{CH3}$ group and to a second oxygen, which carries a hydrogen.

Titanium tetrachloride, $\ce{TiCl4}$, is a colorless molecular liquid, made on a large scale on the way to titanium metal and to the white pigment titanium dioxide. Its molecule is a tetrahedron with four identical Ti–Cl bonds.

| Substance | Normal boiling point |
|---|---|
| methyl formate | 31.7 °C |
| acetic acid | 117.9 °C |
| $\ce{TiCl4}$ | 136 °C |

<Part label="a">

Determine the number of electrons in one molecule of each substance, and identify every intermolecular force between the molecules of each substance.

<Answer>

Carbon has 6 electrons, hydrogen 1, oxygen 8, chlorine 17 and titanium 22.

| Substance | Atoms | Electrons |
|---|---|---|
| methyl formate | 2 C, 4 H, 2 O | 32 |
| acetic acid | 2 C, 4 H, 2 O | 32 |
| $\ce{TiCl4}$ | 1 Ti, 4 Cl | 90 |

**Methyl formate: London dispersion forces and dipole–dipole forces.** Its C=O and C–O bonds are polar, and their dipoles do not cancel, so the molecule is polar. Every hydrogen is bonded to carbon, so although its oxygen atoms have lone pairs, its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond with each other.

**Acetic acid: London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds.** It is polar for the same reason. It also has a hydrogen bonded directly to oxygen, which is attracted to a lone pair on an oxygen atom of a neighboring molecule.

**$\ce{TiCl4}$: London dispersion forces only.** Its four identical Ti–Cl bond dipoles point to the corners of a tetrahedron and cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Acetic acid boils 86.2 °C higher than methyl formate. Explain why the electron count cannot settle this comparison, and what does.

<Answer>

The two are isomers, so both have **32 electrons** and their London dispersion forces are about equal. The electron count points to neither one.

The kind of force decides. Methyl formate adds only dipole–dipole forces to its London forces. Acetic acid adds **hydrogen bonds** as well, the strongest attraction between neutral molecules of similar size. More energy is needed to separate acetic acid molecules, so acetic acid boils higher.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

For $\ce{TiCl4}$ and acetic acid, the electron count and the kinds of force point in opposite directions. State which substance each one points to, and explain why the measured boiling points, not these two rules, decide which substance boils higher.

<Answer>

**The electron count points to $\ce{TiCl4}$.** It has 90 electrons to acetic acid's 32, so its electron cloud is far more polarizable and its London dispersion forces are stronger.

**The kinds of force point to acetic acid.** It forms hydrogen bonds and dipole–dipole forces, while $\ce{TiCl4}$ has London dispersion forces only.

Each rule says which way its factor pushes the boiling point, but not by how much. Nothing in the two rules says how many extra electrons it takes to make up for hydrogen bonds, so when they disagree, only the data can say which wins. Here the data say $\ce{TiCl4}$, at 136 °C, boils higher than acetic acid, at 117.9 °C: in this pair, the larger London forces win. In another pair the hydrogen bonds could win, so the result does not become a new rule.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={3}>1 point for the three counts (32, 32 and 90); 2 points for all three lists of forces complete and correct, 1 point for two of the three. A list for methyl formate that includes hydrogen bonds, or any list that leaves out London dispersion forces, is not correct.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for saying that the two have the same number of electrons, so London forces are about equal, and that acetic acid adds hydrogen bonds, which methyl formate cannot form.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for the electron count pointing to $\ce{TiCl4}$ (90 against 32, or a clear statement that it has far more electrons) and the kinds of force pointing to acetic acid (hydrogen bonds); 1 point for explaining that the rules give the direction of each effect but not its size, so the measured boiling points decide. An answer that applies a rule such as "hydrogen bonds always win" or "more electrons always win" earns 0 points for the second point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={4} title="Hydrazine, alone and mixed with water" topic="3.1" points={4}>

Hydrazine, $\ce{N2H4}$, is a liquid fuel for the small thrusters that steer satellites and spacecraft. In a hydrazine molecule the two nitrogen atoms are bonded to each other, each nitrogen also carries two hydrogen atoms and one lone pair, and the molecule is polar.

Hydrazine and water mix in all proportions, and hydrazine is often sold mixed with water as hydrazine hydrate.

<Part label="a">

Identify every intermolecular force between hydrazine molecules in the pure liquid.

<Answer>

**London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds.** London forces act between all molecules. The molecule is polar, so dipole–dipole forces act. Each hydrogen is bonded directly to nitrogen, and it is attracted to the lone pair on a nitrogen atom of a neighboring molecule: a hydrogen bond.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student says: "In the mixture, a hydrogen of a water molecule can hydrogen-bond to a hydrazine nitrogen. But a hydrogen of a hydrazine molecule cannot hydrogen-bond to a water molecule, because nitrogen is less electronegative than oxygen." Describe each kind of hydrogen bond that can form between a hydrazine molecule and a water molecule, and explain whether the student is right.

<Answer>

Two kinds of hydrogen bond form between the two molecules.

**From water to hydrazine:** a hydrogen bonded to water's oxygen is attracted to the lone pair on a hydrazine nitrogen. The student has this one right.

**From hydrazine to water:** a hydrogen bonded to a hydrazine nitrogen is attracted to a lone pair on water's oxygen.

The student is **wrong** about the second kind. A hydrogen bond needs a hydrogen bonded directly to N, O or F, and a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of the other molecule. An N–H hydrogen meets the first condition, just as it does in pure hydrazine, and water's oxygen has two lone pairs to meet the second. Nitrogen being less electronegative than oxygen makes an N–H hydrogen less strongly δ+ than an O–H hydrogen, but it does not rule out the hydrogen bond.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify every intermolecular force between a hydrazine molecule and a water molecule in the mixture.

<Answer>

**London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds.** London forces act between any two molecules; both molecules are polar, so dipole–dipole forces act; and the hydrogen bonds run both ways, as part (b) describes.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for the complete list: London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds. Adding dipole–induced dipole forces as well does not lose the point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for a water O–H hydrogen attracted to the lone pair on a hydrazine nitrogen; 1 point for a hydrazine N–H hydrogen attracted to a lone pair on water's oxygen, with the reason that a hydrogen bonded directly to N counts, so the student is wrong. Agreeing with the student earns at most the first point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for all three: London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonds. Adding dipole–induced dipole forces as well does not lose the point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={5} title="One molecule changed in two steps" topic="3.1" points={4}>

2-Methylpropane (isobutane), $\ce{(CH3)3CH}$, is the refrigerant in many household refrigerators. Its central carbon atom carries one hydrogen atom and three $\ce{CH3}$ groups, so the molecule is compact. Follow two changes to it:

- **Step 1.** Put a chlorine atom in place of the hydrogen on the central carbon. This gives 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, $\ce{(CH3)3CCl}$, still compact, almost a ball, and polar.
- **Step 2.** Rearrange the same atoms so that the four carbons form a straight chain with the chlorine at one end. This gives 1-chlorobutane, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Cl}$, also polar, with a dipole about the same size as that of 2-chloro-2-methylpropane.

| Substance | Normal boiling point |
|---|---|
| 2-methylpropane | $-11.7$ °C |
| 2-chloro-2-methylpropane | 50.9 °C |
| 1-chlorobutane | 78.4 °C |

<Part label="a">

Step 1 raises the boiling point by 62.6 °C. Compare the number of electrons in the two molecules, and identify each change in the intermolecular forces that contributes to the rise.

<Answer>

Count the electrons: carbon has 6, hydrogen 1 and chlorine 17.

$$
\ce{(CH3)3CH}:\ 4(6) + 10(1) = 34
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ce{(CH3)3CCl}&: \\
4(6) + 9(1) + 17 &= 50
\end{aligned}
$$

Two things change, and both raise the boiling point:

- **More electrons.** Swapping a hydrogen (1 electron) for a chlorine (17) takes the molecule from 34 to 50 electrons. The larger cloud is more polarizable, so the **London dispersion forces are stronger**.
- **A dipole.** 2-Methylpropane has only C–C and C–H bonds and is nonpolar. The polar C–Cl bond makes 2-chloro-2-methylpropane polar, so **dipole–dipole forces** now act on top of the London forces.

The data do not say how much of the 62.6 °C comes from each change.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Step 2 raises the boiling point by another 27.5 °C, although the atoms are the same. Explain the rise. Compare the electrons and the dipoles of the two molecules as part of your answer.

<Answer>

The two molecules are isomers, so both have **50 electrons**, and their dipoles are about the same size. Neither the electron count nor the dipoles can explain the rise.

What changes is the **shape**. A straight-chain 1-chlorobutane molecule can lie alongside its neighbors and touch them over much of its surface. The compact, ball-like 2-chloro-2-methylpropane molecules touch only at a few points. More contact makes the London dispersion forces between 1-chlorobutane molecules **stronger**, so more energy is needed to separate them, and the boiling point is higher.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the electron comparison (34 against 50, or a clear statement that the chlorine adds electrons) with stronger London dispersion forces; 1 point for the new dipole, which adds dipole–dipole forces. An answer that gives only one of the two changes earns 1 point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for the same number of electrons (50) and dipoles of about the same size, so neither explains the rise; 1 point for the straight chain touching its neighbors over a larger area, giving stronger London dispersion forces. Saying that 1-chlorobutane is more polar earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={6} title="Lithium bromide in a building's chiller" topic="3.1" points={4}>

Some large buildings are cooled by absorption chillers. These use a concentrated solution of lithium bromide, $\ce{LiBr}$, in water: the solution soaks up water vapor, and water serves as the refrigerant. In the solution, lithium bromide is present as separate $\ce{Li+}$ and $\ce{Br-}$ ions, each surrounded by water molecules.

<Part label="a">

Describe how the water molecules next to a $\ce{Li+}$ ion are turned, and how the water molecules next to a $\ce{Br-}$ ion are turned. Explain each in terms of the partial charges on a water molecule.

<Answer>

A water molecule is polar: its oxygen is δ− and each hydrogen is δ+.

**Around $\ce{Li+}$**, each water molecule turns its **oxygen** toward the ion, with its hydrogens pointing away. The δ− oxygen is attracted to the positive ion.

**Around $\ce{Br-}$**, each water molecule turns **one hydrogen** toward the ion. The δ+ hydrogen is attracted to the negative ion.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Name the force between each ion and the water molecules around it. Explain why this kind of force is generally stronger than a dipole–dipole force.

<Answer>

It is an **ion–dipole force**. An ion carries a full charge, while each end of a polar molecule carries only a partial charge. By Coulomb's law, the full charge attracts the oppositely charged end of a water molecule more strongly than a partial charge on another polar molecule would.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Identify every intermolecular force between two water molecules in the solution.

<Answer>

**Hydrogen bonds, dipole–dipole forces and London dispersion forces.** A hydrogen bonded to the oxygen of one water molecule is attracted to a lone pair on the oxygen of another. Water is polar, so dipole–dipole forces act, and London dispersion forces act between all molecules.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for oxygen toward $\ce{Li+}$ because the δ− oxygen is attracted to the positive ion; 1 point for a hydrogen toward $\ce{Br-}$ because the δ+ hydrogen is attracted to the negative ion. A correct orientation with no reason from the partial charges earns 1 point for the two together.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for ion–dipole forces with the reason that an ion's full charge attracts more strongly than a partial charge. The name alone earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>1 point for all three: hydrogen bonds, dipole–dipole forces and London dispersion forces.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

## Part B: Solids, liquids and vapor pressure

This part covers topics 3.2 and 3.3: how the particles of a solid and the forces between them explain its properties, what melting and vaporizing overcome and what they leave whole, how vapor pressure and boiling point follow the forces in a liquid, and what the particles of each phase are doing.

<Question number={7} title="Two neighbors in period 4" topic="3.2" points={5}>

Gallium and germanium sit side by side in period 4 of the periodic table. Germanium is a metalloid.

| Property | Gallium, Ga | Germanium, Ge |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point | 29.76 °C | 938.25 °C |
| Hardness | soft enough to cut with a knife | hard; shatters when struck instead of bending |
| Conducts electricity as a solid? | yes | only weakly, far less than a metal (it is a semiconductor) |

<Part label="a">

Classify gallium as ionic, metallic, covalent network or molecular. Give the evidence from the table.

<Answer>

**Gallium is metallic.** It conducts electricity as a solid, which needs charges free to move through the solid. An ionic solid does not conduct as a solid, because its ions are held in place. A molecular solid has no charges free to move at all. A covalent network solid usually does not conduct either, and it is hard, while gallium is soft. In a metal, the valence electrons are delocalized in a sea around the metal ions, and they carry the current.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student classifies germanium as metallic too, because it conducts electricity as a solid. Evaluate the student's reasoning, and use the other evidence in the table to classify germanium.

<Answer>

The reasoning does not hold. Germanium conducts **only weakly**, far less than a metal, so its conduction is not the evidence of a sea of free electrons that a metal's is. The other properties do not fit a metal either. A typical metal bends when struck, because the sea of electrons keeps holding the ions together as layers slide; germanium shatters.

**Germanium is a covalent network solid.** It is hard and brittle, and it melts at a high temperature, 938.25 °C. It cannot be ionic: it is a single element, so it has no cations and anions. Covalent network solids form only from nonmetals and metalloids, and germanium is a metalloid.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Explain, in terms of germanium's particles and what holds them together, why germanium has a high melting point and why it shatters when struck instead of bending.

<Answer>

**High melting point.** Each germanium atom is covalently bonded to its neighbors, four of them, as carbon is in diamond. The bonds run through the whole crystal, so there are no separate molecules. To melt germanium, covalent bonds must be broken, and that takes a great deal of energy.

**Shatters.** The covalent bonds hold the atoms at fixed angles. One layer of atoms cannot slide over the next without breaking bonds, so a sharp blow cracks the crystal instead of bending it. In a typical metal, by contrast, the sea of electrons keeps holding the ions together while layers slide.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={1}>1 point for metallic, with conducting as a solid as the evidence. Calling gallium molecular because of its low melting point earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for rejecting the reasoning: germanium conducts only weakly, far less than a metal, or it shatters where a typical metal bends (either earns it); 1 point for covalent network, supported by its high melting point or its hardness and brittleness (either earns it); its being a metalloid may be added but does not earn the point alone. Agreeing that germanium is metallic earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={2}>1 point for covalent bonds running through the whole solid, which must be broken to melt it; 1 point for bonds at fixed angles, so the atoms cannot slide past one another without breaking bonds and the solid shatters. Saying that melting overcomes forces between germanium molecules earns 0 points for the first point.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={8} title="Potassium fluoride, solid, melted and dissolved" topic="3.2" points={4}>

Potassium fluoride, $\ce{KF}$, forms white crystals that melt at 858 °C. A crystal struck with a hammer shatters. Solid $\ce{KF}$ does not conduct electricity, but melted $\ce{KF}$ does, and so does a solution of $\ce{KF}$ in water.

<Part label="a">

Explain, in terms of particles, why solid $\ce{KF}$ does not conduct electricity but melted $\ce{KF}$ and a solution of $\ce{KF}$ both do.

<Answer>

Potassium fluoride is an ionic solid: a lattice of $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{F-}$ ions held together by the attraction between opposite charges. An electric current is moving charge.

In the solid, each ion is held in a fixed place by its oppositely charged neighbors. The ions are charged, but they **cannot move**, so the solid does not conduct.

When $\ce{KF}$ melts, the ions can move past one another. When it dissolves, the ions separate and move through the water. In both, **moving ions carry the current**. Electrons do not: $\ce{KF}$ has no free electrons in any of the three forms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why a $\ce{KF}$ crystal shatters when it is struck.

<Answer>

The blow pushes one layer of ions along the next by about one ion's width. Now $\ce{K+}$ sits opposite $\ce{K+}$, and $\ce{F-}$ opposite $\ce{F-}$. **Like charges face to face repel**, and the crystal splits apart along that layer.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

A student writes: "When $\ce{KF}$ melts, the K–F bonds break, and the liquid is made of potassium atoms and fluorine atoms." Evaluate the claim.

<Answer>

The claim is **wrong**. Solid $\ce{KF}$ is made of ions, not of $\ce{KF}$ molecules or of atoms. Melting frees the ions from their fixed places, but they **stay $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{F-}$ ions**. Each ion is still attracted to the oppositely charged ions around it; the attractions are rearranged, and the ions can now move past one another.

The data agree: melted $\ce{KF}$ conducts electricity, so it contains ions free to move. A liquid of neutral atoms would not carry a current that way.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for ions held in fixed places in the solid, so they cannot move; 1 point for ions free to move in the melt and in the solution, carrying the current. Saying that electrons carry the current in melted or dissolved KF earns 0 points for the second point.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>1 point for a shifted layer that brings like charges face to face, which repel and split the crystal. "Ionic bonds are brittle" with no particle-level reason earns 0 points.</RubricLine>

The compound 1,4-dichlorobenzene, $\ce{C6H4Cl2}$, was long used in mothballs. Its molecule is a flat ring of six carbon atoms with the two chlorine atoms on opposite sides of the ring, so the molecule is nonpolar. It melts at 53.1 °C.

<Part label="a">

Identify the kind of solid 1,4-dichlorobenzene is. Explain what the shrinking mothball shows about the forces between its molecules and about the vapor pressure of the solid at room temperature.

<Answer>

**The forces are weak.** At room temperature, far below the melting point, molecules still leave the solid and go into the air. They can do that only if the attractions holding them to their neighbors are weak. The low melting point agrees.

**The solid has a noticeable vapor pressure at room temperature.** Molecules escape from its surface into the space above it, and in the closed closet the vapor builds up until you can smell it. </Answer>

<Part label="b">

A student claims: "The mothball must melt at its surface first, at least a little. Molecules cannot go into the vapor straight from a solid." Evaluate the claim.

</Answer>

</Answer>

<Question number={12} title="Krypton between window panes" topic="3.4" points={4}>

An insulated window has two panes of glass with a sealed gap between them. Filling the gap with krypton, $\ce{Kr}$, slows the flow of heat through the window more than air does. At the factory, a gap that holds 9.50 L is filled with krypton at 1.00 atm and 19.0 °C, and then sealed.

<Part label="a">

Calculate the mass of krypton in the gap.

<Answer>

**Kelvin.**

$$
T = 19.0 + 273.15 = 292.2\ \text{K}
$$

**Moles.** Rearrange $PV = nRT$ for $n$, with $P$ in atm, $V$ in liters and $T$ in kelvin.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{PV}{RT} \\
&= \frac{(1.00)(9.50)}{(0.08206)(292.2)} \\
&= 0.3962\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 0.396 mol to three significant figures. Carry the extra digit.

**Mass.** The molar mass of krypton is 83.80 g/mol.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= 0.3962\ \text{mol} \\
&\quad \times 83.80\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= \mathbf{33.2}\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

On a hot, sunny day the krypton warms up. Assume that the volume of the gap does not change and that no gas escapes. Calculate the temperature, in kelvin and in °C, at which the pressure of the krypton reaches 1.10 atm.

<Answer>

The amount of gas and the volume are both fixed, so the pressure is proportional to the kelvin temperature:

$$
\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2}
$$

Rearrange for $T_2$:

$$
T_2 = T_1 \times \frac{P_2}{P_1}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_2 &= 292.2\ \text{K} \times \frac{1.10\ \text{atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} \\
&= \mathbf{321}\ \text{K}
\end{aligned}
$$

The pressures have three significant figures, so the temperature is good to the ones place. Subtract 273.15 from the unrounded 321.4 K to turn it into Celsius:

$$
321.4 - 273.15 = 48.25
$$

That is 48 °C to the ones place, so the krypton reaches 1.10 atm at **48 °C**.

A 10 percent rise in pressure needs a 10 percent rise in kelvin temperature, about 29 K. Multiplying the Celsius temperature by 1.10 instead gives 20.9 °C, which would mean that warming the gas by less than 2 degrees raises its pressure by 10 percent.

</Answer>

</Part>

The masses differ because an $\ce{N2O}$ molecule is heavier than an $\ce{O2}$ molecule (molar masses 44.02 and 32.00 g/mol), so the same number of moles of $\ce{N2O}$ has more mass.

</Answer>

</Part>

## Part D: Kinetic molecular theory and real gases

These five questions use the kinetic molecular theory to explain pressure, temperature and the spread of particle speeds. The last ones ask when and why a real gas strays from $PV = nRT$, in both directions.

<Question number={18} title="Three flasks of one gas" topic="3.5" points={5}>

Three rigid flasks of the same volume hold samples of the same gas. The gas behaves ideally.

| Flask | Amount of gas | Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| A | $n$ | 250 K |
| B | $n$ | 500 K |
| C | $2n$ | 250 K |

<Part label="a">

A student writes: "Flask B is twice as hot as flask A, so its particles move twice as fast." Determine how many times as fast the particles in flask B move, on average, as the particles in flask A. Justify your answer, and state whether the student is right.

<Answer>

The kelvin temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles. The kelvin temperature doubles from flask A to flask B, so the average kinetic energy doubles too:

$$
\frac{\mathit{KE}_{\text{B}}}{\mathit{KE}_{\text{A}}} = \frac{500\ \text{K}}{250\ \text{K}} = 2
$$

The speed does not double. A particle's kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, and the mass is the same in both flasks. So doubling the kinetic energy doubles $v^2$, and the speed rises by the square root of 2:

$$
\frac{v_{\text{B}}}{v_{\text{A}}} = \sqrt{2} = \mathbf{1.41}
$$

The student is wrong. The particles in flask B move about **1.41 times** as fast as those in flask A. Doubling the speed would take four times the kinetic energy, so four times the kelvin temperature: 1000 K.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Flasks B and C each have twice the pressure of flask A. Explain, at the level of particles, how the pressure doubles in flask B and how it doubles in flask C. State how the two cases differ.

<Answer>

The pressure of a gas is the push of its particles hitting the walls, spread over the walls' area.

**Flask B** has the same number of particles as flask A, but they move faster. Faster particles reach the walls more often, and each one strikes harder. The two effects together double the pressure.

**Flask C** is at the same temperature as flask A, so its particles move at the same speeds and each hit is just as hard. But there are twice as many particles, so each part of the wall is hit twice as often. Twice as many hits of the same strength give twice the pressure.

The difference: in flask B the hits are both harder and more frequent. In flask C only the number of hits changes.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

The pressure in flask C is exactly twice the pressure in flask A only because the gas behaves ideally. Identify one assumption of the kinetic molecular theory that this depends on, and explain how.

<Answer>

Either of two assumptions:

- **The particles do not attract each other.** So no neighbor tugs a particle back as it reaches the wall, and every hit is as hard as it would be with the particle alone. Doubling the particles doubles the hits, and nothing softens them.
- **The particles' own volume is negligible.** So adding particles takes none of the room the others move in. Each particle hits the walls as often as before, and twice the particles make exactly twice the hits.

Either way, each particle contributes its own hits, unaffected by how many others there are. In a real gas at high pressure neither assumption holds exactly, and doubling the amount of gas would not exactly double the pressure.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the average kinetic energy doubling because it is proportional to the kelvin temperature; 1 point for the speed rising by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, 1.41, because the kinetic energy goes as the square of the speed, so the student is wrong. Agreeing with the student (a factor of 2) earns the first point at most.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={2}>1 point for flask B: faster particles hit the walls both more often and harder; 1 point for flask C: the same speeds, so hits of the same strength, but twice as many particles making twice as many hits. Saying that the particles in flask C move faster or hit harder earns 0 points for flask C.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>Either assumption earns the point: no attractions between the particles, or the particles' own volume negligible, with how it leaves each particle's hits unchanged when more particles are added. Naming another assumption earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

</Question>

<Question number={19} title="A white ring in a glass tube" topic="3.5" points={4}>

A classic demonstration: a glass tube about a meter long lies flat on the bench at room temperature. At the same moment, a plug of cotton soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is pushed into one end, and a plug soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid into the other. Ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas leave the plugs and move along the air-filled tube. Where they meet, they react to form a white ring of solid ammonium chloride:

$$
\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}
$$

After ten minutes or so the ring appears, closer to the hydrochloric acid end than to the ammonia end. Molar masses: $\ce{NH3}$ 17.03 g/mol, $\ce{HCl}$ 36.46 g/mol.

<Part label="a">

Calculate how many times as fast an ammonia molecule moves, on average, as a hydrogen chloride molecule in the tube. Justify the relationship you use.

<Answer>

Both gases are at the same temperature, so their molecules have the same average kinetic energy:

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}m_{\ce{NH3}}v_{\ce{NH3}}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}m_{\ce{HCl}}v_{\ce{HCl}}^2
$$

The halves cancel. Rearrange for the ratio of the speeds:

$$
\frac{v_{\ce{NH3}}}{v_{\ce{HCl}}} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{\ce{HCl}}}{m_{\ce{NH3}}}}
$$

The masses of the molecules are in the same ratio as the molar masses:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{v_{\ce{NH3}}}{v_{\ce{HCl}}} &= \sqrt{\frac{36.46}{17.03}} \\
&= \sqrt{2.141} = \mathbf{1.46}
\end{aligned}
$$

An ammonia molecule moves about **1.46 times** as fast as a hydrogen chloride molecule. The lighter molecule must move faster to carry the same kinetic energy.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Explain why the ring forms closer to the hydrochloric acid end.

<Answer>

The ammonia molecules move faster on average. So in the time before the two gases meet, the ammonia covers more of the tube than the hydrogen chloride does. The meeting point, where the ring forms, is past the middle of the tube, **nearer the hydrochloric acid end**.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

Gas molecules at room temperature move at hundreds of meters per second, yet the ring takes many minutes to form. Explain, using the kinetic molecular theory.

<Answer>

The model says a particle moves in a straight line only until it collides with another particle, and then it goes off in a new, random direction. The tube is full of air, so an ammonia molecule collides with air molecules constantly. Its path is a zigzag that keeps changing direction and doubling back. Its overall progress along the tube is **far slower than its speed** between collisions, so the two gases take minutes to meet.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Rubric>
<RubricLine part="a" points={2}>1 point for the same average kinetic energy at the same temperature, so the lighter molecule moves faster (the two kinetic energies set equal, or the statement); 1 point for 1.46. The inverted ratio, 0.683, or the ratio of the molar masses without the square root, 2.14, earns the first point only.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="b" points={1}>Ammonia molecules move faster, so ammonia travels farther along the tube than hydrogen chloride in the same time.</RubricLine>
<RubricLine part="c" points={1}>Collisions with the air molecules (or other molecules) keep changing each molecule's direction at random, so its progress along the tube is far slower than its speed. "The molecules slow down in the tube" earns 0 points.</RubricLine>
</Rubric>

<Question number={20} title="Speed curves for neon and radon" topic="3.5, 3.6" points={7}>

Neon, Ne, is the gas that glows red-orange in neon signs. Radon, Rn, is a radioactive gas that seeps into some basements from the ground. Both gases are made of single atoms. Molar masses: Ne 20.18 g/mol, Rn 222 g/mol.

<Answer>

Both gases are at 20 °C, so their atoms have the same average kinetic energy. A radon atom is about 11 times as massive as a neon atom, so the neon atoms must move faster to carry the same energy.

The sketch must show:

- **Radon:** a tall, narrow curve, with its peak at a low speed.
- **Neon:** a lower, broader curve, with its peak at a much higher speed and a long tail toward high speeds.
- **The same area under both curves,** since each curve stands for all the atoms in its flask, and the flasks hold the same number.

$$
\sqrt{\frac{222}{20.18}} = 3.32

</Answer>

The neon is now held at 150 K, and later at 600 K. On a new set of axes, sketch the neon's speed distribution at each temperature. Then state how many times as fast the most probable speed at 600 K is as the most probable speed at 150 K, and justify your answer.

<Answer>

The kelvin temperature rises by a factor of 4, so the average kinetic energy of the atoms rises by a factor of 4:

$$
\frac{600\ \text{K}}{150\ \text{K}} = 4
$$

A particle's kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, and the mass does not change. So $v^2$ rises by a factor of 4, and every typical speed, the most probable speed among them, rises by the square root of 4:

$$
\sqrt{4} = \mathbf{2}
$$

The most probable speed at 600 K is **twice** the most probable speed at 150 K.

The sketch must show the 600 K curve lower and broader than the 150 K curve, with its peak at twice the speed and a tail reaching much higher speeds. The two areas are the same, since both curves stand for the same atoms.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="c">

At what temperature, in kelvins, would neon atoms have the same average speed as radon atoms at 20 °C? Show your reasoning.

<Answer>

Radon at 20 °C is at:

$$
T = 20 + 273 = 293\ \text{K}
$$

At the same speed, the kinetic energies $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ are in the ratio of the masses. The average kinetic energy is proportional to the kelvin temperature, so the kelvin temperatures must be in the same ratio:

$$
\frac{T_{\ce{Ne}}}{T_{\ce{Rn}}} = \frac{m_{\ce{Ne}}}{m_{\ce{Rn}}}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{\ce{Ne}} &= (293\ \text{K}) \times \frac{20.18}{222} \\

\end{aligned}

The neon must be far colder. Its atoms are lighter, so they reach radon's speed with much less kinetic energy.

<Question number={21} title="Ammonia under pressure" topic="3.6" points={4}>

Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, is the refrigerant in many ice rinks and large cold stores. In the plant, a compressor raises the pressure of ammonia gas, and the gas turns to liquid as it is cooled: at 25 °C, ammonia condenses when its pressure reaches 9.9 atm. At 1 atm, ammonia boils at −33.3 °C.

A sample of ammonia gas at 25 °C and 8.0 atm has a value of $PV/nRT$ less than 1.

<Part label="a">

Identify the intermolecular force that makes the attractions between ammonia molecules strong for so small a molecule. Then explain, at the level of particles, how attractions make $PV/nRT$ less than 1 for this sample.

<Answer>

Ammonia molecules attract each other by London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding. With just 10 electrons, an ammonia molecule has weak London dispersion forces. **Hydrogen bonding** is what makes the attractions strong: a hydrogen bonded to nitrogen in one molecule is attracted to the lone pair on the nitrogen of a neighbor. That is why so small a molecule boils as high as −33.3 °C.

At the level of particles: a molecule about to hit the wall is tugged back by the molecules behind it, so it strikes more softly. With every collision softened, the measured pressure is lower than $PV = nRT$ predicts, and $PV/nRT$ is **less than 1**. The effect is noticeable here because at 8.0 atm the molecules are much closer together than at 1 atm, and the gas is close to condensing, which happens at 9.9 atm.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

A student claims: "At 8.0 atm the ammonia molecules are crowded together, so their own volume is what makes this sample differ from an ideal gas." Explain why the student's explanation cannot account for this sample's $PV/nRT$. Give two reasons.

<Answer>

There are two reasons.

**The direction is wrong.** The molecules' own volume makes $PV/nRT$ greater than 1: the space they move in is less than the container's volume, so the gas cannot be squeezed as far as $PV = nRT$ predicts. This sample's ratio is less than 1, the direction that attractions push it.

**The effect is too small at this pressure.** At 8.0 atm the molecules are still far apart compared with their size, and their own volume is a tiny share of the container. At this pressure the molecules' own volume is too small to outweigh the attractions. A gas usually has to be squeezed to a very high pressure before particle volume wins, and ammonia at 25 °C condenses long before that, at 9.9 atm.

</Answer>

</Part>

<Question number={22} title="Hydrogen in a fuel-cell car" topic="3.6" points={5}>
