Put three things on a kitchen counter: a spoonful of table salt, a spoonful of sugar and a copper pot. Heat the salt in your hottest pan and it will not melt, yet melted salt conducts electricity. Sugar melts at about 185 °C, then chars, and it never conducts. The copper pot bends without breaking and conducts while it is solid. Three kinds of bonding sit behind those three behaviors.

## Electronegativity

When two atoms share a pair of electrons, they do not always share it fairly. **Electronegativity** is how strongly an atom in a bond pulls the shared electrons toward itself.

For the main-group elements, electronegativity generally rises as you move across a period and falls as you move down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element of all.

Both trends come from Coulomb's law. The attraction between a nucleus and an electron grows with the charge doing the pulling, and it shrinks as the distance grows.

- **Across a period**, each element adds a proton, while the new electron goes into the same shell and shields the others very little. The pull grows, and the shell is drawn in a little closer.
- **Down a group**, the extra protons are offset by extra inner shells of electrons that shield them. The valence electrons sit in a shell farther out, so the pull is weaker.

Here are some values on the Pauling scale, the usual way of putting numbers on it:

| Period | Electronegativity, left to right |
|---|---|
| 1 | H 2.2 |
| 2 | Li 1.0, C 2.6, N 3.0, O 3.4, F 4.0 |
| 3 | Na 0.9, Mg 1.3, S 2.6, Cl 3.2 |
| 4 | K 0.8, Br 3.0 |

Read along a row and the values climb. Read down a group and they drop: Li 1.0, Na 0.9, K 0.8, and F 4.0, Cl 3.2, Br 3.0. The exam will not ask you to recall these numbers. It asks for the trend and for what the trend explains. The numbers are here so you can see it.

<KeyIdea>

Electronegativity is Coulomb's law at work. More unshielded nuclear charge pulling, or less distance between the nucleus and the shared electrons, means a stronger pull on those electrons.

</KeyIdea>

## Sharing evenly, sharing unevenly

Think of the shared pair as a rope in a tug-of-war. If both atoms pull equally hard, the pair sits in the middle. Two atoms with the same, or nearly the same, electronegativity share the bonding electrons evenly, or so nearly evenly that the difference does not matter. That is a **nonpolar covalent bond**. The bond in Cl₂ is one: both ends are chlorine, so neither wins.

A C–H bond counts as nonpolar too. Carbon is only slightly more electronegative than hydrogen, so the sharing is close to even.

When the two atoms differ, the shared pair sits nearer the atom that pulls harder. That is a **polar covalent bond**. The more electronegative atom ends up a little negative, and the other a little positive. We write these partial charges as δ− and δ+ (δ is the Greek letter delta). In hydrogen chloride, chlorine pulls harder, so chlorine is δ−:

$$
\overset{\delta+}{\ce{H}}{-}\overset{\delta-}{\ce{Cl}}
$$

A pair of partial charges like this, one at each end of a bond, is a **bond dipole**. Among single bonds, the bigger the difference in electronegativity, the bigger the dipole. Bonds to hydrogen from the halogens show it:

- H–F: difference 1.8, the most polar of the three
- H–Cl: difference 1.0
- H–Br: difference 0.8, the least polar

You will also see a bond dipole drawn as an arrow along the bond. The arrow points toward the δ− end, and its tail carries a small cross at the δ+ end, so it looks a little like a plus sign that has been stretched into an arrow.

<Worked title="Ranking four bonds to hydrogen">

Rank C–H, N–H, O–H and F–H from least to most polar, and name the δ− atom where there is one.

1. **Find each difference** from the table.
   - C–H: $2.6 - 2.2 = 0.4$
   - N–H: $3.0 - 2.2 = 0.8$
   - O–H: $3.4 - 2.2 = 1.2$
   - F–H: $4.0 - 2.2 = 1.8$

2. **Put them in order.** A bigger difference means a more polar bond:

   $$
   \ce{C-H} < \ce{N-H} < \ce{O-H} < \ce{F-H}
   $$

3. **Name the δ− atom.** In N–H, O–H and F–H, the atom bonded to hydrogen is δ− and hydrogen is δ+. In C–H, carbon is very slightly richer in electrons, but the difference is so small that the bond is treated as nonpolar, with no δ labels.

4. **Spot the nonpolar one.** C–H has the smallest difference, and it is the one treated as nonpolar.

## Not three boxes but a continuum

Keep making the difference bigger and something changes. Sodium (0.9) and chlorine (3.2) differ by 2.3. That is so lopsided that sodium's electron is, in effect, handed over to chlorine. Sodium chloride is made of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, held together by the attraction between opposite charges. That is **ionic bonding**.

There is no sharp line where polar covalent stops and ionic begins. Every polar bond has some ionic character, and the bigger the difference, the more it has. Nonpolar, polar and ionic are not three boxes. They are stretches of one scale.

So how do you decide what kind of bonding a compound has? The difference alone does not settle it. Two working rules get you most of the way:

- A metal bonded to a nonmetal is generally ionic.
- Two nonmetals bonded together are generally covalent.

Ammonium salts such as NH₄Cl break the second rule. They contain only nonmetals, but they are ionic compounds: NH₄⁺ ions, held together inside by covalent N–H bonds, and Cl⁻ ions.

The best evidence is how the compound behaves. An ionic solid usually has a high melting point. It conducts electricity when it is melted or dissolved in water, but not as a solid. A solid made of molecules usually melts at a lower temperature and does not conduct, as a solid or when melted. That is the salt and the sugar from the kitchen counter.

## Metallic bonding

The copper pot needs a different picture. In a metal, the atoms give up their valence electrons to the whole solid. Those electrons are **delocalized**: they belong to no single atom and move freely through a lattice of positive metal ions.

A current is moving charge. In a metal the moving charges are the delocalized electrons, so a metal conducts while it is still solid. In an ionic solid the ions are locked in place until it melts or dissolves. Topic 2.4 builds this model in full and uses it to explain why metals bend instead of shattering.

<Trap>

Four mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Calling C–H polar.** Carbon and hydrogen are close enough in electronegativity that a C–H bond is treated as nonpolar.
- **Putting δ− on the wrong atom.** The more electronegative atom is the one that is partly negative.
- **Treating ionic and covalent as separate boxes.** They are the two ends of one continuum, and a compound's properties are the best evidence of where it sits.
- **Explaining electronegativity with "the atom wants electrons."** Atoms do not want anything. Explain it with nuclear charge and distance: Coulomb's law.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond pulls the shared electrons toward itself.
- Electronegativity rises across a period and falls down a group, because of nuclear charge and distance (Coulomb's law).
- Equal sharing makes a nonpolar covalent bond. Unequal sharing makes a polar covalent bond, with δ− on the more electronegative atom.
- A bigger electronegativity difference means a bigger bond dipole, and bonds shade gradually from polar covalent into ionic.
- A metal with a nonmetal is generally ionic and two nonmetals are generally covalent, but a compound's properties are the best evidence.
- In a metal, the valence electrons are delocalized across the whole solid.

</KeyPoints>
