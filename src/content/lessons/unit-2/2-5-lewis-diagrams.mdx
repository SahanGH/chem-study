A Lewis diagram is bookkeeping. It is an account of every valence electron in a molecule, and each one has to be somewhere: either shared in a bond between two atoms, or sitting on one atom as a lone pair. Nothing goes missing and nothing appears from nowhere. If the books do not balance, the diagram is wrong.

## Counting the electrons

So the first job is always the count. For a main-group element, read it off the periodic table: the number of valence electrons is the last digit of the group number. Carbon is in group 14, so it has 4. (Helium, with 2, is the one exception.) The ones you will meet most are hydrogen with 1, carbon with 4, nitrogen with 5, oxygen with 6, and fluorine and chlorine with 7 each.

Add them up for every atom in the formula. An ion needs one more step. A negative ion has gained electrons, so add one for each unit of charge. A positive ion has lost them, so take one away for each unit.

- Methane, CH₄: $4 + 4 \times 1 = 8$.
- Ammonium, NH₄⁺: $5 + 4 - 1 = 8$. The + charge means one electron fewer.
- Carbonate, CO₃²⁻: $4 + 3 \times 6 + 2 = 24$. The 2− charge means two more.

Those electrons are then drawn in pairs. A **bonding pair** is shared between two atoms and is drawn as a line between them. A **lone pair** belongs to one atom and is drawn as two dots beside it.

## Five steps

With the count in hand, the drawing goes the same way every time.

1. **Count** the valence electrons, adjusted for any charge.

2. **Write the skeleton.** The central atom is usually the least electronegative atom other than hydrogen. Hydrogen is never central, because it forms only one bond.

3. **Join each outer atom to the center** with a single bond. Each bond uses two electrons.

4. **Finish the outer atoms.** Give each outer atom lone pairs until it has eight electrons around it. Hydrogen needs only two.

5. **Put any electrons left over on the central atom.** If the central atom still has fewer than eight, take a lone pair from an outer atom and turn it into a second bond to the center, making a double bond. Take two pairs from the same atom and you get a triple bond. Keep going until the center has eight. Boron is the exception, as you will see below.

When you count the electrons around an atom, count its lone-pair electrons and both electrons of every bond it is part of. A shared pair counts for both of the atoms that share it.

<KeyIdea>

Check twice at the end. The number of electrons drawn must equal your count. And every C, N, O and F should have eight electrons around it, and every hydrogen two. Boron may stop at six, and a molecule with an odd number of electrons must leave one atom short.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Water">

Draw the Lewis diagram of water, H₂O.

1. **Count.** $2 \times 1 + 6 = 8$ electrons.

2. **Skeleton.** Oxygen goes in the center. Hydrogen cannot.

3. **Single bonds.** Two O–H bonds use 4 electrons.

4. **Outer atoms.** Each hydrogen already has its two, from its bond.

5. **Center.** The 4 electrons left go on oxygen as two lone pairs. Oxygen now has eight around it: four in bonds and four in lone pairs.

Both checks pass: 8 electrons drawn, 8 counted, and every atom has its full number.

<Worked title="Carbon dioxide">

Draw the Lewis diagram of carbon dioxide, CO₂.

1. **Count.** $4 + 2 \times 6 = 16$ electrons.

2. **Skeleton.** Carbon goes in the center, because it is less electronegative than oxygen.

3. **Single bonds.** Two C–O bonds use 4, leaving 12.

4. **Outer atoms.** Each oxygen gets three lone pairs. That uses all 12, and none are left.

5. **Center.** Carbon has only four electrons around it, from its two bonds. So move one lone pair from each oxygen into its bond with carbon. That gives O=C=O, each oxygen with two lone pairs. Now every atom has eight.

Check the books: two double bonds hold 8 electrons and four lone pairs hold 8 more, for 16 in all.

## Ions

An ion is drawn by the same five steps, with the adjusted count at step 1. When the diagram is finished, put it in square brackets and write the charge outside them, at the upper right. The brackets say the charge belongs to the whole ion, not to any one atom.

Ammonium, NH₄⁺, has $5 + 4 - 1 = 8$ electrons. Four N–H bonds use all eight. Nitrogen has eight around it and no lone pairs.

## When eight is not the number

Eight is the target for most atoms, but not all of them.

Hydrogen holds two. Boron is often satisfied with six. In BF₃, boron is in the center with three single bonds, each fluorine has three lone pairs, and that uses every electron. Boron ends up with six around it.

Some atoms go the other way. A central atom from the third period or below can be drawn with more than eight electrons around it in a Lewis diagram. SF₆ has twelve around sulfur, and PCl₅ has ten around phosphorus. Six neighbors do not fit on four sides, so in the diagram of SF₆ two of the fluorine atoms sit on the diagonals.

Atoms of the second period, such as C, N, O and F, never hold more than eight. Only atoms from the third period down are drawn with more.

One more kind of molecule cannot follow the rule at all. A molecule with an odd number of electrons, such as NO or NO₂, must leave one electron unpaired, so some atom is left short of eight. Topic 2.6 takes this up.

<Trap>

Five mistakes to watch for when you draw a Lewis diagram:

- **Forgetting the charge of an ion.** Add electrons for a negative charge, and remove them for a positive one.
- **Putting hydrogen in the middle.** It forms only one bond, so it is always on the outside.
- **Counting a bond as one electron.** Every bond line is two electrons.
- **Leaving the central atom short.** Make a multiple bond from an outer atom's lone pair, unless the center is boron or the molecule has an odd number of electrons.
- **Giving C, N, O or F more than eight.** Only atoms from the third period down are drawn with more.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Start by counting the valence electrons of every atom, then add one for each negative charge or take one away for each positive charge.
- The least electronegative atom other than hydrogen usually goes in the middle; hydrogen never does.
- Draw single bonds first, then give the outer atoms their octets, then put what is left on the central atom.
- If the central atom has fewer than eight, turn lone pairs on outer atoms into double or triple bonds (not for boron, or a molecule with an odd number of electrons).
- Second-period atoms never exceed eight and hydrogen holds two. Boron is often content with six, and atoms from the third period down can hold more than eight.

</KeyPoints>
