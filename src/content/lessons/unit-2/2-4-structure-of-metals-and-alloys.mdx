Bend a paper clip and it stays bent. Hit a salt crystal with a hammer and it shatters. Both are solids held together by electric attraction, so why does one bend and the other break?

## A sea of electrons

A metal is a regular array of positive ions. Each ion is a metal atom without its valence electrons.

Those electrons have not gone far. They are **delocalized**: shared by the whole piece of metal, not by one pair of atoms, and free to move among the ions. No electron belongs to any one ion, and every ion is pulled on by the electrons all around it.

The attraction between the positive ions and this sea of electrons is the **metallic bond**. It holds the whole solid together at once.

## What the model explains

A model is worth keeping when it explains what you can see. This one explains the properties that make metals useful.

**Conductivity.** Connect a metal wire to a battery and the delocalized electrons move along the wire. Moving charge is a current. So a metal conducts as a solid; you do not have to melt it first.

**Malleability and ductility.** A **malleable** solid can be hammered into thin sheets, and a **ductile** one can be drawn out into wire. Both happen because layers of particles slide past each other. When a layer of ions in a metal slides past the next, the electron sea moves with it. After the slide, each ion is still surrounded by the electron sea, so no new repulsion appears. The metal bends instead of breaking.

Compare the ionic crystal in topic 2.3. Slide one of its layers by one ion and cations land next to cations, anions next to anions. The like charges repel, and the crystal cracks.

The same mobile electrons carry heat through a metal, which is why a metal spoon in hot soup soon feels hot. They also give a metal its shine.

<KeyIdea>

In an ionic solid, a shift puts like charges side by side. In a metal, the electron sea flows along with the ions and keeps holding them, so the metal deforms instead of breaking.

</KeyIdea>

## Alloys

Most of the metal you handle every day is not a pure element. An **alloy** is a metal mixed with one or more other elements. Most alloys are **solid solutions**, a kind of mixture, so their composition can vary.

How the second element fits in depends on the size of its atoms.

In a **substitutional alloy**, the atoms are of similar size, so atoms of one element take the places of atoms of the other in the lattice. In brass, zinc atoms (radius about 134 pm) replace some of the copper atoms (about 128 pm).

In an **interstitial alloy**, the second element's atoms are much smaller and fit into the gaps between the larger atoms. The gaps between atoms are called interstitial spaces, which is where the name comes from. In steel, carbon atoms (about 77 pm) sit in the gaps between iron atoms (about 126 pm).

<Worked title="Which kind of alloy?">

Decide whether each mixture forms a substitutional or an interstitial alloy.

1. **Nickel mixed into copper.** Nickel's radius is about 124 pm and copper's is about 128 pm. The radii are within a few percent of each other, so nickel atoms can take the places of copper atoms. The alloy is substitutional.

2. **Carbon mixed into iron.** Carbon's radius is about 77 pm and iron's is about 126 pm. Carbon is much smaller, so it fits into the gaps between the iron atoms. The alloy is interstitial.

3. **The rule.** Compare the radii. Similar sizes swap places; a much smaller atom goes between.

</Worked>

## Why alloys are harder

In a pure metal every atom is the same size, and layers of atoms slide past each other easily. Put atoms of a different size into the lattice and the layers catch on them. Sliding gets harder.

Steel is harder and stronger than pure iron, and less malleable and ductile, because the carbon atoms in the gaps make it harder for the iron layers to slide. Brass, for the same kind of reason, is harder than pure copper. An alloy still has its sea of electrons, so it still conducts electricity.

<Trap>

Four mistakes to avoid on this topic:

- **Describing metal atoms as sharing electrons in pairs.** The valence electrons are delocalized over the whole solid.
- **Explaining malleability by calling metals "soft".** Explain it with layers of ions sliding while the electron sea keeps holding them.
- **Mixing up the two kinds of alloy.** Substitutional: similar size, one atom replaces another. Interstitial: much smaller atoms, in the gaps.
- **Calling an alloy a compound.** Most alloys are solid solutions, and their composition can vary.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A metal is an array of positive ions in a sea of delocalized electrons.
- Moving electrons make metals conduct electricity as solids.
- The electron sea holds the ions as layers slide, so metals bend instead of breaking.
- A substitutional alloy forms from atoms of similar size, one replacing the other in the lattice, as zinc replaces copper in brass.
- An interstitial alloy forms when much smaller atoms fill the gaps between the larger ones, as carbon does in the iron of steel.
- Both kinds of alloy are usually harder than the pure metal, and they still conduct.

</KeyPoints>
