Push a probe thermometer into a chicken just out of the oven, and the number climbs for several seconds before it holds still. A thermometer can report only one temperature: its own. What you are waiting for is the probe coming to the temperature of the meat. This lesson is about what happens, particle by particle, during that wait, and what is true once the number stops.

## Warmer means more kinetic energy, on average

Topic 3.5 pictured the particles of a gas in constant motion, and showed that temperature measures their average kinetic energy. The same holds for a liquid, whose particles move past one another, and for a solid, whose particles vibrate about fixed positions. Whatever a body is made of, a higher temperature means its particles have a greater average kinetic energy.

Two things follow. A warmer body's particles have, on average, more kinetic energy than a cooler body's. And two bodies at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy per particle, even if their particles are quite different.

"Average" matters here, as it did in topic 3.5. In any body, some particles have more kinetic energy than the average and some have less.

The same average kinetic energy does not mean the same speed. Topic 3.5 showed that lighter particles move faster at the same temperature.

A glass bottle of olive oil has stood in a kitchen cupboard at 20 °C for days. How does the average kinetic energy of the particles in the oil compare with that of the particles in the glass?

- The oil's is greater, because the particles of a liquid are free to move around.
- The glass's is zero, because particles that are held in place in a solid do not move.
- Whichever of the two has the greater mass, the oil or the bottle, has the greater average.
- They are equal, because the oil and the glass are at the same temperature.

<div slot="why">After days in the cupboard the oil and the glass are both at 20 °C, and bodies at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy per particle. Being a liquid changes how the particles are arranged and how they can move, not how much kinetic energy they have on average. The particles of the glass are held in place but they vibrate, and vibration is motion. And an average is per particle, so it does not depend on how much oil or glass there is.</div>
</Check>

## Collisions carry the energy across

Two bodies are in **thermal contact** when energy can pass between them as heat (topic 6.1), as it can when they touch.

Where a warm body touches a cool one, the particles at the two surfaces collide. The warm body's particles have more kinetic energy on average. When one of them strikes a particle of the cool body that has less kinetic energy, it usually comes away with less kinetic energy than it had, and the particle it struck comes away with more. Each particle then collides with its own neighbors, and the energy spreads inward.

Any one collision can go either way. Now and then a particle of the cool body gives energy to a particle of the warm one. But collisions that carry energy from the warm body to the cool body outweigh the others. So on balance, energy passes from the warmer body to the cooler one.

Energy that passes from one body to another because of a difference in temperature is called **heat**. "Heat transfer", "heat exchange" and "energy transferred as heat" all name this one process.

Two things follow from the picture. No particles need to cross from one body to the other: energy crosses, and matter stays where it was. And the warmer body cools by losing energy, not by receiving something cold. A stone floor feels cold under bare feet because your skin is losing energy to it.

Heat is the word for energy while it is being passed across, not for something a body stores. Once the energy has arrived it is simply part of the energy of the body's particles. So it is wrong to say a body "has heat". What it has is energy, and a temperature.

You pick up an aluminum can of soda that has been in a refrigerator at 4 °C. Your palm is at about 33 °C. Which statement describes what happens where the skin meets the can?

- Cold flows out of the can into the skin until the skin's particles have slowed down.
- Particles of the skin collide with particles of the can and, on balance, pass kinetic energy to them.
- Fast particles leave the skin and move into the can's wall, carrying their kinetic energy across with them.
- Collisions swap energy back and forth until the hand and the can each hold the same total amount of energy.

<div slot="why">The skin is warmer, so its particles have the greater average kinetic energy. Where skin and can touch, the particles collide, and on balance the collisions pass kinetic energy from the skin's particles to the can's. That net transfer of energy is heat. The skin feels cold because it is losing energy, not because any "cold" comes into it. No particles cross from the skin into the metal: energy crosses, not matter. And the transfer is driven by the difference in temperature, not by the totals. A hand and a can do not need to hold the same amount of energy, and they will not.</div>
</Check>

<Worked title="A cooling iron, particle by particle">

An electric clothes iron is unplugged with its steel soleplate at 150 °C and stood on its heel in a room where the air is at 20 °C. An hour later the plate is at the temperature of the room. Explain, in terms of particles, how the plate cooled and when the cooling stops.

A hot plate also sends out some energy as infrared light (topic 3.11). This example follows the collisions.

1. **Compare the average kinetic energies.** The plate is at the higher temperature, so its atoms have a greater average kinetic energy than the molecules of the air.

2. **Find where the bodies touch.** The molecules of the air are in constant motion (topic 3.5), and every moment some of them strike the surface of the plate.

3. **Describe the collisions.** On balance, a molecule of the air comes away from the plate with more kinetic energy than it arrived with, and the atoms at the plate's surface are left with less. That molecule then collides with other molecules of the air and passes energy on, out into the room. Inside the plate, atoms deeper in pass energy to the atoms at the surface in the same way.

4. **Say what happens to each temperature.** The average kinetic energy of the plate's atoms falls, so the plate cools. The molecules of the air gain that energy. Energy has been transferred as heat from the plate to the air.

5. **Say when it stops.** The net transfer goes on as long as the plate is warmer than the air that touches it. It ends when the plate and the air are at one temperature. Their particles then have the same average kinetic energy.

6. **Write the explanation.** "The atoms of the plate have the greater average kinetic energy. In collisions at its surface they pass kinetic energy, on balance, to the molecules of the air, which pass it on to other molecules of the air. The plate cools until it is at the temperature of the air, when the particles of both have the same average kinetic energy."

</Worked>

## Thermal equilibrium

Two bodies in thermal contact are at **thermal equilibrium** when their temperatures are the same. Their particles then have the same average kinetic energy. The unplugged iron reached thermal equilibrium with the air of the room.

A stainless steel probe thermometer at 22 °C is pushed into a roast chicken at 74 °C. After several seconds the reading holds steady at 74 °C. Which statement is true once the reading is steady?

- Energy still passes both ways between probe and meat in collisions, in equal amounts.
- Collisions between the particles of the probe and the particles of the meat have stopped.
- The probe and the chicken now hold equal amounts of energy, which keeps the reading steady.
- The particles of the steel probe now move at the same average speed as the particles of the meat.

<div slot="why">A steady reading means the probe and the meat are at thermal equilibrium: the same temperature, and so the same average kinetic energy per particle. The particles are still moving at 74 °C, so they still collide, and energy still passes in both directions. The amounts are equal, so there is no net transfer and the reading does not change. A small probe has far fewer particles than a chicken, so it holds far less energy in total. And the same average kinetic energy does not mean the same speed: the probe's iron atoms are much heavier than the water molecules that make up most of the meat, so they move more slowly on average, just as heavier gas particles moved more slowly than lighter ones in topic 3.5.</div>
</Check>

Think about what that steady reading tells you. The number on the probe holds still, yet at 74 °C the probe's atoms are still vibrating and the meat's molecules are still striking them. A reading can hold still in that traffic only if the probe hands its neighbors as much energy as it takes from them. That is thermal equilibrium, seen from the inside: energy crossing in both directions, with no net transfer. The reading is the probe's own temperature, which is now the meat's as well.

Topic 7.1 has the same kind of balance: two opposite processes going on at equal rates.

<KeyIdea>

When two bodies touch, collisions between their particles carry energy, on balance, toward the cooler body. That energy is heat, and it keeps passing until both bodies are at one temperature. Then their particles have the same average kinetic energy, and each body gives energy to the other as fast as it takes it.

</KeyIdea>

## One temperature, different totals

Lift a ladle of stew out of a pot. The ladleful and the pot are at the same temperature, so their particles have the same average kinetic energy. But the pot holds many times as many particles, so it holds many times as much energy. Temperature is about the average for one particle. The energy of a whole body depends also on how much of it there is and on what it is made of.

So at thermal equilibrium two bodies share a temperature. They do not share out the energy equally. Look again at the bottom drawing of the figure. The streaks in the two bodies have the same average length, but the larger body has 10 particles to the smaller one's 6, so it holds more energy in total.

A swimmer whose skin is at 33 °C dives into a lake at 18 °C. The lake holds enormously more energy in total than the swimmer does. Which way is energy transferred as heat, and why?

- From the lake to the swimmer, because the lake holds far more energy in total.
- Neither way, because a lake is far too large for one swimmer to change its temperature.
- From the swimmer to the lake, because the skin's particles have the greater average kinetic energy.
- Both ways in equal amounts from the first moment, because collisions pass energy in both directions.

<div slot="why">The direction is set by temperature, not by the total. The swimmer's skin is warmer than the water, so its particles have the greater average kinetic energy, and collisions with water molecules pass energy, on balance, from the skin to the water. That is why the lake feels cold. The lake's huge total reflects its huge number of molecules, not how energetic each one is. The lake's temperature changes too little to measure, but energy is still transferred to it. And equal amounts both ways describes thermal equilibrium, which a 33 °C swimmer and an 18 °C lake have not reached.</div>
</Check>

The direction of heat is decided by temperature alone. Energy passes from the body with the higher temperature to the body with the lower one, however much energy each holds in total.

The temperature the two bodies end at lies between the two starting temperatures, but not necessarily halfway. Where it falls depends on the amount of each body and on the substances. The iron ended very close to 20 °C, because the air, walls and floor of the room together take far more energy to warm by one degree than a steel plate gives up in cooling by one. Topic 6.4 works it out.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Speaking of the heat a body holds.** Heat is the energy being passed between bodies. What a body holds is energy, and it shows a temperature.
- **Saying cold flows into the warm body.** The warm body cools because, on balance, it loses energy, not because something cold enters it.
- **Saying the collisions stop at thermal equilibrium.** They go on, and energy passes both ways in equal amounts.
- **Saying the two bodies end with equal energy.** They end with equal temperatures. The bigger body usually holds more energy.
- **Letting the body with more energy be the giver.** Temperature decides the direction, not the total.
- **Assuming the final temperature is halfway.** It lies between the two starting temperatures, and topic 6.4 finds where.
- **Reading equal temperature as equal speed.** The average kinetic energies match. Lighter particles are still faster.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The particles of a warmer body have a greater average kinetic energy than the particles of a cooler body, whatever the bodies are made of.
- When two bodies are in thermal contact, collisions between their particles pass energy, on balance, from the warmer body to the cooler one. This transfer of energy is called heat.
- No particles and no "cold" need to cross between the bodies, only energy.
- At thermal equilibrium the two bodies have the same temperature, and their particles have the same average kinetic energy.
- At thermal equilibrium the collisions go on and each body gives as much energy as it gets, so the net transfer is zero.
- Bodies at one temperature can hold different totals of energy, and the final temperature need not be halfway between the starting ones.

</KeyPoints>
