A plant that makes silicon for solar cells and computer chips feeds quartz and carbon into an electric furnace, and the furnace runs day and night on a very large supply of electricity. How much energy does the reaction itself have to take in? Nobody needs to measure it in that furnace. For each compound there is one number in a table, and from those numbers the enthalpy change of any reaction among the substances in the table comes out by multiplying, adding and subtracting.

## One number for each compound

The **standard enthalpy of formation** of a compound, $\Delta H_f^\circ$, is the enthalpy change when one mole of the compound forms from its elements, with each element in its standard state. (The next section says what a standard state is.) Its unit is kJ/mol, for a mole of the compound formed.

The reaction the number belongs to is the compound's **formation reaction**. Here is the one for quartz:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Si(s) + O2(g) -> SiO2(s)} \\
&\Delta H_f^\circ = -910.7\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Forming a mole of quartz from silicon and oxygen releases 910.7 kJ.

A formation reaction does not have to be one that anyone can run. Here is the one for baking soda, sodium hydrogen carbonate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Na(s) + 1/2 H2(g)} \\
&\ce{{}+ C(s, graphite) + 3/2 O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{NaHCO3(s)} \\
&\Delta H_f^\circ = -950.8\ \text{kJ/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Nobody makes baking soda by mixing sodium, hydrogen, graphite and oxygen. The value is worked out from reactions that can be measured (topic 6.9 shows how), and it is just as useful as if the reaction ran.

Three rules for writing a formation reaction:

- The compound is the only product, and there is exactly one mole of it.
- The reactants are elements, each in its standard state, and nothing else.
- Fractions are allowed as coefficients, and are often needed to keep the product at one mole.

## The standard state, and why elements are zero

The **standard state** of a substance is its pure form at a pressure of 1 bar (very nearly 1 atm). Tables give values at 25 °C, so for an element the standard state is normally the form that is most stable at 25 °C and 1 bar:

- O₂(g) for oxygen, not O₃ and not separate O atoms;
- Br₂(l) for bromine;
- N₂(g) for nitrogen;
- Na(s) for sodium;
- graphite for carbon.

The little circle in $\Delta H^\circ$ and $\Delta H_f^\circ$ says "everything in its standard state".

Now the zero. The formation reaction of an element in its standard state would be, for example, this:

$$
\ce{O2(g) -> O2(g)}
$$

Nothing changes, so the enthalpy change is zero. **$\Delta H_f^\circ$ of an element in its standard state is 0**, exactly, by definition. It is not left out of a calculation. It goes in as zero.

The zero is only for that form. Bromine vapor, Br₂(g), has $\Delta H_f^\circ = +30.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$: at 25 °C it takes 30.9 kJ to turn a mole of liquid bromine into vapor. Separate hydrogen atoms, H(g), have $\Delta H_f^\circ = +218.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$. That is half of the H–H bond enthalpy (topic 6.7), because each mole of H₂ gives two moles of atoms.

What about the sign of a compound's value? Most compounds have a negative $\Delta H_f^\circ$: forming them from their elements releases energy. A few are positive. Acetylene, C₂H₂(g), is +227.4 kJ/mol: forming it from graphite and hydrogen takes energy in.

<KeyIdea>

$\Delta H_f^\circ$ is the enthalpy change for forming one mole of a compound from its elements in their standard states. For an element in its standard state it is zero.

</KeyIdea>

## From the table to any reaction

Picture the reaction going by way of the elements. First the reactants are taken apart into their elements: for each reactant that is its formation reaction run backward, so it costs the opposite of its $\Delta H_f^\circ$. Then the elements are put together as the products: each product brings in its $\Delta H_f^\circ$. The route through the elements starts and ends where the direct reaction does, so it has the same enthalpy change (topic 6.9 gives the reason).

<KeyIdea>

The standard enthalpy change of a reaction is the products' total minus the reactants' total:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ ={} & \textstyle\sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) \\
& - \textstyle\sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $n$ is each substance's coefficient in the balanced equation.

</KeyIdea>

This formula is on the equation sheet, but printed without the $n$: the coefficients are yours to put in. The answer is the standard enthalpy change for the equation as written, in $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ (topic 6.6).

And notice the order: products minus reactants. For bond enthalpies (topic 6.7) it was the reactants' bonds first. The two orders are different because the two tables hold different things. A bond enthalpy is the energy to take a substance apart, and an enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change of putting it together.

Unlike a bond-enthalpy estimate, this is not an estimate, and it works for solids, liquids and gases alike, because each value belongs to that substance in that state.

<Worked title="The silicon furnace">

Find $\Delta H^\circ$ for the reaction in the silicon furnace.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{SiO2(s) + 2C(s, graphite) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Si(s) + 2CO(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

1. **Write a value under every substance.** All are in kJ/mol.

   - SiO₂(s): −910.7
   - C(s, graphite): 0, an element in its standard state
   - Si(s): 0, the same
   - CO(g): −110.5

2. **Add up the products, with coefficients.**

   $$
   1(0) + 2(-110.5) = -221.0\ \text{kJ}
   $$

3. **Add up the reactants, with coefficients.**

   $$
   1(-910.7) + 2(0) = -910.7\ \text{kJ}
   $$

4. **Products minus reactants.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta H^\circ &= -221.0 - (-910.7) \\
   &= +689.7\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Subtracting a negative number adds it.

5. **Does it make sense?** The answer is positive: the reaction takes energy in, which is what the electricity is for. One mole of silicon is 28.09 g, so a kilogram is this many moles:

   $$
   \frac{1{,}000\ \text{g}}{28.09\ \text{g/mol}} = 35.60\ \text{mol}
   $$

   Making it takes in this much energy (topic 6.6), with 689.7 kJ for each mole of silicon:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   35.60 \times 689.7 &= 2.46 \times 10^{4}\ \text{kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The real furnace uses more, because it also has to heat everything to about 2,000 °C.

## Signs, states and amounts

Three things to watch.

**Signs.** A table value can be positive, and a positive value subtracted and a negative value subtracted behave differently. Keep every value in parentheses with its own sign until the last line.

**States.** The state in the equation picks the value. H₂O(l) is −285.8 kJ/mol and H₂O(g) is −241.8 kJ/mol. The 44.0 kJ/mol between them is the energy that turns a mole of liquid water into vapor at 25 °C, a little more than the 41 kJ it takes at 100 °C (topic 3.1).

**Amounts.** $\Delta H^\circ$ is for the equation as written. For any other amount, scale it by the moles that react (topic 6.6).

<Worked title="The gas in a carbide lamp">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CaC2(s) + 2H2O(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Ca(OH)2(s) + C2H2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

(a) Find $\Delta H^\circ$. (b) How much energy is released when a 25.0 g lump of calcium carbide is used up?

1. **Values, with the right state for water.** All are in kJ/mol, from the table.

   - CaC₂(s): −59.8
   - H₂O(l): −285.8, the liquid
   - Ca(OH)₂(s): −985.2
   - C₂H₂(g): +227.4

2. **Products.**

   $$
   1(-985.2) + 1(+227.4) = -757.8\ \text{kJ}
   $$

3. **Reactants.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &1(-59.8) + 2(-285.8) \\
   &\quad = -59.8 - 571.6 \\
   &\quad = -631.4\ \text{kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Products minus reactants.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta H^\circ &= -757.8 - (-631.4) \\
   &= -126.4\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The gas-making reaction is exothermic: a carbide lamp's body gets warm.

5. **Scale to the lump.** The molar mass of CaC₂ is 40.08 + 2(12.01) = 64.10 g/mol.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   n &= \frac{25.0\ \text{g}}{64.10\ \text{g/mol}} \\
   &= 0.3900\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The equation uses 1 mol of CaC₂ for each mole of reaction, so the energy released, in kJ, is this:

   $$
   0.3900 \times 126.4 = 49.3\ \text{kJ}
   $$

6. **What the slips would give.** The value for steam in place of liquid water gives −214.4 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$. Leaving out the coefficient 2 gives −412.2 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$. Reactants minus products gives +126.4 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$, an endothermic answer for a lamp that warms up.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Reactants minus products.** The order here is products minus reactants, the opposite of the bond-enthalpy order. An exothermic reaction that comes out positive is the warning.
- **Leaving out a coefficient.** The equation sheet's formula does not show them. Multiply every value by its coefficient.
- **Giving an element a value, or dropping it.** An element in its standard state goes in as zero. Write the zero.
- **Calling every form of an element zero.** Br₂(g), I₂(g) and H(g) are elements and are not zero.
- **Taking the value for the wrong state.** H₂O(l), −285.8, and H₂O(g), −241.8, are different numbers.
- **Losing a sign.** Subtracting a negative value adds it. Keep each value in parentheses.
- **Writing a formation reaction for two moles.** The product is one mole of the compound, so fractions are allowed on the elements.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The standard enthalpy of formation of a compound, $\Delta H_f^\circ$, is the enthalpy change when one mole of it forms from its elements in their standard states.
- The standard state is the pure substance at 1 bar. For an element it is normally the form most stable at 25 °C and 1 bar, such as O₂(g), Br₂(l) and graphite.
- $\Delta H_f^\circ$ of an element in its standard state is zero. Other forms of the element, such as Br₂(g), are not zero.
- The standard enthalpy change of a reaction is the sum of $n\,\Delta H_f^\circ$ for the products minus the sum of $n\,\Delta H_f^\circ$ for the reactants, where $n$ is each coefficient. The equation sheet prints this without the coefficients.
- The value used must match the state in the equation. Liquid water and steam have different values.
- The answer belongs to the equation as written, in $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$, and scales with the amount that reacts.

</KeyPoints>
