Hold a small piece of lithium metal in a burner flame and it catches fire. It burns with a fierce white light edged in crimson and leaves a white powder, lithium oxide. The burner only started it: take the flame away and the lithium goes on burning. The energy comes out of the reaction itself, and a chemist can say exactly how much comes out for each gram of lithium burned. This lesson is about the number that makes that possible.

## Enthalpy change: heat at constant pressure

Most reactions you meet are run open to the air: in a flask, a beaker, a foam cup, a burner flame. The pressure on them is the pressure of the atmosphere, and it does not change while they react. The heat a reaction releases or absorbs under that condition has its own name and symbol: the **enthalpy change** of the reaction, $\Delta H$.

The sign follows the rule of topic 6.1, taken from the side of the reacting substances, which are the system. If the reaction releases heat, $\Delta H$ is negative and the reaction is exothermic. If it absorbs heat, $\Delta H$ is positive and the reaction is endothermic.

Here is the burning lithium:

$$
\ce{4Li(s) + O2(g) -> 2Li2O(s)}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -1{,}195.8\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

The minus sign says that energy leaves the reacting lithium and oxygen: 1,195.8 kJ of it. The next section explains the unit.

**Beyond the exam:** enthalpy is not quite the same thing as the energy of the system, because a reaction that makes or uses up gas also pushes on the atmosphere, but the exam does not ask about the difference.

<KeyIdea>

The enthalpy change of a reaction, $\Delta H$, is the heat it releases or absorbs at constant pressure. Negative means released; positive means absorbed.

</KeyIdea>

## ΔH belongs to the equation

The 1,195.8 kJ is not "per mole of lithium" and not "per mole of oxygen". It goes with the equation as it is written, read in moles: 4 mol of lithium reacting with 1 mol of oxygen to make 2 mol of lithium oxide. One such run of the equation is called a **mole of reaction**, and the unit $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ means kilojoules for each mole of reaction.

So the equation hands you ratios, just as it does for amounts of substance (topic 4.5):

- 1,195.8 kJ released for every 4 mol of Li that burn
- 1,195.8 kJ released for every 1 mol of O₂ used
- 1,195.8 kJ released for every 2 mol of Li₂O formed

To find the energy for any amount, find the moles of reaction, then multiply:

$$
q = n_\text{rxn} \times \Delta H
$$

To get the moles of reaction, divide the moles of a substance by its coefficient in the equation.

Because the value goes with the equation as written, the same reaction written another way has another value:

$$
\ce{2Li(s) + 1/2 O2(g) -> Li2O(s)}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -597.9\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

Half the amounts, half the energy. Topic 6.9 uses this. The state symbols are part of the equation too, and the same reaction written with a substance in a different state has a different $\Delta H$.

Tables and exam questions often shorten $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ to kJ/mol, or give $\Delta H$ beside an equation in kJ alone. In each case read it as "for the equation as written".

<Worked title="Energy from a sliver of lithium">

The piece of lithium in the flame has a mass of 0.694 g, and all of it burns to lithium oxide. How much energy is released?

$$
\ce{4Li(s) + O2(g) -> 2Li2O(s)}
$$

$$
\Delta H = -1{,}195.8\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
$$

1. **Plan the route.** Grams of lithium, to moles of lithium, to moles of reaction, to kilojoules. It is the route of topic 4.5 with energy at the end in place of a product.

2. **Grams to moles.** The molar mass of lithium is 6.94 g/mol.

   $$
   n_{\ce{Li}} = \frac{0.694}{6.94} = 0.100\ \text{mol}
   $$

3. **Moles of lithium to moles of reaction.** The equation uses 4 mol of Li each time it runs.

   $$
   n_\text{rxn} = \frac{0.100}{4} = 0.0250\ \text{mol}
   $$

4. **Multiply by $\Delta H$.** The moles of reaction times the kilojoules for each mole of reaction:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   q &= (0.0250)(-1{,}195.8) \\
   &= -29.9\ \text{kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Say it in words, and test it.** The burning lithium releases 29.9 kJ. That is sensible: 0.694 g is far less than the 4 mol (27.76 g) the equation is written for, so the answer should be far less than 1,195.8 kJ. Forgetting to divide by 4 would have given 120 kJ, four times too much.

</Worked>

## Where the energy comes from

No energy is created in a reaction (the first law, topic 6.4). So where do the 1,195.8 kJ come from? They were stored in the reactants as chemical potential energy: the energy that atoms and ions have because of how they are bonded and attracted to one another.

In a reaction some bonds and attractions are broken and new ones are formed, so the products have a different chemical potential energy from the reactants. Breaking takes energy in and forming gives energy out (topic 2.2), and the two rarely balance. Topic 6.7 does that accounting with numbers; here only the outcome matters.

In an exothermic reaction the products have less chemical potential energy than the reactants. The difference does not vanish: it becomes kinetic energy of the particles, which move faster. Faster particles on average mean a higher temperature (topic 6.3). That is the warming you measure.

In an endothermic reaction the products have more chemical potential energy than the reactants. The difference is taken from the kinetic energy of the particles around them, which slow down, and the temperature falls.

On an energy diagram (topic 6.2) the two levels are these two potential energies. $\Delta H$ is the gap between them, products minus reactants.

## Measuring ΔH in a calorimeter

Run a reaction in solution inside a coffee-cup calorimeter (topic 6.4), and the solution around the reacting particles takes up the energy an exothermic reaction releases, or supplies the energy an endothermic one absorbs. The reacting substances are the system. The rest of the solution is the part of the surroundings whose temperature you can measure.

$\Delta H$ is an energy divided by an amount, so two separate things have to be found, and they come from different measurements.

**The energy, from the thermometer.**

- The solution's temperature change and $q = mc\Delta T$, with the mass of the whole solution, give the energy the solution gained or lost.
- The reaction's energy is the same size with the opposite sign:

  $$
  q_\text{reaction} = -q_\text{solution}
  $$

**The amount, from what was mixed.**

- For each reactant, divide its moles by its coefficient in the equation.
- The smaller result is the moles of reaction that took place, because that reactant is the limiting one (topic 4.5).

**Then divide.** The answer is in $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$.

$$
\Delta H = \frac{q_\text{reaction}}{n_\text{rxn}}
$$

The two halves can be done in either order, and neither needs the other. An answer that stops at the energy is the result of one experiment; only the division makes it a property of the reaction.

<Worked title="From a temperature rise to ΔH">

A student adds 1.00 g of copper powder to 50.0 mL of 0.400 M silver nitrate solution in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The temperature rises from 21.30 °C to 28.10 °C. Take the mass of the solution as 50.0 g and its specific heat capacity as 4.18 J/(g·°C). Find $\Delta H$ for the reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The nitrate ions are spectators, so this is the net ionic equation (topic 4.2).

1. **The amount: moles of reaction from each reactant.** Silver ions first, as liters times moles per liter:

   $$
   (0.0500)(0.400) = 0.0200\ \text{mol}
   $$

   The equation uses 2 mol of Ag⁺ each time it runs, so the silver ions are enough for 0.0100 mol of reaction. Now the copper, whose molar mass is 63.55 g/mol:

   $$
   \frac{1.00}{63.55} = 0.0157\ \text{mol}
   $$

   Its coefficient is 1, so the copper is enough for 0.0157 mol of reaction. The silver ions run out first, so 0.0100 mol of reaction takes place.

2. **The energy: the solution first.** The temperature change is final minus initial: 28.10 − 21.30 = 6.80 °C. With the mass in grams and the answer in joules:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   q_\text{solution} &= (50.0)(4.18)(6.80) \\
   &= 1{,}421\ \text{J}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The solution gained 1,421 J.

3. **The energy: now the reaction.** The reaction released what the solution gained. (Keep the extra figure until the end.)

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   q_\text{reaction} &= -1{,}421\ \text{J} \\
   &= -1.421\ \text{kJ}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Divide.** Kilojoules over moles of reaction:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta H &= \frac{-1.421}{0.0100} \\
   &= -142\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Check it.** The mixture warmed, so the reaction is exothermic and $\Delta H$ must be negative. It is. Dividing by the 0.0200 mol of silver ions would have given −71.1 kJ, the energy for each mole of silver ions, which is not what the equation as written asks for. A foam cup's answer comes out a little small in size, because a little of the energy warms the cup, the thermometer and the metal, and a little escapes.

</Worked>

## Afterward: back to thermal equilibrium

Right after an exothermic reaction, the products are hotter than their surroundings. That cannot last. Thermal energy passes from the hot products to the cooler surroundings until everything is at one temperature: thermal equilibrium (topic 6.3).

This is why the lithium oxide powder is back at room temperature a few minutes after the flame, though the reaction released 29.9 kJ: that energy is now spread through the air of the room. It is also why a calorimeter measurement is made at once, in an insulated cup: the solution catches the energy before it can move on to the room.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Stopping at the energy of one experiment.** That energy is not $\Delta H$ until it is divided by the moles of reaction, and the unit of the answer is then kJ/mol.
- **Leaving out the sign change.** The solution's $q$ and the reaction's $q$ are equal in size and opposite in sign: a solution that warms means a negative $\Delta H$.
- **Reading $\Delta H$ as "per mole of" whichever substance is in the question.** It is for the equation as written. Divide the moles of a substance by its coefficient.
- **Dividing by the moles of the reactant in excess.** The limiting reactant sets how much reaction took place.
- **Saying the energy comes from breaking bonds.** Breaking takes energy in. An exothermic reaction releases energy because the products have less chemical potential energy than the reactants.
- **Thinking the products stay hot or cold.** They exchange thermal energy with the surroundings until thermal equilibrium.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The enthalpy change of a reaction, $\Delta H$, is the heat released or absorbed at constant pressure. It is negative when the reaction releases energy and positive when it absorbs energy.
- $\Delta H$ belongs to the equation as written, in kJ for each mole of reaction. The energy for any amount is the moles of reaction times $\Delta H$, and the moles of reaction are the moles of a substance divided by its coefficient.
- In a calorimeter, $q = mc\Delta T$ for the solution gives the size of the reaction's energy and the opposite of its sign. That energy divided by the moles of reaction is $\Delta H$.
- The energy of a reaction comes from a difference in chemical potential energy between reactants and products, made by the bonds broken and formed.
- That difference shows up as a change in the kinetic energy of the particles, which is the temperature change you measure.
- After a reaction the products exchange thermal energy with their surroundings until thermal equilibrium, giving energy out after an exothermic reaction and taking it in after an endothermic one.

</KeyPoints>
