Pour 200 g of water into one small pan and 200 g of olive oil into another just like it, and set them on two burners turned to the same setting. A minute later the oil has climbed about twice as far above room temperature as the water. Both pans took in the same energy. This lesson is about why the same energy does such different things to different substances, and how chemists turn a thermometer reading into an amount of energy.

## How much energy for one degree

Warming something takes energy, and how much depends on three things: how much of it there is, how far its temperature must rise, and what it is made of. The last of these has a name. The **specific heat capacity**, $c$, of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of it by 1 °C. Its unit is J/(g·°C).

These are the values this lesson uses:

| Substance | $c$ in J/(g·°C) |
|---|---|
| Water, liquid | 4.18 |
| Olive oil | about 2.0 |
| Sodium chloride | 0.864 |
| Titanium | 0.523 |
| Tin | 0.228 |
| Tungsten | 0.132 |

Water's value is unusually large. A gram of water takes more than thirty times the energy a gram of tungsten takes for the same one-degree rise.

The three things multiply:

$$
q = mc\Delta T
$$

Here $q$ is the heat in joules, $m$ is the mass in grams, $c$ is the specific heat capacity and $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature. This equation is on the exam's equation sheet. The value of $c$ for water is not: a question gives it.

$\Delta T$ is always the final temperature minus the starting temperature:

$$
\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}
$$

So a substance that warms has a positive $\Delta T$ and a positive $q$: it gained energy. A substance that cools has a negative $\Delta T$ and a negative $q$: it lost energy. The $q$ you calculate belongs to the substance whose $m$, $c$ and $\Delta T$ you put in. This is topic 6.1's rule for signs, seen from the side of whatever you chose as the system.

A change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K, so $\Delta T$ is the same number in either unit, and this unit's lessons leave temperatures in °C.

One caution: $c$ changes a little with temperature, and these problems treat it as constant over the range.

<KeyIdea>

$q = mc\Delta T$ gives the energy a substance gains or loses when its temperature changes and its phase does not. $\Delta T$ is final minus initial, so the sign of $q$ comes out by itself: positive for energy gained, negative for energy lost.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Heating a salt block">

Some cooks sear food on a thick slab of pink rock salt that has been heated in an oven. Treat the slab as pure sodium chloride, with $c = 0.864$ J/(g·°C). How much energy does a 2.50 kg slab take in as it warms from 22 °C to 205 °C?

1. **List what is known, in the units of $c$.** The specific heat capacity is in J/(g·°C), so the mass must be in grams: 2.50 kg = 2,500 g.

2. **Find $\Delta T$, final minus initial.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta T &= 205\ \text{°C} - 22\ \text{°C} \\
   &= 183\ \text{°C}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Substitute.** With the mass in grams, $c$ in J/(g·°C) and $\Delta T$ in °C, the answer comes out in joules.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   q &= (2{,}500)(0.864)(183) \\
   &= 395{,}280\ \text{J}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Report it sensibly.** 395,280 J is 395 kJ to three figures. It is positive, as it should be: the slab gained energy.

</Worked>

## The same energy, a different rise

Turn the equation around:

$$
\Delta T = \frac{q}{mc}
$$

For a fixed energy and a fixed mass, a larger $c$ means a smaller temperature change. Equal energy does not mean equal warming.

In the figure, 900 J goes into 40.0 g of water and into 40.0 g of titanium, both at 20.0 °C. The water rises 5.4 °C, to 25.4 °C. The titanium rises 43.0 °C, to 63.0 °C: eight times as far, because its specific heat capacity is one-eighth of water's.

**Counting in moles.** Chemists often count in moles, not grams. The **molar heat capacity** of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 mole of it by 1 °C, in J/(mol·°C). It is the specific heat capacity times the molar mass, and it is used the same way, with moles in place of grams:

$$
q = nC\Delta T
$$

Here $n$ is the amount in moles and $C$ is the molar heat capacity. Liquid ethanol has $C = 112$ J/(mol·°C), so warming 0.250 mol of it by 12.0 °C takes (0.250)(112)(12.0) = 336 J. Both forms appear on the exam. Look at the unit of the heat capacity you are given to see whether it wants grams or moles.

| Substance | $c$ in J/(g·°C) | $C$ in J/(mol·°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 4.18 | 75.3 |
| Titanium | 0.523 | 25.0 |
| Tin | 0.228 | 27.1 |
| Tungsten | 0.132 | 24.3 |

Per gram, titanium needs about four times the energy tungsten needs. Per mole the three metals are nearly alike. A gram of tungsten simply holds about a quarter as many atoms as a gram of titanium, because each tungsten atom is about four times as heavy.

## Energy lost, energy gained

Put a hot object in contact with a cold one, and energy passes from the hot one to the cold one until both are at one temperature: thermal equilibrium. Topic 6.3 explains this with collisions between particles.

The **first law of thermodynamics** says that energy is conserved: it is never made or destroyed in a chemical or physical process, only moved or changed in form. So if no energy escapes to anything else, every joule the hot object loses is a joule the cold object gains.

$$
q_\text{lost} = -q_\text{gained}
$$

The two heats are equal in size and opposite in sign, so they add to zero. This line is not on the equation sheet. It is the first law, written for two objects.

What is equal is the energy. The temperature changes are usually not equal. Each object's change is its own $q/(mc)$, so the object with the larger $m \times c$ changes less.

<KeyIdea>

When a hot object and a cold one exchange energy only with each other, the energy one loses equals the energy the other gains. The energies match. The temperature changes do not have to.

</KeyIdea>

## The coffee-cup calorimeter

**Calorimetry** is the measurement of energy transferred as heat, made by watching a temperature change. The container it is done in is a **calorimeter**. The simplest is two foam cups, one inside the other, with a lid, a thermometer and a stirrer.

<Figure
  src="/figures/6-4-coffee-cup-calorimeter.svg"
  alt="A cross-section of a coffee-cup calorimeter with six labeled parts: a thermometer and a stirrer pass through a lid into water held in two foam cups, one inside the other, and a small solid sample rests on the bottom."
  caption="A coffee-cup calorimeter. The foam keeps nearly all the energy inside the cup for the short time a measurement takes."
/>

Foam is a poor conductor, so very little energy passes between the cup's contents and the room during the minute or two a measurement takes. The contents exchange energy with one another only, which is the condition for $q_\text{lost} = -q_\text{gained}$. The cup is open to the air through the lid's holes, so whatever happens in it happens at the pressure of the room. Topic 6.6 needs that.

Every calculation with this cup rests on two assumptions. No energy escapes from the cup. And the cup, lid and thermometer themselves take in so little energy that it is ignored, unless a question gives a number for it.

For a solid put into water, the method has three steps:

- Measure the water's temperature change and compute $q$ for the water.
- The solid's $q$ is the same size with the opposite sign.
- From the solid's $q$, its mass and its own temperature change, find whatever is asked.

<Worked title="Cold ceramic in warm water">

A lab technician checks the specific heat capacity of alumina, Al₂O₃, the hard white ceramic that some laboratory crucibles are made of. She keeps 85.0 g of alumina beads in a freezer at −18.00 °C, then tips them at once into 150.0 g of water at 24.00 °C in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The water is stirred, and the temperature settles at 20.01 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 J/(g·°C). What specific heat capacity does the experiment give for alumina?

1. **Start with the water, because everything about it is known.** Its temperature change is final minus initial.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta T &= 20.01 - 24.00 \\
   &= -3.99\ \text{°C}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Then $q$ for the water, in joules:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   q &= (150.0)(4.18)(-3.99) \\
   &= -2{,}502\ \text{J}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The water lost 2,502 J.

2. **Use conservation of energy.** The beads gained what the water lost.

   $$
   q_\text{beads} = +2{,}502\ \text{J}
   $$

3. **Find the beads' own temperature change.** They end at the same 20.01 °C as the water.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta T &= 20.01 - (-18.00) \\
   &= +38.01\ \text{°C}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Solve $q = mc\Delta T$ for $c$.** Use the beads' own $q$, mass and $\Delta T$.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   c &= \frac{q}{m\,\Delta T} \\
   &= \frac{2{,}502}{(85.0)(38.01)} \\
   &= 0.774\ \text{J/(g·°C)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Check it.** A specific heat capacity must be positive, and this one is: the beads' $q$ and their $\Delta T$ are both positive. It is also a sensible size: handbooks list about 0.78 J/(g·°C) for alumina at room temperature. Notice the two temperature changes: about 4 °C for the water and about 38 °C for the beads, for the same 2,502 J.

</Worked>

## When something dissolves

A coffee-cup calorimeter also measures the energy change when a solid dissolves. Now there is no second object: the solid ends up spread through the water, and the thing whose temperature changes is the whole solution.

If the solution warms, it gained energy, so the dissolving released energy: it is exothermic (topic 6.1). If the solution cools, it lost energy, so the dissolving absorbed energy: it is endothermic.

The size comes from the same equation, used for the solution:

$$
q_\text{solution} = mc\Delta T
$$

Here $m$ is the mass of everything in the cup that changed temperature: the water plus the dissolved solid. For a dilute solution, $c$ is taken as water's, 4.18 J/(g·°C), unless the question gives another value. Then reverse the sign:

$$
q_\text{dissolving} = -q_\text{solution}
$$

Topic 6.6 takes the next step, from this energy to an energy per mole.

**When the cup is counted too.** A real cup, lid and thermometer take in or give up a little energy as well. Some questions give a number for it. The **calorimeter constant** is the heat capacity of the calorimeter itself: the energy needed to change its temperature by 1 °C, in J/°C. The calorimeter changes by the same $\Delta T$ as its contents, so its share is

$$
q_\text{calorimeter} = C_\text{cal}\,\Delta T
$$

and it is added to the solution's $q$ before the sign is reversed. If a question gives no calorimeter constant, leave it out.

A chemical system changes its energy in three main ways: by being heated or cooled, by changing phase, and by reacting. This lesson measured the first; topic 6.5 takes the second and topic 6.6 the third, and the same cup measures all three.

**Beyond the exam:** fuels are burned in a sealed steel vessel called a bomb calorimeter, which works at constant volume. The exam's calorimeter is the open cup.

<Trap>

Six mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Pairing one substance's mass with another's $c$ or $\Delta T$.** Each $q = mc\Delta T$ is about one substance: its mass, its specific heat capacity, its own temperature change.
- **Taking $\Delta T$ as the bigger number minus the smaller.** It is final minus initial, and its sign carries the direction of the energy.
- **Expecting equal temperature changes.** The energies are equal in size. Each temperature change depends on that object's $m \times c$.
- **Leaving the dissolved solid out of the solution's mass.** Everything that changed temperature counts.
- **Reading a rising thermometer as "absorbed".** If the solution warmed, the dissolving released the energy the solution gained.
- **Giving joules where kilojoules were asked for,** or the reverse.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The specific heat capacity $c$ is the energy that raises the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C. The molar heat capacity $C$ is the same for 1 mol.
- $q = mc\Delta T$ (or $q = nC\Delta T$) gives the heat a substance gains or loses when only its temperature changes. $\Delta T$ is final minus initial, so $q$ is positive for energy gained and negative for energy lost.
- For equal masses, the same energy gives a smaller temperature change to the substance with the larger specific heat capacity.
- Energy is conserved (the first law of thermodynamics), so when two objects exchange energy only with each other, $q_\text{lost} = -q_\text{gained}$.
- A coffee-cup calorimeter keeps the exchange inside the cup, at constant pressure, and the water's or the solution's $q$ gives the size of the energy transferred.
- When a solid dissolves, a solution that warms shows the dissolving released energy, and a solution that cools shows it absorbed energy. The mass in $q = mc\Delta T$ is the mass of the whole solution.

</KeyPoints>
