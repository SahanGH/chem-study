A soldier's field ration comes with a heater that needs no flame. Pour a little water into the pouch, and the magnesium powder inside reacts with it. In about ten minutes the meal is steaming. That energy was in the magnesium and the water all along: together they held more energy than the magnesium hydroxide and hydrogen they turned into. An energy diagram is a drawing of exactly that: where the system's energy started, where it ended, and the drop between.

## Two levels and an arrow

An **energy diagram** shows the energy of a system before and after a change. Up the side is energy. There is one horizontal line, called a **level**, for the system at the start, and one for the system at the end. An arrow runs from the starting level to the final level.

Exam diagrams label the vertical axis "energy", "potential energy" or "enthalpy". Read them all the same way.

Reading the diagram uses the two words of topic 6.1. If the final level is lower, the arrow points down. The system ended with less energy than it started with, the difference went to the surroundings, and the change is exothermic. If the final level is higher, the arrow points up. The system took energy in from the surroundings, and the change is endothermic.

The start and the end are labeled with what the system is at each: "reactants" and "products" for a reaction, or the formulas with their state symbols.

The information is in the two levels, the gap between them and the arrow. Left to right there is no scale. Many diagrams draw the starting level on the left and the final level on the right, with "reaction progress" along the bottom, as the profiles of Unit 5 do.

<KeyIdea>

On an energy diagram, follow the arrow from the starting level to the final level. Down means the system released energy: exothermic. Up means the system absorbed energy: endothermic. The length of the arrow is the amount of energy.

</KeyIdea>

- A rise, because the products have 80 kJ more energy to pass on to the water.

- A fall, because the system takes the energy it gains from the water.
- No change, because energy is conserved and the two levels cancel out.

<div slot="why">The products' level is higher, so the arrow points up: the system ends with 80 kJ more energy than it started with, and the reaction is endothermic. That energy has to come from the surroundings, and here the surroundings are the water (topic 6.1). The water loses energy, so its temperature falls. A gap is not always energy released: its direction says whether energy went out or came in. And conservation means the water's loss equals the system's gain, not that nothing changes.</div>

## Putting numbers on it

When the axis has numbers, the gap between the levels is the amount of energy the system released or absorbed. The system's energy change is the final level minus the starting level. For a drop the result is negative, which is the sign topic 6.1 gave an exothermic change. For a rise it is positive.

This is the same subtraction as topic 5.6's overall energy change, $\Delta E$: products minus reactants. Topic 5.6 noted that the exam often labels that gap $\Delta H$, and topic 6.6 says what $\Delta H$ is.

Only the gap matters. Where zero sits on the axis is a free choice, and it is often convenient to put the starting level at zero, as topic 5.10 did.

The amount of energy depends on how much reacts, so a diagram with numbers is drawn for a stated amount, here one mole.

<Worked title="Drawing the diagram for a ration heater">

In a flameless ration heater, magnesium reacts with water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For each mole of magnesium that reacts, the system releases 353 kJ. Draw the energy diagram for one mole of magnesium, and give the system's energy change.

1. **Name the start and the end.** Start: Mg(s) and 2 H₂O(l), the reactants. End: Mg(OH)₂(s) and H₂(g), the products.

2. **Decide which level is higher.** The system releases energy, so it ends with less than it started with. The reactants' level is the higher one.

3. **Draw the axis and the two levels.** Draw a vertical axis labeled "energy (kJ)". Put the reactants' level at 0 and label it Mg(s) + 2 H₂O(l). The products are 353 kJ lower, so their level is at −353 kJ. Label it Mg(OH)₂(s) + H₂(g).

4. **Draw the arrow from start to finish.** It points down, from 0 to −353 kJ. Label it "353 kJ released".

5. **State the energy change.** Final minus starting:

   $$
   -353\ \text{kJ} - 0\ \text{kJ} = -353\ \text{kJ}
   $$

   The sign is negative, and the arrow points down: the reaction is exothermic.

Energy released this way is what heats the meal. The diagram says how much energy there is to be had. It does not say how fast it comes, or what gets the reaction going: the last section of this lesson comes back to that.

</Worked>

## A physical change gets the same diagram

Nothing about the diagram needs a reaction. A change of phase has a start and an end too.

Dry ice is solid carbon dioxide. At −78.5 °C it turns straight from solid to gas, a change called **sublimation**, and it absorbs 25.2 kJ for each mole as it goes. That is where a dry-ice fog machine gets its chill: the energy comes out of the water and the air around the dry ice. On the diagram, CO₂(s) is the lower level, CO₂(g) is the upper level, and the arrow points up: endothermic.

Run the change backward and you get the same two levels with the arrow reversed. When carbon dioxide gas turns to solid, the reverse, called deposition, it releases the same 25.2 kJ per mole. The gap belongs to the pair of levels. The direction of travel decides whether it is absorbed or released. Topic 6.5 uses this for melting and boiling.

## The same picture as Unit 5, without the hill

You have drawn something like this before. The energy profile of topic 5.6 starts at the reactants' level and ends at the products' level, and those are the same two levels as on an energy diagram. The profile adds the path between them: the climb to the transition state. At a given temperature, the height of that climb, the activation energy, decides how fast the reaction goes. All of that is Unit 5's business, and nothing about it changes here.

An energy diagram leaves the path out, and can, because the system's energy change does not depend on it. Topic 5.10 found the overall energy change from the first reactants and the final products, however many steps lay between.

So to turn a profile into an energy diagram, keep the first level and the last level and drop everything between. To read exothermic or endothermic from a profile, do the same with your eyes: compare the two ends. The peak has no say in it.

- Levels at 25 and 140 kJ/mol, with the arrow pointing up: 115 kJ/mol absorbed
- Levels at 140 and 70 kJ/mol, with the arrow pointing down: 70 kJ/mol released
- Levels at 25 and 70 kJ/mol, with the arrow pointing down: 45 kJ/mol released
- Levels at 25 and 70 kJ/mol, with the arrow pointing up: 45 kJ/mol absorbed

<div slot="why">

An energy diagram keeps the two ends of the profile: the reactants at 25 kJ/mol and the products at 70 kJ/mol. The arrow runs from the start to the finish, from 25 up to 70, so it points up. The energy change is:

$$
70 - 25 = +45\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

That is endothermic. The first option measures to the peak:

$$
140 - 25 = 115\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

That is the activation energy of topic 5.6, not the energy change. The second starts from the peak:

$$
140 - 70 = 70\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

That is the activation energy of the reverse reaction. The third has the right levels with the arrow run backward.

</div>
</Check>

The other way around, an energy diagram cannot tell you what a profile can. Two levels say nothing about the hill, so they say nothing about how fast the change goes or whether it needs a push to start. A match sits in its box for years until it is struck.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Reading the arrow backward.** It runs from the start to the finish. Products below reactants means energy was released.
- **Judging exothermic or endothermic by the height of the hill.** That comes from where the profile starts and where it ends. The hill matters for speed (topic 5.6).
- **Dropping the sign.** The energy change is final minus starting, so a drop is negative.
- **Thinking a lower level means a lower temperature.** The levels are the system's energy. An exothermic change warms the surroundings.
- **Putting both phases on one level because the temperature did not change.** A phase change moves energy at a steady temperature.
- **Taking a big drop for a fast reaction.** The drop tells how much energy moves. How fast the reaction goes depends on the hill, which two levels leave out.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- An energy diagram shows the system's energy at the start and at the end of a change, with an arrow from the starting level to the final level.
- An arrow pointing down means the system released energy and the change is exothermic. An arrow pointing up means it absorbed energy and the change is endothermic.
- The system's energy change is the final level minus the starting level: negative for a drop and positive for a rise.
- A physical change such as a change of phase gets the same kind of diagram. Reversing a change keeps the gap and reverses the arrow.
- An energy diagram is an energy profile with only its two ends kept, so it shows how much energy and which way but nothing about how fast.

</KeyPoints>
