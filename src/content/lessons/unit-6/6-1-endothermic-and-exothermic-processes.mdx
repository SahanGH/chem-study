Squeeze the two parts of an epoxy glue into a paper cup and stir. Within a few minutes the cup is hot in your hand, and nobody heated it. An athletic trainer sprays ethyl chloride from a can onto a twisted ankle. The liquid is gone in seconds and the skin is left icy cold, though the can came from a bag at room temperature. In both, energy moved. This lesson is about saying where it went, and getting the direction right.

## System and surroundings

To follow energy, you first draw a line around the thing you are studying. The **system** is the thing whose energy you are tracking: here, the particles that are changing. The **surroundings** are everything else.

Try it on the two scenes.

- **The epoxy.** The system is the resin and hardener molecules that are reacting, and the hard plastic they become. The surroundings are the cup, the stirring stick, the air and your hand.
- **The spray.** The system is the ethyl chloride. The surroundings are the skin and the air.

One case comes up again and again. When substances react while dissolved, the system is the particles that react and what they turn into. All the rest of the setup is surroundings, the water above all, because its temperature is what you read.

Energy is never made or destroyed in a change. So whatever energy the system loses, the surroundings gain, and the other way around. Topic 6.4 turns this into calculations.

## A temperature change is the evidence

Topic 3.5 said what temperature measures: the average kinetic energy of a sample's particles. So when the temperature of something changes, its energy is changing. A thermometer is how you see energy move.

The cup got warmer, so the particles of the cup gained kinetic energy. That energy came from somewhere, and the one thing that was changing was the epoxy. The skin got colder, so its particles lost kinetic energy. That energy went somewhere, and the one thing that was changing was the ethyl chloride, which evaporated.

Two words name the two directions. A change is **exothermic** when the system releases energy to the surroundings: the system's energy falls. A change is **endothermic** when the system absorbs energy from the surroundings: the system's energy rises. The epoxy curing is exothermic. The ethyl chloride evaporating is endothermic.

Topic 5.6 used these words for one step of a reaction, and topic 7.9 used them for heating an equilibrium. Here they get their full meaning. A change can also leave the energy of the system the same, and then it is neither.

The sign follows the system. The system's energy change is negative when the change is exothermic, because the system ends with less. It is positive when the change is endothermic, because the system ends with more. Topic 5.6 used the same convention for the overall energy change of a reaction. In words, never write "negative energy released". Say "the system releases energy".

## Three kinds of change

Three kinds of change can be exothermic or endothermic.

| Kind of change | Exothermic: the system loses energy | Endothermic: the system gains energy |
|---|---|---|
| Cooling or heating a substance | A cast-iron skillet cooling on the stove | A pizza stone heating in an oven |
| Phase change | Freezing, condensing | Melting, vaporizing |
| Chemical reaction | Epoxy curing | Citric acid reacting with baking soda |

For cooling and heating, the system is the substance itself, and its own temperature shows the change.

For a phase change, the direction follows from the attractions between the particles (topics 3.1 and 3.3). To melt a solid or vaporize a liquid, particles have to be pulled away from their neighbors, and that takes energy in. Going the other way, particles settle back into those attractions and the energy is released. A solid that turns straight into a gas, and a gas that turns straight into a solid, follow the same rule. So ethyl chloride evaporating off the skin takes energy from the skin. Topic 6.5 puts numbers on phase changes.

For a reaction, the direction cannot be read off so easily. The energy a reaction releases comes out of the way the atoms are bonded, and the energy it absorbs goes into that bonding (topic 2.2). Topics 6.6 and 6.7 follow that energy. Until then you find the direction by experiment: by watching what happens to the temperature of the surroundings.

## Whose temperature does the thermometer show?

When a reaction runs in water, the thermometer sits in the water, and the water is surroundings. So the temperature you read goes the opposite way from the system's energy.

<Worked title="Reading a thermometer the right way">

A bath bomb is mostly citric acid and baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate), pressed together dry. A student crumbles a piece of one into a cup of water with a thermometer in it. Bubbles stream up through the water, and the reading falls from 21.5 °C to 17.0 °C. Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic, and what is the sign of the system's energy change?

1. **Name the system.** The particles that react: the dissolved citric acid and baking soda, and the substances they turn into. One of those is the carbon dioxide in the bubbles.

2. **Name the surroundings.** Everything else: the water, the cup, the thermometer and the air.

3. **Say whose temperature changed.** The thermometer is in the water, so the reading belongs to the surroundings. It fell by 4.5 °C.

   $$
   21.5\ \text{°C} - 17.0\ \text{°C} = 4.5\ \text{°C}
   $$

4. **Turn the temperature change into an energy change.** A lower temperature means a lower average kinetic energy (topic 3.5). The water lost energy.

5. **Follow the energy.** Energy is not destroyed, and nothing in the cup is changing except the bath bomb's substances. The energy the water lost went into the system.

6. **Write the claim.** "The reaction is endothermic. The temperature of the water, which is part of the surroundings, fell, so the surroundings lost energy. The system gained that energy, so the system's energy change is positive."

The reasoning has the same six moves every time, and only the direction changes.

</Worked>

<KeyIdea>

Exothermic and endothermic describe the system. An exothermic change lowers the system's energy and warms the surroundings. An endothermic change raises the system's energy and cools the surroundings. Before you read any temperature, ask whether it belongs to the system or to the surroundings.

</KeyIdea>

A system and its surroundings can trade energy in two ways. One is **heat**: energy passed from a warmer body to a cooler one, which topic 6.3 explains with particles. The other is **work**: a push that moves something. A reaction that makes a gas in an open cup has to push the air back to make room for the gas. That push carries energy from the system to the surroundings without warming anything on the way. Work can also be done on a system, which adds to its energy. In this unit you will calculate with heat. For work, know that it is the other way energy can leave or enter.

## Dissolving can go either way

A soap maker stirs solid sodium hydroxide into water, and the solution gets hot enough to scald. A sugar-free mint sweetened with xylitol feels cool on the tongue as it dissolves. One dissolving is exothermic and the other is endothermic. The reason is in the attractions between the particles.

Topic 3.10 described dissolving as a trade of attractions. Here is the same trade with the energy of each part written in:

1. The solute's particles leave their neighbors. Overcoming those attractions takes energy in.
2. The solvent's molecules move apart to let them in. That takes energy in too.
3. Each solute particle ends up held by solvent molecules. Forming those attractions releases energy.

Whether the whole change is exothermic or endothermic depends on which is larger: the energy taken in by parts 1 and 2, or the energy released by part 3.

The water plays two parts here. The attractions of its molecules belong to the change. Its temperature, which is the motion of its molecules, is what the thermometer reads, and that is what we count as surroundings.

- **Sodium hydroxide.** The attractions between its ions and the water molecules are so strong that forming them releases more energy than parts 1 and 2 took. The extra goes to the surroundings, and the solution warms.
- **Xylitol.** Pulling its molecules out of the crystal and making room in the water takes more energy than the new attractions release. The difference comes out of the surroundings, your tongue included.

<KeyIdea>

Dissolving overcomes some attractions, which takes energy, and forms others, which releases energy. If more is released than taken in, the dissolving is exothermic. If more is taken in than released, it is endothermic.

</KeyIdea>

An endothermic change like the xylitol's happens all by itself, which may seem odd. Energy is not the only thing that decides whether a change happens, and Unit 9 takes that up.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Taking any rise in temperature as a sign that the system gained energy.** A thermometer nearly always sits in the surroundings. If they warmed, the system lost energy.
- **Switching point of view halfway.** Exothermic, endothermic and the sign all describe the system. Decide what the system is before anything else.
- **Thinking the spray delivers a chill.** The ankle gets cold because energy leaves the skin and goes into the evaporating liquid. The skin gives up energy; it is not handed anything cold.
- **Counting the water as part of the system.** For a reaction or a dissolving in water, the water whose temperature you read is surroundings.
- **Thinking that pulling particles apart releases energy.** Overcoming an attraction always takes energy in. Forming one releases energy, as topic 2.2 said for bonds.
- **Assuming dissolving always cools, or always warms.** It depends on which is larger, the energy taken in or the energy released.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The system is what you are studying and the surroundings are everything else. For a change in water, the water is surroundings.
- A temperature change shows that energy is changing, because temperature measures average kinetic energy.
- An exothermic change lowers the system's energy and the surroundings gain that energy. An endothermic change raises the system's energy at the surroundings' expense.
- Cooling, freezing and condensing are exothermic for the substance. Heating, melting and vaporizing are endothermic.
- Energy enters or leaves a system as heat or as work.
- Dissolving is exothermic when forming the new attractions releases more energy than overcoming the old ones takes, and endothermic when it releases less.

</KeyPoints>
