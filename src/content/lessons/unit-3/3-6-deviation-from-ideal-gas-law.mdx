Look inside a carbon dioxide fire extinguisher and you would find that most of the CO₂ is a liquid, even at room temperature. It is held under about 57 atm, the vapor pressure of CO₂ at 20 °C. $PV = nRT$ never predicts a liquid. It says a gas squeezed harder just gets smaller and smaller. But squeeze real molecules close together, and their attractions hold them as a liquid. The ideal gas model leaves those attractions out.

## What the ideal model leaves out

Two assumptions of the kinetic molecular theory (topic 3.5) are not quite true. The model says gas particles do not attract each other, and that they take up no room of their own. Real particles do attract each other: those are the intermolecular forces of topic 3.1. And real particles do take up space.

Most of the time neither matters much. Near room temperature and 1 atm, most gases follow $PV = nRT$ to within about 1 percent. Nitrogen and oxygen follow it to within about 0.1 percent.

To see how far a gas strays, compare its measured $PV$ with $nRT$. Divide one by the other:

$$
\frac{PV}{nRT}
$$

For an ideal gas this ratio is exactly 1. Below 1, the gas has less $PV$ than predicted. Above 1, it has more.

## Attractions: close to condensing

Picture a particle about to hit the wall of its container. The particles behind it tug it back, so it hits a little more softly. With every collision softened, the measured pressure is lower than $PV = nRT$ predicts. So $PV/nRT$ is less than 1.

The tug matters most when the particles are slow and close together. Slow means a low temperature. Close together means a higher pressure. Those are conditions close to the ones where the gas would condense.

Argon shows it. Argon boils at 87 K at 1 atm. At 90 K and 1 atm, just above that, it deviates noticeably from ideal behavior: its $PV$ is about 3 percent below $nRT$. At 400 K and 1 atm it is very nearly ideal.

The stronger the forces between the particles, the bigger the deviation. Polar molecules, molecules that hydrogen-bond and molecules with many electrons deviate more.

<Worked title="Testing a gas against PV = nRT">

A 2.00 L container holds 0.500 mol of gas X at 255 K, and the measured pressure is 4.95 atm. (Gas X is made up for this example.) How far is it from ideal, and why?

1. **Ideal pressure.** Find the pressure an ideal gas would have, using R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   P &= \frac{nRT}{V} \\
   &= \frac{(0.500)(0.08206)(255)}{2.00} \\
   &= 5.23\ \text{atm}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **The ratio.** Divide the measured $PV$ by $nRT$.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{PV}{nRT} &= \frac{(4.95)(2.00)}{(0.500)(0.08206)(255)} \\
   &= \frac{9.90}{10.46} \\
   &= 0.946
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The measured pressure divided by the ideal pressure gives the same number.

   $$
   \frac{4.95\ \text{atm}}{5.23\ \text{atm}} = 0.946
   $$

3. **Read it.** The ratio is below 1. The gas pushes about 5 percent less than an ideal gas would.

4. **Explain it.** Attractions between the particles are softening their collisions with the walls. So gas X is probably cool enough to be fairly close to condensing.

</Worked>

## Particle volume: very high pressure

At extremely high pressure the particles are packed so tightly that their own volume is a real share of the container. The space they actually move in is less than $V$. So the gas cannot be squeezed as far as $PV = nRT$ predicts. Its volume is larger than ideal, and $PV/nRT$ is more than 1.

Both effects are always present. At moderate pressures attractions usually win, and the ratio dips below 1. At very high pressures particle volume wins, and the ratio climbs above 1. In between, the ratio passes through 1. There the two effects happen to cancel; the gas is not ideal.

## When a gas behaves ideally

Two conditions bring a real gas close to ideal.

At **high temperature** the particles move so fast that attractions barely slow their collisions.

At **low pressure** the particles are far apart. Their attractions are weak, and their own volume is a tiny share of the container.

The gas itself matters too. The gases closest to ideal have weak forces and small particles, such as helium and hydrogen. The gases that deviate most have strong forces and large particles: polar molecules, molecules that hydrogen-bond, and molecules with many electrons.

<KeyIdea>

A real gas behaves most like an ideal gas at high temperature and low pressure. It deviates most at low temperature, where attractions matter, and at high pressure, where attractions and then the particles' own volume matter.

</KeyIdea>

**Beyond the exam:** chemists sometimes correct $PV = nRT$ with the van der Waals equation:

$$
\left(P + \dfrac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT
$$

Each correction mends one thing the ideal model leaves out. The term with $a$ adds back the pressure lost to attractions, and $a$ is larger for gases with stronger intermolecular forces. The term with $b$ subtracts the space the particles themselves fill, and $b$ is larger for bigger particles. The equation sheet does not give this equation, and the exam does not ask you to use it.

<Trap>

Four mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Swapping the two effects.** Attractions make $PV/nRT$ less than 1. Particle volume makes it more than 1.
- **Thinking high temperature causes deviations.** High temperature makes a gas behave more ideally.
- **Forgetting that gas particles attract each other at all.** Every real gas has at least London dispersion forces (topic 3.1).
- **Blaming particle volume at ordinary pressures.** At around 1 atm the particles fill a tiny share of the container. Their volume matters only at very high pressure.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The ideal gas law ignores the attractions between particles and the particles' own volume, so real gases deviate from it.
- Attractions make $PV/nRT$ less than 1, and they matter most close to condensation, at low temperature.
- The particles' own volume makes $PV/nRT$ more than 1, and it matters at very high pressure.
- Real gases behave most ideally at high temperature and low pressure.
- Gases with strong intermolecular forces and large particles deviate most, and small, nonpolar gases with few electrons, such as helium, come closest to ideal.

</KeyPoints>
