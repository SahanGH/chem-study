The nitrogen molecules in the air around you right now move at about 475 m/s on average. That is nearly twice the cruising speed of an airliner (about 250 m/s). Yet you feel no hail of tiny impacts, only a steady, even push. That push is air pressure, and this lesson's model explains it.

## The model

You cannot watch a single gas particle. But you can picture what the particles are doing, and then test the picture against what a gas does. The **kinetic molecular theory** pictures a gas this way:

1. The particles are in constant, random, straight-line motion. They change direction only when they collide, with each other or with a wall.
2. The particles are tiny compared with the distances between them. Their own volume is negligible, so the gas is mostly empty space.
3. The particles neither attract nor repel each other, except during collisions.
4. Collisions are **elastic**: no kinetic energy is lost when particles bounce off each other or off a wall.
5. The kelvin temperature measures how much kinetic energy the particles have on average. The two are proportional.

A gas that fits this model exactly is the ideal gas of topic 3.4. No real gas fits it perfectly. Topic 3.6 shows where assumptions 2 and 3 break down.

Constant, random motion is not only for gases. The particles of a liquid move past one another, and the particles of a solid vibrate about fixed positions. All the particles in any sample of matter are moving, all the time.

## Pressure, one collision at a time

One particle hitting a wall gives it a tiny push. A gas has so many particles that the pushes never stop. The pressure of a gas is the push of countless particles striking the wall, spread over the wall's area.

That one idea explains every relationship from topic 3.4:

- **A hotter gas in a rigid container.** Faster particles reach the walls more often and strike them harder, so the pressure rises.
- **The same gas in less room.** At the same temperature the particles move at the same speeds, but each part of the wall is now hit more often. Halve the volume and the hits on each part of the wall double, so the pressure doubles.
- **More gas in the same container.** At the same temperature, more particles make more hits, so the pressure is higher.
- **A hotter gas in a container that can stretch.** The gas expands. It keeps expanding until the particles are far enough apart that each part of the wall is hit less often, by just enough to make up for the harder hits. The pressure ends where it began.

## Temperature measures kinetic energy

A moving particle has kinetic energy, the energy of motion. For a particle of mass $m$ moving at speed $v$, it is:

$$
\mathit{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

Temperature measures this motion. The kelvin temperature of a sample is proportional to the average kinetic energy of its particles. Double the kelvin temperature and the average kinetic energy doubles.

That works only on the kelvin scale. Zero on the Celsius scale is just where water freezes. Particles at 0 °C still have plenty of kinetic energy.

Here is a consequence that surprises people. At the same temperature, every gas has the same average kinetic energy, whatever its particles are. A heavy molecule and a light atom in the same room carry the same kinetic energy on average.

Now look at the equation again. If the kinetic energy is the same and the mass is smaller, the speed has to be greater. A lighter particle must move faster to carry the same energy. So at one temperature, lighter gases move faster on average.

<Worked title="Helium against sulfur hexafluoride">

Sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆, is a dense gas used to insulate high-voltage electrical equipment. Suppose a sealed vessel holds a mixture of helium and SF₆ at 25 °C. Which particles move faster on average, and by what factor?

1. **Same temperature, same average kinetic energy.** Both gases are at 25 °C, so a helium atom and an SF₆ molecule have the same kinetic energy on average.

2. **Set the kinetic energies equal.** Write $v$ for a typical speed of each kind of particle.

   $$
   \tfrac{1}{2}m_{\ce{He}}v_{\ce{He}}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}m_{\ce{SF6}}v_{\ce{SF6}}^2
   $$

   The halves cancel. Rearrange for the ratio of the speeds.

   $$
   \frac{v_{\ce{He}}}{v_{\ce{SF6}}} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{\ce{SF6}}}{m_{\ce{He}}}}
   $$

   The masses of the particles are in the same ratio as the molar masses: 146.06 g/mol for SF₆ and 4.003 g/mol for helium.

3. **Calculate.** The units cancel, so the answer is a plain number.

   $$
   \sqrt{\frac{146.06}{4.003}} = \sqrt{36.49} = 6.04
   $$

4. **Interpret.** Helium atoms move about six times as fast as SF₆ molecules: 6.04 times. An SF₆ molecule is about 36.5 times as massive as a helium atom. To carry the same kinetic energy, its speed must be smaller by the square root of 36.5, which is 6.04.

The heavy molecules carry the same kinetic energy at a much lower speed. The ratio is the same for any typical speed you compare. For scale: at 25 °C, helium atoms average about 1260 m/s, and SF₆ molecules about 208 m/s.

</Worked>

**Beyond the exam:** the speed of a particle that has exactly the average kinetic energy is called the root-mean-square speed:

$$
u_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}
$$

Here R is 8.314 J/(mol·K) and M is the molar mass in kg/mol. This speed is a little higher than the average speed. The equation sheet does not give it, and the exam does not ask you to calculate it.

## A spread of speeds

So far "average" has done a lot of work. Here is why it has to. The particles of a gas collide constantly, and in every collision they trade energy: one speeds up, the other slows down. So at any moment their speeds are spread out. Some particles crawl, a few race, and most are somewhere in between.

The **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** is the curve of that spread. Speed runs across the bottom. Up the side is the fraction of the particles at each speed. The curve starts at zero, because almost no particle is nearly still. It rises to a peak, then trails off in a long tail toward high speeds.

Read areas on this graph, not just heights. The area under the whole curve stands for all the particles. The area between two speeds is the fraction of the particles with speeds in that range.

The peak is the **most probable speed**: the speed that more particles have than any other. It is not the average speed. The long tail pulls the average to the right of the peak. For nitrogen at 300 K, the peak is at about 422 m/s and the average is about 476 m/s. The peak is not the top speed either. The tail thins out with no sharp end.

Two things change the curve:

- **Warmer.** The peak moves to a higher speed, and the curve flattens and stretches toward high speeds. The total area is unchanged, since it still stands for the same particles. The figure below shows it for nitrogen.
- **A heavier gas at the same temperature.** The curve is narrower and taller, with its peak at a lower speed. A lighter gas does the reverse.

You can also draw the curve against kinetic energy instead of speed. Then it depends only on the temperature. At one temperature, every gas gives the same energy curve, whatever its molar mass. That is the "same average kinetic energy" idea as a picture. Topic 5.5 uses the energy curve to explain why reactions speed up when they are warmed.

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Treating one temperature as one speed.** What gases at the same temperature share is their average kinetic energy. The lighter particles are quicker.
- **Reading the peak as the average or the top speed.** The peak marks the most probable speed. The average lies a little to its right, and the curve has no top speed.
- **Reading the curve's height as a number of molecules.** A lower peak for a warmer gas does not mean fewer molecules. The count is the area under the curve, and warming leaves it unchanged.
- **Using Celsius in a proportion.** Warming a gas from 25 °C to 100 °C raises the average kinetic energy by a factor of 1.25, not 4.
- **Saying particles get bigger when heated.** They move faster. A heated gas expands because its faster particles push the walls outward, not because the particles swell.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The kinetic molecular theory pictures a gas as tiny particles in constant random motion, with negligible volume, no attractions and elastic collisions.
- Gas pressure comes from particles hitting the walls, so anything that makes the hits more frequent or harder raises it.
- The kelvin temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles, and a particle's kinetic energy is KE = ½mv².
- At the same temperature every gas has the same average kinetic energy, so lighter particles move faster.
- On a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve the peak is the most probable speed, and the area stands for all the particles.
- Warming makes the curve lower and broader with its peak moved right, while a heavier gas at the same temperature gives a narrower, taller curve with its peak to the left.

</KeyPoints>
