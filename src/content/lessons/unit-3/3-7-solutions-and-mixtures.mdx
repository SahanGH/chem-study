Stir sugar into a cup of tea and every sip is equally sweet, the first and the last. Now pour a glass of pulpy orange juice and let it sit. The pulp sinks, so a sip from the top is thin and a sip from the bottom is thick with pulp. Both are mixtures. Only one of them is the same all the way through, and that difference is where this lesson starts.

## Solutions and heterogeneous mixtures

A **solution** is a mixture that is the same throughout. Take a sample from anywhere in it, and its color, its density and its taste are the same. Another name for a solution is a **homogeneous mixture**.

The substance present in the larger amount is usually called the **solvent**. Whatever is dissolved in it is the **solute**. In sweet tea, water is the solvent and sugar is a solute.

Solutions are not only liquids:

- Air is a gas solution: nitrogen, oxygen, argon and a little else.
- Salt water is a liquid solution. So is vinegar, which is acetic acid in water.
- Brass is a solid solution, zinc spread through copper. Topic 2.4 calls it an alloy.

In a **heterogeneous mixture**, the properties depend on where you look. Granite shows separate grains of different minerals. The pulp in orange juice settles to the bottom of the glass.

<KeyIdea>

The test is the sample, not the ingredients. If every part of it has the same properties, it is a solution. If the properties change from place to place, it is a heterogeneous mixture.

</KeyIdea>

## Molarity

How much solute is in a solution? Chemists answer with a concentration, and the one used most in the lab is molarity. **Molarity** is the moles of solute in each liter of solution:

$$
M = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{L_{\text{solution}}}
$$

Its unit is mol/L, written M. A label that says "0.200 M" is read "0.200 molar".

Look hard at the bottom of that fraction. It is liters of the finished solution, not liters of the water you started with. Dissolving a solid changes the volume a little. So stirring 0.250 mol of solute into 500 mL of water does not give exactly 500 mL of solution.

That is why a solution is made in a **volumetric flask**, a flask with a long neck and one etched line that marks a single, exact volume. The steps:

1. Weigh the solute.
2. Put it in the flask, and add water to fill the flask only partway. Swirl until all of the solid has dissolved.
3. Add water until the bottom of the curved liquid surface, the **meniscus**, sits on the line. Look at it at eye level.
4. Stopper the flask and turn it over several times to mix.

The mass comes from the moles needed and the molar mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.200\ \text{mol/L} \times 1.000\ \text{L} \\
&\quad \times 82.03\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= 16.4\ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

A mass of 8.20 g would give 0.100 M. The molar mass is the sum for one Na, two C, three H and two O:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&22.99 + 2(12.01) \\
&\quad + 3(1.008) + 2(16.00) \\
&= 82.03\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Worked title="Making 500.0 mL of 0.200 M sodium carbonate">

How many grams of sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, are needed to make 500.0 mL of a 0.200 M solution, and how is the solution made?

1. **Moles of solute.** Rearrange the molarity equation: moles are molarity times volume. The volume goes in liters, and 500.0 mL is 0.5000 L.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   n &= M \times V \\
   &= 0.200\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.5000\ \text{L} \\
   &= 0.100\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Molar mass.** One Na₂CO₃ has two Na, one C and three O.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &2(22.99) + 12.01 + 3(16.00) \\
   &= 105.99\ \text{g/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Mass.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &0.100\ \text{mol} \times 105.99\ \text{g/mol} \\
   &= 10.6\ \text{g}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Three significant figures, from the 0.200.

4. **Make it.** Weigh 10.6 g of sodium carbonate. Dissolve it in about 300 mL of water in a 500-mL volumetric flask. Then add water to the line, stopper the flask and invert it to mix.

## Counting the ions

Glucose is a molecular compound, and it stays as whole molecules in water, as most molecular solutes do. (Acids are the main exception; Units 4 and 8 cover them.) So a 0.100 M glucose solution is 0.100 M in glucose molecules.

An ionic solute is different. It comes apart into its ions, and the formula says how many of each. One Na₂CO₃ gives two Na⁺ ions and one CO₃²⁻ ion:

$$
\ce{Na2CO3(s) -> 2Na+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq)}
$$

Here (s) marks a solid and (aq) marks something dissolved in water.

So the 0.200 M solution of the worked example is 0.400 M in Na⁺ and 0.200 M in CO₃²⁻. Square brackets around a formula stand for its molarity, so you can write [Na⁺] = 0.400 M.

The polyatomic ion CO₃²⁻ stays together. The bonds inside it are covalent, and dissolving does not break them.

To count particles, multiply moles by Avogadro's number, 6.022 × 10²³ per mole. The 500.0 mL of solution holds

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.400\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.5000\ \text{L} \\
&= 0.200\ \text{mol of}\ \ce{Na+}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.200 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \\
&= 1.20 \times 10^{23}\ \text{sodium ions}
\end{aligned}
$$

It also holds 0.100 mol of CO₃²⁻, half as much, which is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.100 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \\
&= 6.02 \times 10^{22}\ \text{carbonate ions}
\end{aligned}
$$

\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.170\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.0500\ \text{L} \\
&= 8.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(8.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}) \\
&\quad \times (6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}) \\
&= 5.12 \times 10^{21}\ \text{ions}
\end{aligned}
$$

The calcium ion concentration stays 0.0850 M.

## Dilution

Adding water to a solution spreads the same solute through more volume. The moles of solute do not change. Only the volume does, so the molarity falls.

The moles are molarity times volume, both before and after. Set the two equal:

$$
M_1V_1 = M_2V_2
$$

The 1s are the concentrated solution you start with, and the 2s are the diluted solution you end with. This equation is not on the equation sheet. It follows from the molarity equation, so you can always work through moles instead.

Either volume unit works here, liters or milliliters, as long as both volumes use the same one.

To dilute accurately, measure the concentrated solution with a **volumetric pipet**, a pipet that delivers one exact volume. Run it into a volumetric flask, then fill to the line.

<Worked title="Diluting a stock solution">

A stockroom bottle holds 2.40 M potassium chloride, KCl. How much of it is needed to make 500.0 mL of 0.120 M KCl?

1. **Moles needed in the final solution.** 500.0 mL is 0.5000 L.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &0.120\ \text{mol/L} \times 0.5000\ \text{L} \\
   &= 0.0600\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Volume of stock that holds them.** Rearrange the molarity equation for the volume.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   V &= \frac{n}{M} \\
   &= \frac{0.0600\ \text{mol}}{2.40\ \text{mol/L}} \\
   &= 0.0250\ \text{L} = 25.0\ \text{mL}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Check with the dilution equation.** Solve it for the volume of stock.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   V_1 &= \frac{M_2V_2}{M_1} \\
   &= \frac{(0.120\ \text{M})(500.0\ \text{mL})}{2.40\ \text{M}} \\
   &= 25.0\ \text{mL}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The same answer, and the milliliters never had to become liters.

4. **Make it.** Deliver 25.0 mL of the stock with a 25-mL volumetric pipet into a 500-mL volumetric flask. Add water to the line, stopper the flask and invert it to mix.

</Worked>

\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}
$$

**Beyond the exam:** concentration is also given in other ways, such as percent by mass on a product label, or molality (moles of solute per kilogram of solvent). The exam asks you to calculate molarity, not those.

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Dividing by the volume of water instead of the volume of solution.** Molarity uses liters of the finished solution. That is why the flask is filled to the line after the solute has dissolved.
- **Leaving the volume in milliliters** in $M = n/V$. Convert to liters first: 25.0 mL is 0.0250 L.
- **Thinking dilution changes the moles of solute.** Adding water changes the volume and the concentration. The moles stay the same.
- **Forgetting the subscripts when finding ion concentrations.** 0.0850 M CaCl₂ is 0.170 M in Cl⁻, not 0.0850 M.
- **Splitting a polyatomic ion.** CO₃²⁻ stays together in solution.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A solution has the same properties in every part, and it can be a solid, a liquid or a gas.
- A heterogeneous mixture changes from place to place.
- Molarity is moles of solute per liter of solution, $M = n/V$, with the volume in liters.
- To make a solution, dissolve the solute in part of the water in a volumetric flask, then fill to the line.
- Dilution keeps the moles of solute, so $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$.
- An ionic solute gives ions in the ratio of its formula, so 0.0850 M CaCl₂ is 0.170 M in Cl⁻.

</KeyPoints>
