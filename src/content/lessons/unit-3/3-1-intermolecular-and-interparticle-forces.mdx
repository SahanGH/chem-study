Look around a garage and you can find three things made of the same two elements, carbon and hydrogen, all three of them nonpolar. Propane, C₃H₈, the fuel in a camping-stove tank, is a gas at room temperature and ordinary pressure. Octane, C₈H₁₈, one of the hydrocarbons in gasoline, is a liquid. A paraffin candle, made of hydrocarbons with about 20 to 40 carbons each, is a solid. Same atoms, same kind of bonds. What changes is the size of the molecules, and with it how strongly they hold on to each other. Those attractions are what this lesson is about.

## Forces between molecules, not within them

An **intermolecular force** is an attraction between separate molecules. It is not a bond. The covalent bonds of Unit 2 hold atoms together inside a molecule. Intermolecular forces hold whole molecules near each other.

When water boils, its molecules move apart, but every O–H bond stays whole. The steam is still H₂O.

The two kinds of attraction are very different in size. Boiling a mole of water at 100 °C takes about 41 kJ. Breaking the O–H bonds in a mole of water would take more than 900 kJ, over twenty times as much.

**Interparticle forces** are the same idea widened to every kind of particle: atoms, molecules and ions. All of these forces are Coulomb attractions between opposite charges, full or partial.

<KeyIdea>

Intermolecular forces hold molecules near each other. Covalent bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule. Melting or boiling a molecular substance overcomes the first and leaves the second alone.

</KeyIdea>

## London dispersion forces: in every substance

The electrons in a molecule never sit still. At any instant they may be bunched a little more on one side than the other. That lopsided moment is a **temporary dipole**: one side briefly δ−, the other briefly δ+.

A temporary dipole tugs on the electrons of a neighboring molecule and gives it a dipole too, lined up so the two attract. A moment later both have shifted, and the attraction turns up somewhere else. These flickering attractions between temporary dipoles are **London dispersion forces**.

They act between all molecules and atoms, polar or not. So the question is never whether a substance has London forces. It is only how strong they are. Two things make them stronger.

**More electrons.** A large electron cloud, held loosely far from the nuclei, is easy to distort. That ease of distortion is called **polarizability**. The noble gases show it. Helium, with 2 electrons, boils at −269 °C. Argon, with 18, boils at −186 °C, and xenon, with 54, at −108 °C. Each step up in electrons raises the boiling point. Pi bonds help too. The pi electrons of a double or triple bond lie beside the line between the two nuclei, not on it, where they are held less tightly. They shift more easily, so pi electrons add more to a molecule's polarizability than the same number of electrons held in single bonds.

**More contact.** A long, straight molecule lies flat against its neighbor, so a lot of its surface is in contact. Compact, branched ones touch at fewer points. Octane and 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (the "isooctane" of octane ratings) are both C₈H₁₈, with 66 electrons each. Yet straight-chain octane boils at about 126 °C, and branched, compact isooctane at about 99 °C.

One warning about names. Some books use "van der Waals forces" as a collective name for several attractions between neutral molecules. London dispersion forces are only one member of that group, so do not use the two names as if they meant the same thing. On the exam, name the specific force.

<KeyIdea>

Every substance has London dispersion forces. They grow with the number of electrons and with the area over which molecules touch, and between large molecules they are often the strongest attraction of all.

</KeyIdea>

## Dipole–dipole forces

A polar molecule (topic 2.7) has a permanent δ+ end and a permanent δ− end. Neighboring polar molecules turn so that the δ+ end of one sits near the δ− end of the next. That attraction is a **dipole–dipole force**.

Its strength depends on how large the dipoles are and on how the molecules line up. Head to tail, they attract. Two δ− ends side by side would repel, so in a liquid the molecules spend more of their time in the arrangements that attract.

Dipole–dipole forces act on top of London forces, never instead of them. So a polar molecule usually attracts its neighbors more strongly than a nonpolar molecule of about the same size.

Topic 2.7 gave two boiling points without explaining them: cis-1,2-dichloroethene boils at a higher temperature than trans. Here is the explanation. The two isomers have the same atoms and the same 48 electrons, so about the same London forces. But cis is polar and trans is not. Cis boils at about 60 °C, trans at about 48 °C. Those extra 12 degrees are the dipole–dipole forces.

## A polar molecule and a nonpolar one

What happens when a polar molecule meets a nonpolar one? The polar molecule's permanent dipole distorts the electron cloud of its nonpolar neighbor. That gives the neighbor a dipole of its own, an induced dipole, lined up so the two attract. The attraction is a **dipole–induced dipole force**.

It is always attractive. It is stronger when the polar molecule's dipole is larger, and when the nonpolar molecule is more polarizable.

Noble gases dissolving in water show it. Water is polar and the noble gases are nonpolar, yet they dissolve a little. Xenon, with 54 electrons, dissolves about ten times as well as helium, with 2. One reason is that its large electron cloud is far easier to distort.

This force acts, on top of London forces, whenever a polar molecule meets a nonpolar one.

## Hydrogen bonds

When hydrogen is covalently bonded to N, O or F, the bond is polar. O–H differs in electronegativity by 1.2, F–H by 1.8 and N–H by 0.8. The small hydrogen atom is left with a strong δ+.

The size of the difference is not the whole story, though. H–Cl differs by 1.0, more than N–H's 0.8, yet HCl does not form hydrogen bonds. Chlorine is a larger atom. Its lone pairs are spread out over more space, and a hydrogen cannot get as close to them.

What counts is a strongly δ+ hydrogen, a tiny atom with no inner electrons shielding its nucleus, that can get very close to a lone pair on a neighboring N, O or F. A **hydrogen bond** is the attraction between such a hydrogen and a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of a neighboring molecule, or of another part of the same molecule.

Between neutral molecules of similar size, the hydrogen bond is the strongest attraction. But it is still an attraction between molecules, not a covalent bond inside one. It is drawn as a dotted line.

Two conditions, and you need both:

- the hydrogen must be bonded directly to N, O or F;
- the molecule it is attracted to must offer a lone pair on N, O or F.

Trimethylamine, N(CH₃)₃, has a nitrogen with a lone pair, but all nine of its hydrogens are on carbon. So its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond with each other.

Water is the great example. Each water molecule can take part in up to four hydrogen bonds: two through its hydrogens and two through the lone pairs on its oxygen.

Hydrogen bonds can also form between two different substances. Acetone, (CH₃)₂CO, has all its hydrogens on carbon, so its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond with each other. Mix it with water, though, and a hydrogen of water is attracted to the lone pairs on acetone's oxygen. That is a hydrogen bond between two different molecules, and it is part of why acetone and water mix in any proportion.

Now put three of these forces to work at once.

<Worked title="Ranking four boiling points">

Rank butane (C₄H₁₀), acetone ((CH₃)₂CO), 1-propanol (CH₃CH₂CH₂OH) and octane (C₈H₁₈) from lowest to highest boiling point, and explain.

| Substance | Electrons |
|---|---|
| butane | 34 |
| acetone | 32 |
| 1-propanol | 34 |
| octane | 66 |

1. **Count electrons.** Butane, acetone and 1-propanol have 32 to 34 electrons each, so their London forces are about equal. Octane has 66, far more.

2. **Find the extra forces in the first three.** Butane is nonpolar: London forces only. Acetone has a polar C=O bond (electronegativity difference 0.8) that nothing cancels, so it adds dipole–dipole forces. But all its hydrogens are on carbon, so its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond with each other. 1-Propanol has an O–H bond, so it adds hydrogen bonds. So butane < acetone < 1-propanol.

3. **Place octane.** It has only London forces. But with about twice the electrons and a long chain, here they outweigh everything 1-propanol has.

4. **Write the order.** Butane < acetone < 1-propanol < octane.

5. **Compare with the data.** The boiling points are about −1 °C, 56 °C, 97 °C and 126 °C, in that order.

The lesson of step 3: compare sizes before naming forces. A large nonpolar molecule can out-boil a small hydrogen-bonded one.

It does not always go that way. Hexane, with 50 electrons, boils at 69 °C, below 1-propanol. When more electrons compete against a stronger kind of force, the data decide, and your job is to explain them.

</Worked>

## Inside large molecules

The same attractions act within and between very large molecules. The two strands of DNA are held together by hydrogen bonds between the bases on opposite strands.

A protein is one long chain that folds into a particular shape. Hydrogen bonds, London forces and attractions between charged groups hold that shape by acting between different parts of the same chain. The same attractions also hold separate molecules together, as in DNA's two strands.

These are **noncovalent** attractions: no covalent bonds are made or broken when they form. One at a time they are weak, but there are thousands of them, and the shape they hold decides what the molecule can do. Topic 3.2 returns to this.

## Ions and polar molecules

An ion carries a full charge, so it strongly attracts the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule. That attraction is an **ion–dipole force**.

Around a cation, water molecules turn their δ− oxygen toward the ion. Around an anion, they turn a δ+ hydrogen toward it. The partial charges decide the direction, exactly as in a dipole–dipole force. But the ion's full charge makes the attraction stronger than a dipole–dipole force.

The attraction is stronger for an ion with a larger charge, and for a smaller ion, just as Coulomb's law predicts.

Here are all five forces side by side:

| Force | Acts between | What decides its strength |
|---|---|---|
| London dispersion | all molecules and atoms | number of electrons and contact area |
| Dipole–dipole | polar molecules | size of the dipoles and how the molecules line up, on top of London forces |
| Dipole–induced dipole | a polar molecule and a nonpolar one | size of the dipole and polarizability of the nonpolar molecule, on top of London forces |
| Hydrogen bond | H on N, O or F, and a lone pair on N, O or F | a strongly δ+ H that can get very close to the lone pair; the strongest attraction between neutral molecules of similar size |
| Ion–dipole | an ion and a polar molecule | charge and size of the ion |

The attractions between ions in an ionic solid, and between ions and electrons in a metal, are usually stronger still. Topic 3.2 uses all of them to explain the properties of solids.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Ranking by the name of the force alone.** Count electrons and compare sizes first: octane out-boils 1-propanol, and hexane out-boils HF.
- **Saying nonpolar molecules have no intermolecular forces.** Every substance has London dispersion forces.
- **Assuming a molecule hydrogen-bonds because it holds hydrogen and also N, O or F somewhere.** Having both is not enough; the hydrogen itself must be bonded directly to N, O or F. Trimethylamine, N(CH₃)₃, has a nitrogen with a lone pair, but all nine hydrogens sit on carbon, so it cannot hydrogen-bond with itself.
- **Confusing a hydrogen bond with a covalent bond.** The O–H bond inside a water molecule is covalent. The hydrogen bond is the weaker attraction from that H to a lone pair on another molecule, or on a distant part of one large molecule.
- **Saying boiling breaks covalent bonds.** Boiling water separates H₂O molecules; the O–H bonds stay.
- **Using "van der Waals forces" as another name for London dispersion forces.** Name the specific force.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules, far weaker than the covalent bonds inside them.
- London dispersion forces act in every substance and grow with the number of electrons and the contact area between molecules.
- Polar molecules add dipole–dipole forces on top of London forces, and a polar molecule also induces a dipole in a nonpolar neighbor (dipole–induced dipole).
- A hydrogen bond needs H bonded directly to N, O or F, attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule, or of another part of the same large molecule.
- Ion–dipole forces, between an ion and a polar molecule, are stronger than dipole–dipole forces and are what pull the ions of a salt into solution when it dissolves in water.
- Compare sizes before naming forces: a large nonpolar molecule can out-boil a small hydrogen-bonded one.

</KeyPoints>
