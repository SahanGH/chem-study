Shrink yourself down to the size of a molecule and stand in a glass of salt water. You would not find any tiny salt crystals. You would find sodium ions and chloride ions drifting about on their own, each one wrapped in water molecules that have turned to face it. Drawing that scene correctly is a skill the exam tests directly.

## Ionic solutes come apart into ions

A **particle drawing** shows a sample as its individual particles: a circle for each atom or one-atom ion, a small group of joined circles for each molecule. A polyatomic ion may be drawn as one labeled shape or as its joined atoms, and it counts as one particle either way. Before you can draw a solution this way, you have to know which particles are in it.

When an ionic solid dissolves, its ions separate. (Topic 2.3 describes the solid they leave.) So the drawing shows separate cations and separate anions, spread through the water. It never shows NaCl pairs, and it never shows little clusters of ions.

Count the ions from the formula. MgCl₂ gives one Mg²⁺ for every two Cl⁻. Label each ion with its charge.

A polyatomic ion stays in one piece, because the bonds inside it are covalent, and dissolving does not break them. K₂SO₄ gives two K⁺ and one SO₄²⁻. It never gives sulfur and oxygen atoms on their own.

Here is a quick test of any drawing of a dissolved salt: the charges in it add up to zero. For two K⁺ and one SO₄²⁻:

$$
2(+1) + (-2) = 0
$$

## Molecular solutes stay whole

Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is a molecular compound, and a molecular solute dissolves as whole molecules. The molecules move apart from one another, but the covalent bonds inside each one do not break. So you draw complete molecules among the water. Never draw loose atoms, and never draw ions.

A polar molecule such as glucose attracts water strongly. Its O–H groups form hydrogen bonds (topic 3.1) with water molecules. A drawing can show those as dotted or dashed lines between the molecules.

One caution: some molecular substances, hydrogen chloride among them, react with water and form ions. Units 4 and 8 deal with those.

## Water turns to face the ions

Water is a polar molecule (topic 2.7). Its oxygen end is partly negative, δ−, and its hydrogen ends are partly positive, δ+.

Topic 3.1 called the attraction between an ion and polar molecules the ion–dipole force, and showed what it does to the water beside an ion. A drawing has to show that too. Each water molecule points its oppositely charged end at the ion: the oxygen when the ion is positive, one of its hydrogens when the ion is negative.

The same force is what pulls the ions out of the solid.

## Drawing concentration to scale

A box in a particle drawing stands for one small volume of solution. Concentration is how many solute particles that volume holds. So twice the concentration means twice the particles in the same box.

To draw that, you need a scale. A **formula unit** is one set of the ions in a compound's formula: one formula unit of MgCl₂ is one Mg²⁺ and two Cl⁻. In this lesson, every box holds one formula unit of solute for each 0.05 M. For a molecular solute, that is one molecule for each 0.05 M. The water is left out of these boxes, so the solute can be counted.

On that scale, 0.10 M glucose is two glucose molecules. But 0.10 M NaCl is two Na⁺ and two Cl⁻, four particles, because the salt comes apart.

<KeyIdea>

A box is a fixed volume, so concentration shows up as a count. Scale the number of formula units to the molarity, then draw each formula unit of a salt as its separate ions.

</KeyIdea>

On this lesson's scale, a box shows a solution that is 0.20 M in glucose and 0.10 M in calcium bromide, CaBr₂. How many solute particles does the drawing show?

<div slot="why">

Glucose is molecular, so each 0.05 M is one whole molecule. Its 0.20 M is 4 glucose molecules.

Calcium bromide is ionic. Its 0.10 M is 2 formula units, and each one comes apart into one Ca²⁺ and two Br⁻. That makes 2 Ca²⁺ and 4 Br⁻.

$$
4 + 2 + 4 = 10
$$

The glucose molecules carry no charge, and the charges of the ions add to zero:

$$
2(+2) + 4(-1) = 0
$$

</div>
</Check>

<Worked title="Drawing a second solution on the same scale">

A box for 0.10 M sodium chloride shows 2 Na⁺ and 2 Cl⁻. Draw the same volume of 0.15 M strontium nitrate, Sr(NO₃)₂.

1. **Scale the formula units.** The first box shows 2 formula units of NaCl for 0.10 M. So one formula unit stands for 0.05 M, and 0.15 M is 3 formula units of Sr(NO₃)₂.

2. **Split each formula unit into ions.** Each Sr(NO₃)₂ gives one Sr²⁺ and two NO₃⁻. Three of them give 3 Sr²⁺ and 6 NO₃⁻, which is 9 ions in all.

3. **Draw them.** Each NO₃⁻ is one particle, because a polyatomic ion stays whole. Spread the 9 ions through the box, with no NO₃⁻ paired with a Sr²⁺.

4. **Check the charge.**

   $$
   3(+2) + 6(-1) = 0
   $$

   The box is neutral, as every solution is.

</Worked>

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again in these drawings:

- **Drawing an ionic solute as pairs or formula units.** Dissolved ions are separate, each one on its own in the water.
- **Getting the ratio wrong.** Count from the formula: two Cl⁻ for each Mg²⁺. Then check that the charges add to zero.
- **Breaking up a polyatomic ion.** SO₄²⁻ and NO₃⁻ stay whole.
- **Breaking a molecular solute into atoms or ions.** Glucose dissolves as whole molecules.
- **Turning water the wrong way.** Oxygen toward cations, hydrogen toward anions.
- **Drawing a more concentrated solution with the same number of particles.** Double the concentration, and you double the particles in the same box.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A dissolved ionic compound is drawn as separate ions, in the ratio its formula gives, with charges that add to zero.
- A polyatomic ion such as SO₄²⁻ or NO₃⁻ stays in one piece when its compound dissolves.
- A molecular solute such as glucose is drawn as whole molecules, never as atoms or ions.
- Water turns its partly negative oxygen toward a cation and a partly positive hydrogen toward an anion. That attraction is the ion–dipole force.
- In a box drawing, the number of solute particles is proportional to the concentration, so twice the molarity means twice the particles in the same box.

</KeyPoints>
