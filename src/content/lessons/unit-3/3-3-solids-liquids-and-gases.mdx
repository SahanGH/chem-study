Fill an ice-cube tray to the brim and put it in the freezer. The cubes come out bulging a little above the rim, but only a little. Now boil the same water away in a pan, and the steam spreads through the whole kitchen. Freezing hardly changes how much room the water takes up. Boiling changes it enormously. The particles explain both.

## Three pictures of one substance

You can describe each phase by two things: how its particles are arranged, and how they move.

In a **solid**, the particles touch and hold fixed places. Each one vibrates where it is, but it does not move past its neighbors.

In a **liquid**, the particles still touch, but they have no fixed places. They slide past one another, moving and colliding all the time. That is why a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, yet keeps its own volume.

In a **gas**, the particles are far apart. Each one flies in a straight line until it hits another particle or a wall. With so much empty space between them, and so little attraction, a gas has neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume. It fills whatever container it is in.

In a liquid, the forces between the particles matter too. Glycerol is a polar molecule with three O–H groups, so each molecule can hydrogen-bond to its neighbors at several points. Glycerol flows slowly, like syrup. Warm it, and the molecules move past each other more easily, so it pours faster.

## Crystalline and amorphous

Solids come in two arrangements. In a **crystalline solid**, the particles sit in a regular pattern that repeats in all three dimensions. Table salt, ice and quartz are crystalline. In an **amorphous solid**, the particles are jumbled, with no regular pattern. Window glass and many plastics are amorphous.

One substance can be either. Rock candy is crystalline sucrose. It grows slowly from a sugar solution, so the molecules have time to line up. A hard candy, such as a lollipop, is mostly sucrose, cooled from a hot syrup too fast for crystals to form, so it is amorphous.

Either way, the particles of a solid only vibrate in place. They do not move past one another. Which structure forms depends on the attractions between the particles and on how well they pack together.

## Solid or liquid: about the same room

The particles touch in a solid, and they still touch in a liquid. So a substance's solid and its liquid take up nearly the same space per mole. They have similar **molar volumes**, the volume that one mole takes up. A gas is different, because its particles are far apart. The worked example below puts numbers on all three.

Water is unusual in one way: its solid takes up a little more room than its liquid, which is why ice floats. The two volumes are still close. For most substances the solid is slightly denser than the liquid, as you will see for lead and for substance X below.

<Worked title="One mole, three volumes">

How much room does one mole of lead (207.2 g/mol) take up as a solid and as a liquid? How does that compare with one mole of a gas?

1. **Solid.** At room temperature, solid lead has a density of 11.34 g/cm³. A cubic centimeter and a milliliter are the same volume.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   V &= \frac{207.2\ \text{g/mol}}{11.34\ \text{g/cm}^3} \\
   &= 18.3\ \text{mL/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Liquid.** Just above its melting point (327 °C), liquid lead has a density of 10.66 g/mL.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   V &= \frac{207.2\ \text{g/mol}}{10.66\ \text{g/mL}} \\
   &= 19.4\ \text{mL/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The liquid takes up about 6 percent more room than the solid. Part of that is the solid expanding as it warms. The atoms are still in contact.

3. **A gas, for comparison.** Lead does not boil until about 1750 °C, so compare with any gas near room conditions. A mole of gas at 25 °C and 1 atm takes up about 24.5 L, which is 24,500 mL. Topic 3.4 shows why. Divide by the liquid's volume.

   $$
   \frac{24{,}500\ \text{mL/mol}}{19.4\ \text{mL/mol}} \approx 1260
   $$

   A mole of gas takes up about 1300 times the room of a mole of liquid lead.

4. **Spacing.** The same number of particles spread through about 1300 times the volume sit about 11 times farther apart, because the cube root of 1300 is about 10.9.

In a gas, most of the space is empty.

</Worked>

## Gases: space and collisions

Gas particles never stop moving. They attract each other so weakly that each one travels freely until it hits another particle or a wall. How far apart the particles are on average, and how often they collide, depend on three things you can measure.

- **Volume.** Squeeze a gas into a smaller space at the same temperature, and its particles are closer together. They collide with each other and with the walls more often, so the pressure goes up.
- **Temperature.** Heat a gas in a rigid container, and its particles move faster. They collide more often and harder. The spacing stays the same, because the container has not changed.
- **Pressure.** At a fixed temperature, a gas at a higher pressure has its particles closer together, and they collide more often.

The amount of gas matters as well. Pump more gas into the same container, and the particles are closer together and collide more often. Topic 3.4 puts numbers on all of these changes, and topic 3.5 explains them with the kinetic molecular theory.

## Vapor pressure and boiling

Topic 3.2 defined vapor pressure: the pressure of the vapor above a liquid in a closed container, once molecules escaping and molecules returning balance.

The molecules of a liquid do not all have the same energy. Some move slowly, a few move very fast. (Topic 5.5 comes back to this spread of energies.) Only the most energetic molecules at the surface can break free of their neighbors. Warm the liquid, and many more molecules have that much energy. So the vapor pressure rises steeply with temperature. Here is water:

| Temperature | Vapor pressure of water |
|---|---|
| 30 °C | 31.8 torr |
| 50 °C | 92.5 torr |
| 70 °C | 234 torr |
| 90 °C | 526 torr |
| 100 °C | 760 torr |

A liquid **boils** when its vapor pressure equals the pressure pushing down on it (in an open pan, the air's). Then bubbles of vapor can form inside the liquid, not just at its surface. At sea level the air pressure is about 1 atm, which is 760 torr, so water boils at 100 °C. That is its **normal boiling point**: the boiling point at 1 atm.

Go up a mountain and the air pressure is lower, so water boils at a lower temperature. It boils at about 93 °C in Mexico City, and at about 88 °C in La Paz, Bolivia, at about 3600 m.

While a pure liquid boils at a steady pressure, its temperature stays at the boiling point until all of it has turned to vapor. Topic 6.5 takes this further.

<KeyIdea>

Vapor pressure rises with temperature, and a liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure above it. Lower the pressure above it, and the liquid boils at a lower temperature.

</KeyIdea>

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Saying the particles of a solid do not move.** They vibrate in place. They just do not move past one another.
- **Drawing a liquid with its particles spread apart.** Liquid particles touch, as in the solid. Only their arrangement and motion differ.
- **Thinking a solid and its liquid have very different volumes.** Their molar volumes are close: for lead, 18.3 and 19.4 mL/mol. The big jump comes on boiling.
- **Saying gas particles get bigger or smaller when a gas is squeezed or heated.** The particles stay the same. Their spacing and speed change.
- **Thinking water always boils at 100 °C.** It boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure above it. 100 °C is the boiling point at 1 atm.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- In a solid the particles touch and vibrate in place; in a liquid they touch and move past one another; in a gas they are far apart and move freely.
- A crystalline solid has a regular, repeating pattern and an amorphous solid has none, but in both the particles stay in place.
- A substance's solid and liquid have similar molar volumes because their particles touch, while a gas near room conditions takes up about a thousand times more room.
- How far apart gas particles are, and how often they collide, depend on temperature, pressure and volume.
- Vapor pressure rises with temperature, and a liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure above it.

</KeyPoints>
