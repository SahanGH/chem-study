Coffee is made by filtering. The paper catches the grounds, and what drips through is brown, bitter and full of caffeine. The filter stopped the solid bits but let every dissolved thing straight through. To pull a solution apart, you need a property that differs between its parts. For the parts of a solution, that property is how strongly their particles attract.

## Why a filter fails on a solution

A filter holds back particles that are too big for its pores. Coffee grounds are big. With the water they make a heterogeneous mixture (topic 3.7), and a filter separates the two.

A solution is different. Its solute is spread out as single molecules or ions, far smaller than any pore in the paper. So the solute goes through with the solvent.

Separating a solution takes a difference in intermolecular forces between its components. Two methods use one: chromatography and distillation. This lesson covers both.

Evaporating the water from a salt solution uses such a difference too. Water molecules escape into the air. The ions, held by strong attractions, stay behind.

## Chromatography

**Chromatography** separates the parts of a mixture by letting a moving solvent carry them over a surface that holds some of them back more than others. It has two parts. The **stationary phase** stays put: a sheet of paper, a thin layer of silica on a plate, or a column packed with a powder. The **mobile phase** is a solvent that moves through it.

Here is how a plate is run. Put a small spot of the mixture on a pencil line near the bottom of the plate. Stand the plate in a little solvent, with the solvent's surface below the line. The solvent creeps up the plate and carries the mixture with it. The leading edge of the rising solvent is the **solvent front**.

Each component is pulled two ways. It is attracted to the surface of the stationary phase, and it is attracted to the solvent molecules moving past. **A component more strongly attracted to the stationary phase moves less. A component more strongly attracted to the solvent moves farther.**

Silica and paper are both polar: their surfaces are covered with O–H groups. A polar component is held to those O–H groups by dipole–dipole forces, and often by hydrogen bonds as well. A nonpolar component is held to the surface only weakly, by London dispersion and dipole–induced dipole forces, and its London dispersion forces with a less polar solvent carry it along.

So run a silica plate with a less polar solvent, and polarity decides the order. The more polar a component is, the more it clings to the surface and the shorter the distance it travels. A mostly nonpolar component travels with the solvent nearly to the top.

Paper run with a less polar solvent behaves the same way. With a polar solvent such as water the order can change, because a polar component is then strongly attracted to the solvent too.

The plate after the run, with its spots, is a **chromatogram**.

Distances on a chromatogram are compared with the **$R_f$ value**: the distance a spot moved from the start line, divided by the distance the solvent moved from the start line.

$$
R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by spot}}{\text{distance moved by solvent}}
$$

$R_f$ is always between 0 and 1. Under the same conditions, a substance always gives the same $R_f$. So a spot can be matched with a known substance run on the same plate.

A column works the same way. The mixture is washed down a tube of packing, and the component least attracted to the packing comes out of the bottom first.

<KeyIdea>

In chromatography, a component that is more attracted to the stationary phase moves less. With a polar stationary phase and a less polar solvent, the most polar component travels the shortest distance.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Reading a chromatogram">

A mixture of three dyes, labeled A, B and C, is spotted on a silica plate and run with a less polar solvent. The solvent front ends 8.0 cm above the start line. Spot A is 6.4 cm above the start line, spot B is 3.6 cm above it and spot C is 1.2 cm above it. Find each dye's $R_f$, and rank the dyes by polarity. (The dyes are made up.)

1. **Measure from the start line** to the center of each spot, and to the solvent front, not to the top of the plate. Those are the distances given.

2. **Calculate each $R_f$.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{A: } R_f &= \frac{6.4\ \text{cm}}{8.0\ \text{cm}} = 0.80 \\[0.4em]
   \text{B: } R_f &= \frac{3.6\ \text{cm}}{8.0\ \text{cm}} = 0.45 \\[0.4em]
   \text{C: } R_f &= \frac{1.2\ \text{cm}}{8.0\ \text{cm}} = 0.15
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Rank the polarity.** The silica is polar and the solvent is less polar, so the dye that clings hardest to the silica moves least. C is the most polar, then B, and A is the least polar.

4. **Identify a dye.** A known dye, run beside the mixture on the same plate, gives $R_f$ = 0.45. That matches spot B, so dye B is probably that dye.

</Worked>

## Distillation

Heat a liquid mixture, and the component with the weaker intermolecular forces escapes into the vapor more easily. At a given temperature it has the higher vapor pressure (topic 3.2). So the vapor above the boiling mixture is richer in that component than the liquid is.

**Distillation** puts that to use: the mixture is boiled, and its vapor is led away and turned back into liquid. The vapor is cooled in a **condenser**, a tube with cold water flowing through a jacket around it. The liquid that forms drips into a second flask, and it is called the **distillate**.

The first distillate is richer in the component with the weaker forces and the lower boiling point. The liquid left in the boiling flask grows richer in the other one.

Take a mixture of acetone and water. Acetone boils at 56 °C, and water at 100 °C. Acetone molecules attract one another by London dispersion forces and dipole–dipole forces, but they cannot hydrogen-bond to one another. Water molecules form a network of hydrogen bonds. Topic 3.2 compared these two liquids: at 25 °C, acetone's vapor pressure is nearly ten times water's.

So acetone has the higher vapor pressure, and the first liquid collected is mostly acetone.

A single distillation enriches the distillate. It does not make it perfectly pure: some water molecules escape with the acetone.

A thermometer in the neck of the flask reads the temperature of the vapor entering the side arm, the tube that leads to the condenser. That tells you what is coming over. A reading near 56 °C means the vapor is mostly acetone. As the reading climbs toward 100 °C, more and more of it is water.

A salt solution is the extreme case. The ions have practically no vapor pressure, so the distillate is water, and the salt stays behind.

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Filtering a solution.** Dissolved particles pass straight through filter paper. Filtration only separates undissolved solids.
- **Thinking the most polar spot always moves farthest.** It depends on the phases. A component more attracted to the stationary phase moves less. On a polar plate run with a less polar solvent, that is the most polar one.
- **Measuring $R_f$ from the bottom of the plate, or to the top of the plate.** Measure both distances from the start line, and use the solvent front, not the plate's edge.
- **Getting an $R_f$ greater than 1.** A spot cannot travel farther than the solvent that carries it.
- **Thinking the higher-boiling liquid distills first.** The first distillate is richer in the liquid with the weaker forces, the higher vapor pressure and the lower boiling point.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Filter paper cannot separate a solution, because the dissolved particles are far smaller than its pores.
- Chromatography and distillation separate the parts of a solution by their different intermolecular forces.
- In chromatography, a component more attracted to the stationary phase moves less, so on a polar plate run with a less polar solvent the most polar component has the smallest $R_f$.
- $R_f$ is the distance moved by the spot divided by the distance moved by the solvent, both measured from the start line.
- In distillation, the first distillate is richer in the liquid with the weaker forces and the higher vapor pressure.

</KeyPoints>
