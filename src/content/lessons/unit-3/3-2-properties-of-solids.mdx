A locksmith puffs powdered graphite into a sticky lock to make the key turn. A stonecutter bolts a diamond-tipped blade onto a saw to cut granite. Graphite and diamond are the same element, carbon. One is a powder slick enough to work as a dry lubricant; the other has an edge that cuts stone. Nothing differs but how the atoms are joined. What a solid does, whether it melts, bends, shatters or conducts, comes from what its particles are and what holds them together.

## Four ways to hold a solid together

You can sort every solid by asking two things: what are its particles, and what force holds them together? The answers give four kinds of solid.

| Kind | Particles, and what holds them | Typical properties |
|---|---|---|
| **Ionic** | cations and anions; attraction between opposite charges | melts high; hard and brittle; conducts only when melted or dissolved |
| **Metallic** | metal ions in a sea of delocalized electrons; metallic bonding | conducts electricity and heat as a solid; malleable and ductile |
| **Covalent network** | atoms; covalent bonds through the whole solid | melts very high; usually hard; usually does not conduct (graphite does) |
| **Molecular** | separate molecules; intermolecular forces (topic 3.1) | melts low; soft; does not conduct |

Potassium chloride is ionic. Iron is metallic. Diamond and silicon carbide are covalent network solids. Ice and naphthalene are molecular.

You have met the first two kinds already, so here they are in brief.

An ionic solid is a lattice of cations and anions. Every ion is held by all of its oppositely charged neighbors, so pulling the ions apart takes a lot of energy. Ionic solids melt and boil at high temperatures, and almost none of the solid escapes as vapor. [Topic 2.3](/unit-2-compound-structure/2-3-structure-of-ionic-solids/) explains why an ionic solid is brittle: a shifted layer brings like charges face to face. It also explains why the solid conducts only when melted or dissolved, once its ions are free to move.

A metallic solid is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalized electrons. [Topic 2.4](/unit-2-compound-structure/2-4-structure-of-metals-and-alloys/) explains why a metal conducts as a solid and bends instead of breaking: the electrons are free to move, and the sea keeps holding the ions as layers slide. It also explains why an interstitial alloy such as steel is harder, and less malleable and ductile, than the pure metal, while it still conducts.

Metals cover a huge range of melting points. Mercury melts at −39 °C, so it is a liquid at room temperature. Tungsten melts at 3422 °C.

## Covalent network solids

In a **covalent network solid**, every atom is covalently bonded to its neighbors, and the bonds run through the whole crystal, or, in graphite, through whole sheets of it. There are no small, separate molecules. You cannot say where one unit stops and the next begins: the bonded network is as big as the crystal, or as big as the sheet.

Only nonmetals and metalloids form covalent network solids. Some are elements: diamond, graphite and silicon. Others are compounds of two elements: silicon dioxide, which is the mineral quartz, and silicon carbide, the grit on some sandpaper and cutting discs.

To melt one, you have to break covalent bonds, so the melting points are high. Silicon melts at 1414 °C.

In **diamond**, each carbon is bonded to four others at fixed tetrahedral angles. The bonds cannot bend or shift, so the network is rigid, and diamond is the hardest natural substance.

In **graphite**, each carbon is bonded to three others in flat sheets of hexagons. Within a sheet the bonds are strong. Between the sheets there are only London dispersion forces, so a small push is enough to shift one sheet over the next. That is why graphite is soft and slippery.

One more difference: graphite conducts electricity along its sheets, and diamond does not. Each carbon in graphite uses three of its four valence electrons in bonds to its three neighbors. The fourth is delocalized over the whole sheet, like the electrons in a metal, so it can move along the sheet.

## Molecular solids

A **molecular solid** is made of separate molecules. Covalent bonds hold each molecule together inside, and the intermolecular forces of topic 3.1 hold the molecules to each other.

Those forces are weak next to ionic, metallic or covalent bonds, so molecular solids melt at low temperatures and are often soft. Naphthalene, C₁₀H₈, once common in mothballs, melts at 80 °C.

Molecular solids do not conduct electricity, as solids or as liquids. Every electron is held in a bond or a lone pair of its own molecule, so there is no charge free to move.

Here is the single most common mistake on this topic, stated plainly: **melting or boiling a molecular substance frees the molecules from their places or separates them; it never breaks the covalent bonds inside them.** Melted naphthalene is still C₁₀H₈.

Some molecular solids are made of very large molecules. A **polymer** is a long chain built of many repeating units. The chains of polyethylene, the material of plastic bags, are held to each other by London forces. The long chains touch over a large area, so the forces add up to a solid.

In proteins and other biomolecules, the noncovalent attractions of topic 3.1 act between molecules and between parts of one chain. They hold the molecule in its shape, and the shape decides what it does. Fry an egg and the clear white turns opaque: heat disrupts those attractions, and the protein chains unfold and tangle. The chains themselves mostly stay whole.

## Naming an unknown solid

The properties point back to the kind of solid. Test conductivity first, because it sorts the most cleanly. Then look at the melting point and the hardness. Here is how that works on four unknowns.

<Worked title="Four unknown solids">

Four unlabeled solids are tested. What kind of solid is each?

| Solid | Melts at | Conducts as a solid? | Conducts melted or dissolved? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 681 °C | no | yes |
| B | 232 °C | yes | yes |
| C | about 1700 °C | no | no |
| D | 167 °C | no | no |

What else you see:

- A: the crystals shatter when struck. It conducts both melted and dissolved in water.
- B: a shiny, silvery solid that bends without breaking. It conducts when melted; it does not dissolve in water.
- C: very hard; does not dissolve in water.
- D: soft crystals; dissolves in water, but the solution does not conduct.

1. **Conducts as a solid.** Only B does, so B is metallic. Its fairly low melting point does not change that: metals range from −39 °C to over 3400 °C. Graphite is the one common exception to this test, a network solid that conducts as a solid, but graphite does not bend the way B does.

2. **Conducts only once its particles can move.** A conducts when melted or dissolved but not as a solid. It is also brittle, with a high melting point. A is ionic.

3. **Never conducts, very high melting point, very hard.** C is a covalent network solid.

4. **Never conducts, low melting point.** D is molecular. Dissolving does not make it conduct, because it dissolves as whole, neutral molecules, not as ions. A few molecular substances, such as acids, do form ions in water; D is not one of them.

5. **The real substances.** A is potassium iodide, B is tin and C is quartz (silicon dioxide). D is mannitol, a sugar alcohol used as a sweetener.

The lesson of step 1: a melting point alone can mislead. Conductivity sorts first.

</Worked>

## Boiling, vapor pressure and melting

When a liquid boils, its particles separate completely, so every attraction between them must be overcome. That makes the boiling point a direct measure of how strong the forces are.

There is a second measure. In a closed container, molecules escape from the surface of a liquid into the space above it, and some of them return. Once escaping and returning balance, the pressure of the vapor is the liquid's **vapor pressure** at that temperature.

Weak forces let molecules escape easily. So, at a given temperature, weak forces mean a high vapor pressure, and they also mean a low boiling point. A liquid with a high vapor pressure is called **volatile**. Vapor pressure and boiling point run opposite to each other.

An ionic solid sits at the other extreme: its strong attractions let almost no particles escape, so its vapor pressure is very low.

Acetone and water show it. At 25 °C, acetone has a vapor pressure of about 230 torr, nearly ten times water's 24 torr. Acetone boils at 56 °C; water boils at 100 °C. Topic 3.3 shows how vapor pressure changes with temperature.

Melting is different. The particles stay in contact, and the attractions are only rearranged. So how well the particles pack into a crystal matters too, and melting points follow force strength less cleanly.

Two isomers of C₈H₁₀, with 58 electrons each, show it: ortho-xylene and para-xylene. Each is a ring of six carbons carrying two CH₃ groups. In o-xylene the two CH₃ groups sit on neighboring carbons of the ring; in p-xylene they sit on opposite sides. The o-isomer boils about 6 °C higher (144 °C against 138 °C), so its forces are a little stronger. Yet p-xylene melts about 38 °C higher (13 °C against −25 °C), because its more symmetrical molecules pack together better in a crystal.

To compare strengths of attraction, use boiling points or vapor pressures, not melting points.

<KeyIdea>

Stronger forces mean a higher boiling point and a lower vapor pressure. Melting points follow the same trend only roughly.

</KeyIdea>

<Trap>

Six mistakes to watch for on this topic:

- **Saying that melting or boiling a molecular substance breaks covalent bonds.** It separates molecules; the bonds inside them stay.
- **Calling every covalent network solid hard.** Graphite is soft, because its sheets slide.
- **Classifying by melting point alone.** Tin melts at 232 °C and is a metal. Test conductivity first, and remember the one common exception: graphite is a network solid that conducts as a solid.
- **Reading vapor pressure the wrong way round.** A high vapor pressure means weak forces and a low boiling point.
- **Saying an ionic solid conducts as a solid.** It conducts only when melted or dissolved.
- **Ranking forces by melting point.** Melting depends on packing too; use boiling points.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Ionic solids are ions held by opposite charges: high melting, brittle, and conducting only when melted or dissolved.
- Metallic solids are ions in a sea of electrons: they conduct as solids and are malleable and ductile, and interstitial alloys are less so.
- Covalent network solids are atoms bonded throughout: very high melting and usually hard, but graphite is soft because its sheets slide.
- Molecular solids are separate molecules held by intermolecular forces: low melting and nonconducting, and melting them never breaks covalent bonds.
- Stronger forces mean a higher boiling point and a lower vapor pressure, while melting points follow less cleanly.
- The shape of a large biomolecule, held by noncovalent attractions, decides how it behaves.

</KeyPoints>
