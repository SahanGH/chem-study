Pour cooking oil into a glass of water and stir as hard as you like. The moment you stop, the oil gathers into a layer on top. Pour in rubbing alcohol (2-propanol) instead, and it disappears into a single clear liquid. Both liquids went into the same water. What differs is how their molecules get along with water molecules.

## Dissolving is a trade of attractions

For a solute to dissolve, three things have to happen:

1. Solute particles are pulled away from one another. Their attractions are broken.
2. Solvent molecules are pushed apart to make room. Some of their attractions are broken too.
3. Solute and solvent particles attract each other. New attractions form.

Dissolving goes well when the new solute–solvent attractions are about as strong as the ones that were broken. It goes badly when they are much weaker.

Take oil and water. Water molecules hold on to one another by hydrogen bonds. An oil molecule is mostly long hydrocarbon chains, and a hydrocarbon chain can attract a water molecule only weakly, through London dispersion and dipole–induced dipole forces (topic 3.1). To let the oil in, water molecules would have to give up strong hydrogen bonds and get weak attractions in return. So the water molecules stay together, and the oil is left in a layer of its own.

The oil is not repelled by the water. Oil molecules and water molecules do attract, just far less than water molecules attract each other. And the oil is on top only because it is less dense. Density decides which layer floats, not whether the two liquids mix.

Now 2-propanol. Its molecule is a chain of three carbons with an O–H group on the middle one. That O–H group forms hydrogen bonds with water, about as strong as the ones water gives up. The trade costs little, so the two liquids mix. Two liquids that mix in any proportion are called **miscible**.

The short rule is **"like dissolves like"**: substances with similar intermolecular forces tend to mix. The rule is shorthand. The reason is the trade of attractions.

## Ionic and polar solutes in water

An ionic solid dissolves in water when the ion–dipole attractions (topic 3.1) between its ions and the water molecules can make up for pulling the ions apart. Topic 3.8 shows the water molecules turned toward each ion.

Many ionic compounds dissolve in water, but not all. In some, such as calcium carbonate (limestone), the ions attract each other too strongly. Units 4 and 7 come back to which ones dissolve.

A molecular solute with many O–H groups for its size dissolves well in water too. Table sugar (sucrose) is one: each molecule forms many hydrogen bonds with the water around it.

## Nonpolar with nonpolar

A nonpolar solvent such as hexane, C₆H₁₄, offers only London dispersion forces. That is enough for other nonpolar substances, which are held together by the same kind of force. Oils mix with hexane, and hexane is used to pull the oil out of crushed soybeans. Iodine, I₂, dissolves in hexane to give a violet solution, while it barely dissolves in water.

For an ionic solid, hexane's attractions are far too weak. London dispersion attractions to hexane cannot pay for pulling ions apart, so salts do not dissolve in hexane.

<Worked title="Choosing a solvent">

Three substances are to be dissolved. For each one, choose water or hexane.

- Sodium bromide, NaBr.
- Limonene, C₁₀H₁₆, the oil in orange peel. It is a hydrocarbon.
- Menthol, C₁₀H₂₀O, the cooling compound in peppermint. It has ten carbons, with one O–H group on a ring of carbons.

1. **NaBr.** It is ionic. Water's polar molecules attract Na⁺ and Br⁻ by ion–dipole forces, strong enough to replace the attractions in the solid. Hexane offers only London dispersion forces, far too weak. Choose water.

2. **Limonene.** It is a hydrocarbon, held to its neighbors by London dispersion forces. Hexane offers the same kind, so trading one set of attractions for the other costs little. In water, limonene would break up hydrogen bonds and give back only weak attractions. Choose hexane.

3. **Menthol.** Its one O–H group can form a hydrogen bond with water. But ten carbons of nonpolar hydrocarbon outweigh it: most of the molecule can offer water only weak attractions. Menthol dissolves far better in hexane than in water. Choose hexane.

4. **The pattern.** In each case, the chosen solvent can form attractions with the solute about as strong as the ones broken: ion–dipole for NaBr, London dispersion for limonene and for most of the menthol molecule.

</Worked>

## Alcohols: a polar end on a nonpolar chain

An alcohol has two parts. Its O–H group hydrogen-bonds with water. Its hydrocarbon chain can offer water only weak attractions.

The small alcohols, methanol, ethanol and 1-propanol, are miscible with water: the O–H group is a large part of the molecule. As the chain grows, more and more of the molecule is nonpolar, and the solubility in water falls. At room temperature, 1-butanol, with four carbons, dissolves to about 7 g per 100 mL of water. 1-Pentanol, with five, dissolves to about 2 g, and 1-hexanol, with six, to about 0.6 g. In all six, the O–H group is on an end carbon.

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Explaining solubility with "like dissolves like" and stopping there.** Name the attractions that are broken and the attractions that form.
- **Saying oil does not dissolve because it is less dense than water.** Density decides which layer floats. The reason is water's hydrogen bonds.
- **Thinking every ionic compound dissolves in water.** In some, the ions attract each other too strongly.
- **Calling any molecule with an O–H group soluble in water.** A long nonpolar chain outweighs one O–H group: 1-hexanol dissolves only slightly.
- **Saying the covalent bonds of sugar break when it dissolves.** Dissolving separates molecules from one another. The molecules stay whole.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Dissolving breaks solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions and forms solute–solvent attractions.
- A solute dissolves well when the new attractions are about as strong as those broken, so substances with similar intermolecular forces tend to mix.
- Many ionic compounds dissolve in water through ion–dipole forces, and small polar molecules with O–H groups through hydrogen bonds. Ionic compounds do not dissolve in a nonpolar solvent.
- Nonpolar molecular substances dissolve in nonpolar solvents such as hexane.
- As an alcohol's carbon chain grows, its solubility in water falls.

</KeyPoints>
