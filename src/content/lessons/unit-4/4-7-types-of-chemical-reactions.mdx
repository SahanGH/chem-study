A roll of black-and-white film carries a thin layer of tiny light-sensitive crystals, mostly silver bromide. They are made by mixing two solutions, so that silver ions and bromide ions join into a solid. In the darkroom, a developer hands electrons to silver ions, and the crystals that light has struck turn to dark silver metal long before the rest do. Then the film goes into a stop bath, which is a dilute acid. The acid gives protons to the developer, which is basic, and the developing stops. That is three kinds of reaction in one photograph. They are the three the exam asks you to recognize, and they are sorted by what moves.

## Three kinds, sorted by what moves

To sort a reaction, ask what passed from one particle to another. Chemists call any atom, molecule, ion or ionic compound that takes part in a reaction a **species**, so the question is what passed from one species to another. There are three answers to know.

- **Acid–base:** one or more protons, H⁺, pass from one species to another. A proton is a hydrogen atom without its one electron.
- **Oxidation–reduction** (redox for short): one or more electrons pass from one species to another.
- **Precipitation:** ions in a solution join into an ionic compound that does not stay dissolved, and it comes out as a solid.

<KeyIdea>

To name the kind of reaction, ask what moved. A proton went from one species to another: acid–base. Electrons went from one species to another: oxidation–reduction. Dissolved ions came together as a solid: precipitation.

</KeyIdea>

The next three sections take them one at a time, starting with the one that leaves a solid you can see.

## Precipitation: ions join into a solid

A chemist pours a solution of sodium sulfide into a solution of cadmium sulfate. A bright yellow solid appears at once and settles. It is cadmium sulfide, CdS, which is insoluble, the same compound as the painter's pigment cadmium yellow. That solid is a **precipitate**, the name topic 4.1 gave to a solid that forms out of a solution.

Written out in full, as a molecular equation (topic 4.2), the reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CdSO4(aq) + Na2S(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CdS(s) + Na2SO4(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sodium ions and the sulfate ions are spectators. They were dissolved before, and they are dissolved after. Take them out, and the net ionic equation is

$$
\ce{Cd^2+(aq) + S^2-(aq) -> CdS(s)}
$$

"Insoluble" does not mean that nothing at all dissolves. It means that so little dissolves that the solid comes out of solution when the ions meet at any ordinary concentration. Some compounds are **sparingly soluble** (you will also see "slightly soluble", which means the same). A little more of them dissolves, and they still precipitate unless the solutions are dilute. Topic 7.11 puts numbers on both.

**Which combinations are insoluble?** The exam expects one rule from memory: treat every sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salt as soluble in water. Any other solubility you need is given to you, in words ("cadmium sulfide is insoluble") or in an observation ("a yellow solid forms"). No other solubility needs memorizing.

**Using the rule.** When two solutions of ionic compounds are mixed, the cation of each can meet the anion of the other. To find out whether a precipitate can form:

1. Write down the two new pairings: the cation of each solution with the anion of the other.
2. Ask the rule about each pairing. One that holds Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻ is treated as soluble, so it is not the precipitate.
3. If the rule clears both pairings, no precipitate forms.
4. If it leaves a pairing open, the rule cannot tell you about that one. Look for its solubility in the question.

When no precipitate forms, and no proton and no electron can move either, the four ions usually just share the water. Then the right answer is "**no reaction**".

<Check answer={4}>
Each of these pairs of solutions is mixed. Using only the rule about sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts, for which pair can you be sure that no precipitate forms?

- Cadmium nitrate and sodium sulfide
- Strontium bromide and potassium chromate
- Calcium nitrate and potassium fluoride
- Sodium chromate and ammonium bromide

<div slot="why">

Swap the partners in each pair and look at the two new compounds.

Sodium chromate and ammonium bromide could only make sodium bromide and ammonium chromate. One holds Na⁺ and the other holds NH₄⁺, so the rule says both are soluble. No precipitate forms.

In each of the other pairs, one new compound is covered by the rule (sodium nitrate, potassium bromide, potassium nitrate) and the other is not (cadmium sulfide, strontium chromate, calcium fluoride). The rule says nothing about those three, so you cannot be sure without more information.

In fact all three precipitate. Cadmium sulfide is the yellow solid above. Calcium fluoride is insoluble. Strontium chromate is sparingly soluble, so it comes out as a yellow solid unless the solutions are dilute. A question about them would tell you so.

## Acid–base: a proton moves

Nitrous acid, HNO₂, is a weak acid: in water, only some of its molecules give up their proton. Potassium hydroxide is a strong base: in water it is K⁺ ions and OH⁻ ions. Mix their solutions and there is nothing to see, though the mixture warms a little. The net ionic equation (topic 4.2) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HNO2(aq) + OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{NO2^-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Nitrous acid is written whole because it is weak. The potassium ion is a spectator.

Compare each species before and after. HNO₂ became NO₂⁻. It has one H fewer and its charge is one lower, so it lost exactly H⁺. OH⁻ became H₂O. It has one H more and its charge is one higher, so it gained H⁺. A proton passed from the nitrous acid to the hydroxide ion. That is the whole test for an acid–base reaction: one species loses H⁺ and another gains it.

The proton does not have to land on a hydroxide ion, and water does not have to form. Any species that takes the proton will do, and topic 4.8 looks at which ones can.

## Oxidation–reduction: electrons move

Drip orange bromine water, which is bromine dissolved in water, into a colorless solution of potassium iodide. The mixture turns brown, the color of iodine.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2I^-(aq) + Br2(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{I2(aq) + 2Br^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The potassium ion is a spectator.

Each iodide ion gave up one electron, and the bromine molecule took the two of them. The species that loses electrons is **oxidized**. The species that gains them is **reduced**. Electrons go from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced. The two always happen together: an electron that leaves one species has to land on another.

Here the charges show it: the iodide ions lost their negative charge, and the bromine atoms each gained one. Often the charges do not show it, because in molecules electrons are shared, and nothing in the equation carries a charge. So chemists keep the books with oxidation numbers.

**Beyond the exam:** the species that is reduced is also called the oxidizing agent, because it takes electrons from something else, and the species that is oxidized is called the reducing agent. The exam does not test these two names.

## Oxidation numbers

An **oxidation number** is the charge an atom would have if the shared electrons in each of its bonds were handed over completely to the more electronegative atom (topic 2.1). Electrons shared by two atoms of the same element are split evenly between them. For a one-atom ion the oxidation number is the real charge. For an atom in a molecule or in a polyatomic ion it is bookkeeping, not a real charge.

Chemists assign them with a short list of rules. Neither the exam's periodic table nor its equation sheet gives the rules, so you need to know them. Work down the list from the top. Once the rules you have used so far settle an atom's number, it stays: a rule further down the list does not change it.

1. An atom of an element on its own (Co, O₂, Br₂) is 0.
2. A one-atom ion is its charge: Cd²⁺ is +2 and I⁻ is −1. That covers the ions of an ionic compound too: in KBr, potassium is +1 and bromine is −1.
3. The oxidation numbers of all the atoms add up to the charge of the species: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for a polyatomic ion.
4. Fluorine in a compound is −1.
5. Hydrogen is +1 when it is bonded to a nonmetal, as in H₂O, NH₃ and the OH of a hydroxide. It is −1 when it is bonded directly to a metal, as in a metal hydride such as NaH.
6. Oxygen is −2, except in peroxides, where it is −1. (A peroxide has two oxygen atoms bonded to each other, as in hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂. Unless you are told that a compound is a peroxide, take oxygen as −2.)

Here are five short examples. In each sum, $x$ is the number you are looking for.

**V₂O₅**, which is used in making sulfuric acid. Oxygen is −2, so

$$
2x + 5(-2) = 0
$$

and each vanadium is +5.

**The iodate ion, IO₃⁻**, the form of iodine added to table salt in many countries. Oxygen is −2 again, so

$$
x + 3(-2) = -1
$$

and iodine is +5. The sum is the ion's charge, not zero.

**Sodium hydride, NaH.** Here the hydrogen is bonded directly to a metal. Sodium forms Na⁺, which is +1, so hydrogen is −1.

**Oxygen difluoride, OF₂.** Fluorine is −1 by rule 4, and with the sum that settles the oxygen before rule 6 is reached:

$$
x + 2(-1) = 0
$$

So oxygen is +2.

**Calcium peroxide, CaO₂.** Calcium forms Ca²⁺, which is +2, so

$$
(+2) + 2x = 0
$$

and each oxygen is −1. That is the −1 of rule 6.

<Check answer={2}>
Sodium dithionite, Na₂S₂O₄, is a bleach used on wood pulp and for stripping dye out of cloth. It holds the dithionite ion, S₂O₄²⁻. What is the oxidation number of each sulfur atom in the ion?

- +6
- +3
- +4
- +5

<div slot="why">

Oxygen is −2, and the numbers add up to the ion's charge:

$$
2x + 4(-2) = -2
$$

So $2x = +6$, and each sulfur is +3.

Stopping at +6 gives the total for both sulfur atoms, not the number for each. Setting the sum to 0 in place of −2 gives +4. Taking the charge as +2 gives +5.

The two sulfur atoms in this ion are alike, so +3 is the number of each.

</div>
</Check>

## Finding what was oxidized

Assign an oxidation number to every atom on both sides of the equation, then compare. An atom whose number went **up** lost electrons: it was oxidized. An atom whose number went **down** gained electrons: it was reduced. The species named as oxidized or reduced is the reactant that holds that atom. When every atom ends with the number it started with, no electrons were passed along, and the reaction is not an oxidation–reduction.

<Worked title="Inside a watch battery">

Many watches run on a silver oxide button cell. While it runs the watch, this reaction goes on inside it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn(s) + Ag2O(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{ZnO(s) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Is this an oxidation–reduction reaction? If so, which species is oxidized and which is reduced?

1. **Number the reactants.** Zinc metal is an element, so it is 0. In Ag₂O, oxygen is −2, so

   $$
   2x + (-2) = 0
   $$

   and each silver is +1.

2. **Number the products.** In ZnO, oxygen is −2, so

   $$
   x + (-2) = 0
   $$

   and zinc is +2. Silver metal is an element, so it is 0.

3. **Compare.** Oxygen is −2 before and after. Zinc went from 0 to +2. Each silver went from +1 to 0.

4. **Name the species.** A rise is a loss of electrons, so Zn is oxidized. A fall is a gain, so Ag₂O is reduced. Numbers changed, so this is an oxidation–reduction reaction, and electrons pass from Zn to Ag₂O.

5. **Count the electrons.** The one zinc atom loses 2 electrons. Each of the two silver atoms gains 1, which makes 2 gained. Electrons lost equal electrons gained, as they must. (Topic 4.9 turns that into a way of balancing equations.)

</Worked>

Nothing in this equation carries a charge, so it takes the oxidation numbers to show that electrons moved. Inside the cell the zinc and the silver oxide are kept apart, so the electrons have to travel from one to the other through a wire, and they run the watch on the way. Unit 9 is about that.

## Combustion

**Combustion** is an important kind of oxidation–reduction: a substance reacts with oxygen gas, O₂. The oxygen starts at 0 and, in the examples here, ends in compounds at −2, so oxygen is reduced and the fuel is oxidized.

A **hydrocarbon** is a compound of carbon and hydrogen only. When a hydrocarbon burns completely, with plenty of oxygen, the products are carbon dioxide and water and nothing else. A welder's torch burns acetylene, C₂H₂:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2C2H2(g) + 5O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{4CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

In C₂H₂ hydrogen is +1, so

$$
2x + 2(+1) = 0
$$

and each carbon is −1. In CO₂ carbon is +4. Its number rose, so the acetylene is oxidized. Oxygen goes from 0 to −2, so it is reduced. Hydrogen stays +1.

Count the electrons. The 4 carbon atoms lose 5 each, which is 20 lost. The 10 oxygen atoms gain 2 each, which is 20 gained.

Other fuels burn too, and what they give depends on the elements in them.

<Check answer={3}>
Ammonia, NH₃, is being tried as a fuel for cargo ships because it holds no carbon. When it burns cleanly, the reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{4NH3(g) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2N2(g) + 6H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Which statement is correct?

- It is not a combustion, because no carbon dioxide is formed at all
- It is a combustion, and hydrogen is oxidized, from 0 to +1
- It is a combustion, and nitrogen is oxidized, from −3 to 0
- It is a combustion, and oxygen is oxidized, from 0 to −2

<div slot="why">

Burning in oxygen gas is what makes a reaction a combustion. Carbon dioxide comes from the carbon in a fuel, and ammonia has no carbon to give.

In NH₃ hydrogen is +1, so

$$
x + 3(+1) = 0
$$

and nitrogen is −3. In N₂ it is 0. Its number rose, so the ammonia is oxidized.

Oxygen went from 0 to −2. That is a fall, which is reduction, not oxidation. Hydrogen is +1 on both sides.

Count the electrons. The 4 nitrogen atoms lose 3 each, which is 12 lost. The 6 oxygen atoms gain 2 each, which is 12 gained.

</div>
</Check>

## Telling them apart

Sorting a reaction takes three questions, one for each kind. Ask all three, because a reaction can be more than one kind, and it can be none.

Try them on a reaction this lesson has not sorted yet. A piece of cobalt metal is dropped into dilute hydrochloric acid, a strong acid: in water it is H⁺ ions and Cl⁻ ions. The cobalt slowly dissolves. Bubbles of hydrogen rise from it, and the solution turns pink. The net ionic equation is

$$
\ce{Co(s) + 2H^+(aq) -> Co^2+(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

The chloride ion is a spectator.

**Did dissolved ions join into a solid?** No. The one solid in the equation is the cobalt, and it is used up, not formed. This is not precipitation.

**Did a proton pass from one species to another?** With an acid in the beaker, it is tempting to say yes. But follow the H⁺ ions. For a proton to have been passed, some species would have to gain H⁺: one more H, and a charge one higher. None does. The cobalt ends as Co²⁺, with no hydrogen on it, and the H⁺ ions end up as H₂, which has no charge. This is not acid–base, and an acid in the equation does not make it so.

**Did any oxidation number change?** Yes. Cobalt went from 0 to +2, and hydrogen went from +1 to 0. The hydrogen ions took electrons from the cobalt. This is oxidation–reduction.

**Say why.** On the exam, naming the kind is half the answer. The other half is the evidence in the equation: what moved, from which species, to which. A model answer for this reaction reads: "This is oxidation–reduction, because cobalt's oxidation number rises from 0 to +2 and hydrogen's falls from +1 to 0, so electrons pass from Co to H⁺."

<Check answer={1}>
Here are four reactions. A is zinc carbonate, a zinc ore that holds Zn²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ ions, being heated in a furnace. B is tin(II) chloride solution added to iron(III) chloride solution. C is methylamine, CH₃NH₂, a weak base, meeting the hydrogen sulfate ion, HSO₄⁻, a weak acid. D is strontium chloride solution mixed with potassium chromate solution; strontium chromate is sparingly soluble.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{A}\quad & \ce{ZnCO3(s) ->} \\
& \quad \ce{ZnO(s) + CO2(g)} \\[1em]
\textbf{B}\quad & \ce{2Fe^3+(aq) + Sn^2+(aq) ->} \\
& \quad \ce{2Fe^2+(aq) + Sn^4+(aq)} \\[1em]
\textbf{C}\quad & \ce{HSO4^-(aq) + CH3NH2(aq) ->} \\
& \quad \ce{SO4^2-(aq) + CH3NH3^+(aq)} \\[1em]
\textbf{D}\quad & \ce{Sr^2+(aq) + CrO4^2-(aq) ->} \\
& \quad \ce{SrCrO4(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Which one is none of the three kinds: not acid–base, not oxidation–reduction and not precipitation?

- Equation A
- Equation B
- Equation C
- Equation D

<div slot="why">

Equation A is none of them. In zinc carbonate the zinc is the ion Zn²⁺, so it is +2 (rule 2). With oxygen at −2, that leaves carbon at +4 in the carbonate ion, the same as in CO₂. In ZnO the zinc is still +2 and the oxygen −2. No number changed, so no electrons moved. There is no hydrogen in the equation, so no proton moved. And the solid and the gas come from heating a solid, not from ions meeting in a solution. It is a chemical change all the same: one substance became two.

Equation B is oxidation–reduction. Sn²⁺ becomes Sn⁴⁺, losing 2 electrons, and each of the two Fe³⁺ ions gains one.

Equation C is acid–base, though there is no hydroxide ion in it and no water forms. HSO₄⁻ lost H⁺ and became SO₄²⁻. CH₃NH₂ gained it and became CH₃NH₃⁺. The charges changed because the proton carried its +1 charge with it. No oxidation number changed: sulfur is +6, oxygen −2 and hydrogen +1 on both sides. The rules cannot split carbon from nitrogen in CH₃NH₂ or in CH₃NH₃⁺, but they do give the two together: −5 before and −5 after. Nothing happened to those two atoms except that a proton arrived, and it arrived as +1 and stays +1.

Equation D is precipitation: dissolved ions joined into a sparingly soluble solid. Strontium is +2 before and after, and the chromate ion is unchanged.

</div>
</Check>

**Not every reaction is one of the three.** Heat can break a compound apart with no proton and no electron changing hands, as in Equation A of the last question. Such a reaction is still a chemical change. It just has none of these three names.

<Check answer={3}>
When blue copper(II) sulfate solution is mixed with colorless potassium iodide solution, a pale solid, copper(I) iodide, settles out, and the liquid turns brown. The net ionic equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2Cu^2+(aq) + 4I^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CuI(s) + I2(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A student writes "precipitation" and stops. What is the best comment on that answer?

- It is complete, because a solid formed from ions in solution and nothing else changed
- It is wrong, because no solid can form when every potassium salt is soluble
- It is incomplete, because copper goes from +2 to +1, so electrons moved as well
- It is incomplete, because a proton also moved, from an iodide ion to a copper ion

<div slot="why">

A solid did form from dissolved ions, so "precipitation" is true as far as it goes. Now check the oxidation numbers.

In CuI the iodine is the iodide ion, I⁻, so it is −1 (rule 2), and the copper is +1. Copper went from +2 to +1 and was reduced. Of the four iodide ions, two are still −1 in the solid, and two became I₂, at 0. Those two were oxidized. Two electrons were lost and two were gained.

So the reaction is oxidation–reduction as well, and the brown color is the iodine.

The rule about potassium salts does not apply to the solid, which holds no potassium. And there is no hydrogen anywhere in the equation, so no proton can have moved.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Six mistakes to watch for on this topic:

- **Calling a reaction acid–base because an acid is in it.** Cobalt in hydrochloric acid is oxidation–reduction. The H⁺ ions take electrons, and no proton is passed to another species.
- **Reading a change of charge as a transfer of electrons.** HSO₄⁻ becomes SO₄²⁻ by losing a proton. Check oxidation numbers, not the charges of whole species.
- **Setting the sum to zero for an ion.** The numbers add up to the charge: iodine in IO₃⁻ is +5, not +6.
- **Calling a fall in oxidation number oxidation.** Down is reduced, and up is oxidized.
- **Expecting carbon dioxide from every combustion.** CO₂ and H₂O are the products for a hydrocarbon burned completely. Ammonia, burned cleanly, gives N₂ and H₂O.
- **Forcing every reaction into one kind.** A reaction can be two of the three, or none.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- An acid–base reaction passes one or more protons, H⁺, from one species to another.
- An oxidation–reduction reaction passes one or more electrons from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced. It shows as oxidation numbers that change: up for the atom oxidized, down for the atom reduced.
- Oxidation numbers follow six rules, and in every species they add up to its charge.
- Combustion is an oxidation–reduction reaction with oxygen gas, and a hydrocarbon burned completely gives only carbon dioxide and water.
- A precipitation reaction joins dissolved ions into an insoluble or sparingly soluble solid. For the exam, treat sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts as soluble, and expect any other solubility to be given.
- To justify the kind, say what moved, from which species, to which.

</KeyPoints>
