Light a gas burner, and countless molecules are being pulled apart and put back together in front of you. You cannot see one of them. A chemical equation is one way to say what they are doing. A **particle drawing** is another: a before-and-after picture of a few of the molecules, small enough to count. On the exam you go both ways, from an equation to a drawing and from a drawing to an equation.

## What the drawing has to agree with

The exam asks for a drawing that is consistent with the equation, whichever form the equation is written in: molecular, complete ionic or net ionic (topic 4.2). A drawing is **consistent** with an equation when it obeys the same rules the equation does. Here are those rules, in the order you would use them to make a drawing. They are what a drawing is marked on.

1. **Start with the right particles.** A dissolved ionic compound goes in as separate ions, as many of each as its formula gives (topic 3.8).
2. **React them in the ratio of the coefficients.** The numbers of particles used up and formed stand in the same proportions as the coefficients do.
3. **Pack a solid that forms into a cluster.** The ions of a precipitate, a solid that forms out of a solution (topic 4.1), are drawn together in one place. Every ion touches ions of the opposite charge, and no two cations or two anions touch.
4. **Draw what was left over.** A reactant that did not all react still has particles in the "after" box.
5. **Draw the ions that took no part.** Spectator ions are dissolved before the reaction and after it, unchanged (topic 4.2). They are drawn in the "after" box as they were before, dissolved and apart.
6. **Count the atoms.** Each element has the same number of atoms in both boxes.
7. **Add up the charges.** The total is the same before and after.

<KeyIdea>

A drawing agrees with its equation when every atom in the "before" box is in the "after" box, the total charge is unchanged, the particles that reacted did so in the ratio of the coefficients, and whatever did not react is still there.

</KeyIdea>

Try the rules on a reaction between molecules. Ethene, C₂H₄, is a small hydrocarbon gas. (It is the gas that makes fruit ripen.) It burns in oxygen:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C2H4(g) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The box labeled after holds 4 carbon dioxide molecules, 4 water molecules and 1 oxygen molecule left over. Each box holds 4 carbon atoms, 8 hydrogen atoms and 14 oxygen atoms."
  caption="Ethene burning, before and after. Two ethene molecules use up six oxygen molecules; the seventh is left over, and every atom is still there."

Read the two boxes against the rules.

- **Before:** 2 C₂H₄ and 7 O₂.
- **After:** 4 CO₂, 4 H₂O and 1 O₂.
- **The atoms.** Before, the box holds 4 C, 8 H and 14 O. After, the 4 C are in the CO₂ and the 8 H are in the H₂O. The O atoms are 8 in the CO₂, 4 in the H₂O and 2 in the O₂, which makes 14. Nothing made, nothing lost.
- **The ratio.** 2 C₂H₄ and 6 O₂ were used up, and 4 CO₂ and 4 H₂O formed. Divide 2, 6, 4 and 4 by 2 and you have the coefficients: 1, 3, 2 and 2.
- **The leftover.** The ethene ran out first. The one O₂ molecule that had nothing to react with is still in the box. It is not part of the equation, but it is part of the drawing: it goes in the "after" box.

That is also how you read a pair of drawings backward, into an equation. Count what was used up and what formed, leave out what is the same in both boxes, and reduce to the smallest whole numbers.

Lightning turns some of the oxygen in air into ozone, O₃. A drawing of a sample of pure oxygen before a spark shows 9 O₂ molecules. The drawing after the spark shows 4 O₃ molecules and 3 O₂ molecules. Which equation do the two drawings represent?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{A}\quad & \ce{9O2(g) -> 4O3(g) + 3O2(g)} \\[1.4em]
\textbf{B}\quad & \ce{O2(g) -> O3(g)} \\[1.4em]
\textbf{C}\quad & \ce{3O2(g) -> 2O3(g)} \\[1.4em]
\textbf{D}\quad & \ce{2O3(g) -> 3O2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

- Equation A
- Equation B
- Equation C
- Equation D

<div slot="why">

Count what was used up and what formed. 9 − 3 = 6 O₂ molecules were used up, and 4 O₃ formed. Dividing 6 and 4 by 2 gives 3 O₂ for every 2 O₃, which is Equation C.

The 3 O₂ molecules still in the box did not react, so they do not belong in the equation: Equation A has them on both sides. Equation B is not balanced, with 2 O atoms on the left and 3 on the right. Equation D is the same change run backward, and the drawings show ozone forming, not breaking down.

The atom count agrees: 18 O atoms before, and 12 + 6 = 18 after.

</div>
</Check>

## Ions in solution, and a solid that forms

Many reactions on the exam happen in water, between ions. The drawing then has more to get right: which particles are apart, which are packed together, and which are only watching.

The drawings here follow topic 3.8. A one-atom ion is one circle, and a polyatomic ion is one labeled particle. Cations are dark and anions are pale. The sizes are not to scale. Water molecules are left out of every box, so that the ions can be counted.

<Worked title="Drawing a precipitation">

Manganese(II) carbonate is a pale powder that is added to fertilizers for crops that are short of manganese. It is made by mixing a solution of manganese(II) sulfate with a solution of sodium carbonate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MnSO4(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{MnCO3(s) + Na2SO4(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Manganese(II) carbonate is insoluble. Sodium sulfate is soluble, by the rule of topic 4.7: treat every sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salt as soluble.

A box of the manganese(II) sulfate solution is drawn with 3 formula units, and a box of the sodium carbonate solution with 2. (A formula unit is one set of the ions in a compound's formula, as in topic 3.8.) Draw the mixture after the reaction.

1. **Draw each solution as separate ions.** 3 MnSO₄ give 3 Mn²⁺ and 3 SO₄²⁻. 2 Na₂CO₃ give 4 Na⁺ and 2 CO₃²⁻: two sodium ions for every carbonate, as the formula says. Each polyatomic ion is one particle.

2. **Find what reacts.** Take the spectator ions, Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻, out of the equation. What is left is the net ionic equation:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Mn^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{MnCO3(s)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Use the ratio.** One Mn²⁺ joins one CO₃²⁻. There are only 2 CO₃²⁻, so 2 Mn²⁺ react. The carbonate runs out, and 1 Mn²⁺ is left over.

4. **Draw the solid as a cluster.** Put 2 Mn²⁺ and 2 CO₃²⁻ at the bottom of the box, packed together. Every ion touches ions of the opposite charge, and no two cations or two anions touch.

5. **Put back everything that did not react.** That is 1 Mn²⁺, 3 SO₄²⁻ and 4 Na⁺, each on its own, apart from the others.

6. **Check the atoms.** Mn: 3 before; 2 in the solid and 1 dissolved after. CO₃: 2 and 2. SO₄: 3 and 3. Na: 4 and 4.

7. **Check the charge.** Before mixing, the ions of the two solutions give

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &3(+2) + 3(-2) \\
   &\quad {} + 4(+1) + 2(-2) = 0
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   After, the dissolved ions give

   $$
   (+2) + 3(-2) + 4(+1) = 0
   $$

   and the solid gives

   $$
   2(+2) + 2(-2) = 0
   $$

</Worked>

A box of barium nitrate solution is drawn with 4 Ba²⁺ ions and 8 NO₃⁻ ions. A box of sodium phosphate solution is drawn with 6 Na⁺ ions and 2 PO₄³⁻ ions. The two are mixed, and insoluble barium phosphate forms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{3Ba^2+(aq) + 2PO4^3-(aq) ->} \\

In a correct drawing of the mixture after the reaction, how many ions are still dissolved, on their own?

<div slot="why">

The ratio is 3 Ba²⁺ for every 2 PO₄³⁻. The 2 PO₄³⁻ take 3 Ba²⁺, and then the phosphate has run out. The solid is one cluster of 3 Ba²⁺ and 2 PO₄³⁻.

$$
1 + 8 + 6 = 15
$$

The charge agrees. The dissolved ions give

$$
(+2) + 8(-1) + 6(+1) = 0
$$

and the solid gives

$$
3(+2) + 2(-3) = 0
$$

## Where the marks are lost

The same few mistakes turn up in these drawings again and again. The next figure has three of them in one box. A student's drawing of the manganese carbonate mixture is on the left, and the corrected one is on the right.

<Figure
  src="/figures/4-3-wrong-and-right.svg"
  alt="Two boxes showing the mixture after manganese sulfate and sodium carbonate solutions react. In the student's drawing on the left, only 2 sodium ions are drawn, marked 1; one carbonate is drawn as a separate carbon atom and three separate oxygen atoms, marked 2; and in the solid at the bottom the 2 manganese ions touch each other, marked 3. The box also holds 1 whole carbonate ion in the solid, and 1 manganese ion and 3 sulfate ions dissolved. In the corrected drawing on the right, 2 manganese ions and 2 carbonate ions are packed together at the bottom with each manganese ion next to carbonate ions, and 1 manganese ion, 3 sulfate ions and 4 sodium ions are scattered above, apart from one another."
  caption="Three mistakes in one drawing, and the drawing that fixes them. The student's box is neutral all the same, so count atoms as well as charge. Water molecules are left out."
/>

The numbers in the small squares mark the mistakes.

1. Only 2 Na⁺ are drawn, one for each carbonate. The formula Na₂CO₃ puts two sodium ions with every carbonate ion, so 2 formula units give 4 Na⁺.
2. One carbonate is drawn as four separate atoms, a carbon atom and three oxygen atoms, none of them joined to another. A polyatomic ion that is only dissolving (topic 3.8) or joining a solid, as carbonate is here, stays in one piece and keeps its charge.
3. In the solid, the two Mn²⁺ touch each other. A solid salt is held together by the attraction between opposite charges, so a cation touches anions, not another cation.

Now add up the charges in the student's box. It is neutral:

$$
3(+2) + (-2) + 3(-2) + 2(+1) = 0
$$

The −2 that was lost when a carbonate was drawn as uncharged atoms happens to cancel the +2 of the two missing sodium ions. So a check of the charge alone would have passed this drawing. Count the atoms as well: there are 2 Na where there should be 4.

    3: "Spectator ions take no part in the reaction, so they stay dissolved. They do not leave the box.",

>
To recover silver from a solution of silver nitrate, a jeweler hangs a strip of zinc in it. Each zinc atom gives up two electrons, and each silver ion takes one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn(s) + 2Ag+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Zn^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A student draws the box before the reaction with a strip of 4 Zn atoms, 4 Ag⁺ ions and 4 NO₃⁻ ions. The student's box after the reaction shows 4 Ag atoms as a solid, 4 Zn²⁺ ions, 4 NO₃⁻ ions and no zinc atoms. What is wrong with the box after the reaction?

- Charge is not conserved: the dissolved ions now add up to +4
- Silver is not conserved: the box has gained four silver atoms
- The nitrate ions should be gone, because they are spectator ions
- Nothing is wrong: every atom in the first box is in the second

<div slot="why">

The atoms do match: 4 zinc, 4 silver and 4 nitrate in each box. The charge does not. Before the reaction, the ions give

$$
4(+1) + 4(-1) = 0
$$

In the student's box after it, they give

$$
4(+2) + 4(-1) = +4
$$

Electrons cannot appear or disappear. Each zinc atom gives up 2 and each silver ion takes 1, so 4 Ag⁺ can take electrons from only 2 Zn atoms. The correct box holds 4 Ag atoms and 2 Zn atoms on the strip, and 2 Zn²⁺ and 4 NO₃⁻ dissolved:

$$
2(+2) + 4(-1) = 0
$$

The nitrate ions took no part, so all 4 are drawn in the box after the reaction, still dissolved.

</div>
</Check>

## A physical process is drawn the same way

A physical process can have an equation too (topic 4.2), and it can be drawn as particles before and after. In a physical process, the particles in the "after" box are the same ones that were in the "before" box. The exam can ask for that drawing as well.

<Check
  answer={4}
  misses={{
    1: "Gas molecules keep moving. What happens once the valve is open and each gas can reach more space?",
    2: "Every atom is there, but in new molecules. That would be a reaction, and these two gases do not react at room temperature.",
    3: "More space means the molecules are farther apart. Does it change how many there are?",
  }}
>
Two glass bulbs are joined by a closed valve. One bulb holds methane, CH₄, and the other holds carbon dioxide, CO₂. At room temperature these two gases do not react with each other. A drawing of the bulbs shows 4 CH₄ molecules in the left bulb and 4 CO₂ molecules in the right bulb. The valve is opened, and the bulbs are left for a long time. Which drawing shows the two bulbs then?

- 4 CH₄ molecules still in the left bulb and 4 CO₂ molecules still in the right
- 8 CO molecules and 8 H₂ molecules, spread through both bulbs
- 2 CH₄ molecules and 2 CO₂ molecules in all, because the gases now have twice the space
- 4 CH₄ molecules and 4 CO₂ molecules, every one whole, spread through both bulbs

<div slot="why">

Mixing is a physical process, so no particle changes and none is lost. Gas molecules keep moving, so each gas spreads through all the space it can reach. After a long time, both kinds of molecule are found in both bulbs: 4 CH₄ and 4 CO₂, every one whole.

The first option forgets that the molecules move. The second keeps every atom (8 C, 16 H and 8 O) but shows new molecules. That would be a reaction, and these two gases do not react at room temperature. The third loses half the molecules. More space means the molecules are farther apart, not that there are fewer of them.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Eight ways to lose marks on a particle drawing:

- **An atom in one box with no counterpart in the other.** Count each element in both boxes.
- **Leaving out the reactant that was left over.** The seventh O₂ molecule is drawn in the "after" box.
- **Writing leftovers into the equation.** When you read a drawing, a molecule of a reactant that was left over, the same in both boxes, is not part of the equation.
- **Drawing a dissolved salt the wrong way.** Topic 3.8 has the rules: its ions apart, as many of each as the formula gives, each polyatomic ion in one piece.
- **Drawing a precipitate as scattered ions, or with like charges touching.** A solid is a cluster: every ion touches ions of the opposite charge, and no two cations or two anions touch.
- **Erasing the spectator ions.** They took no part, so they are drawn after the reaction as they were before it, dissolved and apart.
- **Letting the charge change.** 4 Ag⁺ cannot turn 4 Zn into 4 Zn²⁺.
- **Changing the particles in a physical process.** Mixing methane with carbon dioxide gives a mixture of CH₄ and CO₂ molecules, not new molecules.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A particle drawing that is consistent with an equation shows the particles that are really there, with the same atoms and the same total charge in the "before" and "after" boxes.
- Particles react in the ratio of the coefficients, and a reactant that is left over is drawn in the "after" box.
- A dissolved ionic compound is drawn as separate ions, in the ratio of its formula, with each polyatomic ion whole.
- A precipitate is drawn as a cluster of ions packed together, and the spectator ions are still drawn, dissolved.
- To read a pair of drawings as an equation, count what was used up and what formed, leave out what did not change, and reduce to the smallest whole numbers.
- A physical process, such as two gases mixing or a change of phase, keeps every particle whole. Only where the particles are, how far apart they are and how they move can change.

</KeyPoints>
