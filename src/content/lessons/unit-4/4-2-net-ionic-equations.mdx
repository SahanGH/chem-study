Strontium carbonate is a white powder, a raw material of the ceramic magnets in loudspeakers and small electric motors. A chemist can make it by mixing a solution of strontium nitrate with a solution of potassium carbonate. Or strontium iodide with sodium carbonate. Or strontium chloride with potassium carbonate. That is three recipes and five different bottles, and it is one reaction. To see that it is one reaction, you need an equation that shows what is really there in the water. This lesson builds up to it.

## Every change has an equation

An equation puts what you start with on the left of an arrow and what you end with on the right. Any change can be written this way, physical or chemical.

An equation written out in full gives each formula a **state symbol**, a letter or two in parentheses that says what form the substance is in.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (s) | solid |
| (l) | liquid |
| (g) | gas |
| (aq) | dissolved in water |

Here are three changes and their equations.

Dew forming on a lawn is a change of phase:

$$
\ce{H2O(g) -> H2O(l)}
$$

The calcium chloride pellets in a closet moisture absorber dissolve in the water they pull out of the air:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CaCl2(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Ca^2+(aq) + 2Cl-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Baking soda breaks down in a hot oven, which helps lift a cookie:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2NaHCO3(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Na2CO3(s) + H2O(g) + CO2(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Two words to fix before going on. A **coefficient** is the number in front of a formula, and it says how many of that particle there are. A **subscript** is part of the formula, and it says what the substance is. In the baking soda equation, the 2 in front of NaHCO₃ is a coefficient and the 3 inside it is a subscript.

Write the state symbols whenever you can. They show what is dissolved, what is solid and what is a gas, and a question may ask for them.

## Balanced means the same atoms and the same charge

A physical or chemical change can rearrange atoms, but it never makes or destroys them. So a correct equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, and that is why mass is conserved. Electrons are not made or destroyed either, so the total charge is the same on both sides too.

Count the baking soda equation. On the left, 2 NaHCO₃ hold 2 Na, 2 H, 2 C and 6 O. On the right there are 2 Na, 2 H and 2 C again, and 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 O. Nothing in the equation has a charge, on either side.

Now the dissolving equation. Each side has 1 Ca and 2 Cl. The ions on the right carry charges, and they add up to

$$
(+2) + 2(-1) = 0
$$

which is the same as the uncharged solid on the left.

<KeyIdea>

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element, and the same total charge, on both sides. Nothing is made and nothing is lost. At most, the atoms are rearranged.

</KeyIdea>

## Balancing by inspection

**Balancing by inspection** means counting and adjusting until both sides agree. There is no formula for it, but a few rules make it quick.

1. Write the correct formula and state of every substance first. After that, change coefficients only. Change a subscript and you have written a different substance.
2. Start with an element that appears in only one formula on each side.
3. Leave for last an element that stands alone, such as the oxygen in O₂, or that appears in several formulas.
4. A polyatomic ion that is the same on both sides can be counted as one unit.
5. A coefficient that ends in a half is fine as a stepping stone. Double the whole equation and the half is gone, as the 11/2 is in the example below.
6. Finish by counting every element on both sides, and the charge. Use the smallest whole numbers.

<Worked title="Balancing by inspection">

Pyrite, FeS₂, is the brassy yellow mineral known as fool's gold. Roasted in air, it turns into iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃, and sulfur dioxide gas. That is one way the makers of sulfuric acid get their sulfur dioxide. Write the balanced equation.

1. **Write the formulas and states.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{FeS2(s) + O2(g) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Fe2O3(s) + SO2(g)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   From here on, only coefficients change.

2. **Start with iron.** It is in one formula on each side. Fe₂O₃ holds 2 Fe, so put a 2 in front of FeS₂.

3. **Then sulfur.** 2 FeS₂ hold 4 S, so put a 4 in front of SO₂.

4. **Leave oxygen for last.** It is in both products, and on the left it stands alone as O₂. The right side now has 3 O in Fe₂O₃ and 8 O in 4 SO₂. That makes 11 O atoms, which is eleven halves of an O₂.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{2FeS2(s) + 11/2 O2(g) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Fe2O3(s) + 4SO2(g)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Clear the fraction.** Multiply every coefficient by 2.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{4FeS2(s) + 11O2(g) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{2Fe2O3(s) + 8SO2(g)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Count.** Fe: 4 and 4. S: 8 and 8. O: 22 on the left, and 6 + 16 = 22 on the right. No charges on either side. The coefficients 4, 11, 2 and 8 have no common factor, so these are the smallest whole numbers.

</Worked>

<div slot="why">

Nitrogen is already balanced, 1 and 1. Now the charge. The left side is +1. On the right, NO₃⁻ is −1, so 2 H⁺ are needed:

$$
(-1) + 2(+1) = +1

\end{aligned}

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2NH4+(aq) + 3O2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2NO3-(aq) + 8H+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

It has the same atoms on both sides (2 N, 8 H, 6 O), but its charge goes from +2 to +6, so it is not balanced.

## What is really in the solution

The formula Sr(NO₃)₂(aq) is shorthand. A dissolved ionic compound is present as separate ions (topic 3.8), so the solution holds Sr²⁺ ions and NO₃⁻ ions, two nitrates for each strontium. An equation can show that. Here is what gets written as separate ions, and what does not.

| Written as separate ions | Written as one formula |
|---|---|
| A dissolved ionic compound, such as Na₂CO₃(aq) | A solid, a liquid or a gas, such as SrCO₃(s), H₂O(l) or CO₂(g) |
| A strong acid in water, such as HNO₃(aq) | A weak acid in water |
| A strong base in water, such as KOH(aq) | A weak base in water, such as NH₃(aq) |
| | A dissolved molecular substance that is not an acid or a base |

A **strong acid** is one in which essentially all of the molecules break up into ions in water. Nitric acid in water is H⁺ ions and NO₃⁻ ions, with essentially no HNO₃ molecules left. A **weak acid** gives up only some of its protons to water, for most weak acids a small fraction, so it is written whole.

The same goes for bases. A **strong base** is fully separated into ions in water, and OH⁻ is one of them. A **weak base**, such as ammonia, takes protons from water only in part, so it is written whole too.

The common strong acids are HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (its first proton) and HClO₄. The common strong bases are the hydroxides of the group 1 metals (LiOH, NaOH, KOH and the rest) and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂. Topic 8.2 returns to them. In this unit, a question tells you whether an acid or a base is strong or weak.

Water is a liquid, so it keeps its formula. Do not split it into ions.

When you split a compound, the subscript that counts an ion becomes the number of those ions: one Sr(NO₃)₂ gives one Sr²⁺ and two NO₃⁻. A polyatomic ion stays whole, so the 3 inside NO₃⁻ stays where it is.

Which ionic compounds dissolve? The rule to use is this: treat every sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salt as soluble (topic 4.7). For any other compound, the question says. From here on, this lesson says "by the rule".

## Three forms of one reaction

A reaction in solution can be written three ways.

The **molecular equation** writes every substance as a whole formula, as if nothing were split. It shows what was mixed.

The **complete ionic equation** writes every substance the way it is really present, with the dissolved ionic compounds, strong acids and strong bases as separate ions.

Some of those ions are dissolved and separate before the reaction, and dissolved and separate after it, unchanged. They are **spectator ions**. Take them out of both sides, and what is left is the **net ionic equation**: only the particles that changed.

Work in the same order every time: molecular equation, complete ionic equation, find the spectator ions, net ionic equation.

<Worked title="From a mixture to its net ionic equation">

In a laboratory, a few drops of sodium phosphate solution, Na₃PO₄, are added to a solution of silver nitrate, AgNO₃. A yellow solid forms at once: silver phosphate, which is insoluble. A solid that forms out of a solution is a **precipitate**. Write the molecular, complete ionic and net ionic equations. The ions in the two solutions are Ag⁺, NO₃⁻, Na⁺ and PO₄³⁻.

1. **Write the formulas of the products.** The ions trade partners. Ag⁺ with PO₄³⁻ needs three Ag⁺ for the charges to cancel: Ag₃PO₄, the yellow solid. Na⁺ with NO₃⁻ makes NaNO₃. Sodium nitrate is soluble, by the rule, so it stays dissolved.

2. **Balance the molecular equation.** Three Ag need 3 AgNO₃. Then three NO₃ need 3 NaNO₃, which also balances the 3 Na.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{3AgNO3(aq) + Na3PO4(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ag3PO4(s) + 3NaNO3(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Write the complete ionic equation.** Split the three dissolved ionic compounds into their ions, and leave the solid whole.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{3Ag+(aq) + 3NO3-(aq) +} \\
   &\quad \ce{3Na+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ag3PO4(s) + 3Na+(aq) +} \\
   &\quad \ce{3NO3-(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Find the spectator ions.** 3 Na⁺ and 3 NO₃⁻ are on both sides, dissolved and unchanged.

5. **Write the net ionic equation.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{3Ag+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ag3PO4(s)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Check atoms and charge.** There are 3 Ag, 1 P and 4 O on each side. The charges on the left add up to

   $$
   3(+1) + (-3) = 0
   $$

   and the solid on the right has no charge.

</Worked>

The net ionic equation tells you what actually happened, and it is the one the exam asks for most often.

## Different bottles, one reaction

Back to the strontium carbonate of the opening. Strontium nitrate, strontium iodide and strontium chloride are all soluble, and strontium carbonate is insoluble. The sodium and potassium salts are soluble, by the rule. Here is the first recipe in full. The molecular equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Sr(NO3)2(aq) + K2CO3(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{SrCO3(s) + 2KNO3(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The complete ionic equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Sr^2+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{2K+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{SrCO3(s) + 2K+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The net ionic equation, with the spectator ions K⁺ and NO₃⁻ taken out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Sr^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{SrCO3(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Do the same for the other two recipes, and only the spectator ions change.

| Solutions mixed | Spectator ions |
|---|---|
| Sr(NO₃)₂ and K₂CO₃ | K⁺ and NO₃⁻ |
| SrI₂ and Na₂CO₃ | Na⁺ and I⁻ |
| SrCl₂ and K₂CO₃ | K⁺ and Cl⁻ |

All three have the same net ionic equation, because the only thing that happens in any of them is a strontium ion meeting a carbonate ion. That is what the net ionic equation is for: it shows that mixtures made from different bottles can be the same reaction in the beaker.

The same thing happens with acids and bases. Here are three cases.

**Strong acid with strong base.** Calcium nitrate, a fertilizer, can be made by adding nitric acid, a strong acid, to limewater, which is a dilute solution of calcium hydroxide, a strong base. The molecular equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Ca(NO3)2(aq) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The complete ionic equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2H+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{Ca^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Ca^2+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Take out Ca²⁺ and 2 NO₃⁻. What is left is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2H+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide it by 2:

$$
\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}
$$

This is the net ionic equation when a strong acid such as nitric or hydrochloric acid meets a strong base and the salt they leave is soluble.

**Weak acid with strong base.** Go back to benzoic acid, the weak acid that sodium hydroxide neutralized to make sodium benzoate. The acid is written whole, so the net ionic equation is different:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HC7H5O2(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C7H5O2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The only spectator ion is Na⁺.

**Strong acid with weak base.** Pyridine, C₅H₅N, is a weak base. It reacts with hydrobromic acid, a strong acid, to give dissolved pyridinium bromide:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C5H5N(aq) + HBr(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C5H5NHBr(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Pyridine is written whole, the acid and the dissolved salt are written as ions, and the net ionic equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C5H5N(aq) + H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C5H5NH+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The only spectator ion is Br⁻. Ammonia and other weak bases are written whole in the same way.

<KeyIdea>

Write as separate ions only what is really present as separate ions: dissolved ionic compounds, strong acids and strong bases. Everything else keeps its whole formula. Take away the ions that did not change, and the net ionic equation is what is left.

</KeyIdea>

## When nothing happens

Sometimes every ion is a spectator. A gardener dissolves two fertilizers in one watering can: ammonium sulfate and potassium nitrate. In the can are NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions from the first fertilizer, and K⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions from the second.

Trade partners and you get ammonium nitrate and potassium sulfate. Both are soluble, by the rule: one is an ammonium salt and a nitrate, and the other is a potassium salt. So no solid forms, no molecule forms, and the same four ions are on both sides of the complete ionic equation. Everything cancels.

There is no net ionic equation to write, and the right answer is the words "no reaction". That can feel like a mistake, as if every mixture in a chemistry question had to do something. This one does nothing. An equation that makes a solid out of soluble ions is wrong, however balanced it looks.

<Check
  answer={1}
  misses={{
    2: "A spectator ion is dissolved and separate on both sides. Look at where the calcium is on the right.",
    3: "A spectator ion is the same on both sides of the equation. Look for OH⁻ on the right.",
    4: "A net ionic equation can contain molecules. CO₂ is written whole because it is a gas.",
  }}
>
Limewater is a clear, dilute solution of calcium hydroxide, a strong base. Bubble a little carbon dioxide gas through it and it turns milky, as insoluble calcium carbonate forms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Which statement about this reaction is correct?

- It has no spectator ions: every ion ends up in a new substance
- Ca²⁺ is a spectator ion, because calcium appears on both sides of it
- OH⁻ is a spectator ion, because hydroxide is not part of the solid
- It has no net ionic equation, because carbon dioxide is not ionic

<div slot="why">

Only the calcium hydroxide is written as ions, so the complete ionic equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Ca^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) +} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A spectator ion is dissolved and separate on both sides. Ca²⁺ ends up locked in the solid, and OH⁻ is used up: its atoms end up in the water and in the carbonate. Nothing is the same on both sides, so nothing cancels, and the complete ionic equation is already the net ionic equation.

This is the opposite extreme from "no reaction". There, every ion is a spectator. Here, none is.

A net ionic equation can contain molecules. CO₂ is written whole because it is a gas.

The same reaction slowly hardens the lime plaster under a fresco.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Eight mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Splitting a weak acid, a weak base or water into ions.** A weak acid gives up only some of its protons, and only some of a weak base's molecules take one, so each is written whole. Benzoic acid is written HC₇H₅O₂(aq).
- **Splitting a solid.** A precipitate is not dissolved. Ag₃PO₄(s) stays whole.
- **Changing a subscript to make an equation balance.** That changes the substance. Change coefficients only.
- **Leaving a spectator ion in, or striking out an ion that changed.** An ion is a spectator only if it is dissolved and separate on both sides.
- **Checking atoms and forgetting charge.** 2 NH₄⁺(aq) + 3 O₂(g) → 2 NO₃⁻(aq) + 8 H⁺(aq) has the same atoms on both sides and is not balanced.
- **Leaving coefficients that share a factor.** 2 H⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) → 2 H₂O(l) becomes H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
- **Leaving off the states when a question asks for them.** Without (s) and (aq), an equation cannot show what precipitated and what stayed dissolved. Write them.
- **Forcing a reaction.** If every ion is dissolved and unchanged on both sides of the complete ionic equation, everything cancels and the answer is "no reaction".

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Every change, physical or chemical, can be written as a balanced equation with a state symbol on each formula.
- Balanced means the same number of atoms of each element and the same total charge on both sides, reached by changing coefficients and never subscripts.
- In a complete ionic equation, dissolved ionic compounds, strong acids and strong bases are written as separate ions. Solids, liquids, gases, weak acids, weak bases and water keep their whole formulas.
- Spectator ions are dissolved and unchanged on both sides. Removing them leaves the net ionic equation, which shows only what changed.
- Mixtures that look different can share one net ionic equation, such as H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) for nitric acid or hydrochloric acid with a strong base whose salt is soluble.
- When every ion is dissolved and unchanged on both sides of the complete ionic equation, everything cancels and the answer is "no reaction".
