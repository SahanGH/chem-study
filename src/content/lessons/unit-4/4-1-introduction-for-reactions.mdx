Drop a lump of dry garden soil into a glass of water, and bubbles stream up from it. Drop a spoonful of descaler into a kettle crusted with white scale, half full of water, and bubbles pour off the scale. Bubbles both times. In the kettle a new substance was made: a gas that was not there before. In the glass the bubbles are not a new substance. They are air that was sitting in the pores of the soil, pushed out as the water soaked in. This lesson is about telling those two apart from what you can see.

## Two kinds of change

A **physical change** alters a substance's properties but not its composition, which is what the substance is made of. It is the same substance afterward. Two kinds are common.

The first is a change of phase, among solid, liquid and gas. A jar of coconut oil is a white solid in a cool pantry and a clear liquid on a hot day. Dew is water vapor from the air that has turned to liquid on a cold lawn.

The second is making or separating a mixture. Stir pigment powder into linseed oil and you have paint. A drop of food coloring spreads through a glass of water. A coffee filter holds back the grounds and lets the coffee through. Dissolving a salt is a harder case, and topic 4.4 comes back to it.

A **chemical change** turns substances into new substances, which typically have a different composition. A chemical change is also called a chemical reaction.

Raw sienna is a yellow-brown earth that painters have used for centuries. Roast it and it turns red-brown, and it stays red-brown when it cools. The iron compound that made it yellow has become a different iron compound, one that holds no hydrogen and less oxygen for each atom of iron. Water vapor has come off. Painters call the new pigment burnt sienna.

Which of these changes turns a substance into a new substance?

- A square of chocolate left in a warm car goes soft and runny, then hardens again in the shade
- A slice of bread in a toaster turns brown and smells different
- A wet towel hung on a line is dry by the evening
- Muddy water left overnight goes clear above a layer of silt

<div slot="why">

Toasting makes new substances at the surface of the bread. You can tell from the new color, the new smell and the new taste, and cooling the toast does not bring the bread back.

The chocolate changed phase, from solid to liquid and back, and it is still chocolate. The towel dried because its water turned to vapor, which is a change of phase too. The mud settled out of the water: a mixture separating. In those three, the substances at the end are the ones that were there at the start.

## What a reaction can look like

You cannot watch atoms rearrange, but a chemical change often gives itself away. Four kinds of observation are possible evidence of one.

- **Heat or light is given off.** A tub of wet plaster of Paris grows warm as it sets. A firefly's flash is light made by a reaction inside its body.
- **A gas forms.** The kettle scale fizzes because the acid in the descaler reacts with it. The gas is carbon dioxide, which was not there before.
- **A precipitate forms.** A **precipitate** is a solid that forms out of a solution: a clear liquid turns cloudy, and a solid settles.
- **The color changes.** A slice of apple turns brown in the air.

Each of these is possible evidence, and the next section says why "possible" matters.

## Why one observation is not enough

Each of the four has a look-alike in which no new substance forms.

| What you see | With a new substance | With no new substance |
|---|---|---|
| Bubbles | Kettle scale fizzing in descaler | Air bubbling from the air stone of an aquarium |
| A new color | A cut apple turning brown | Wet sand looks darker than dry sand; blue and yellow paint stirred together look green |
| Warmth | Plaster warming as it sets | Steam warming a cold window as it condenses on it |
| A solid appears | Two clear solutions turn cloudy when mixed | Sugar crystals growing in an old jar of honey |

The sand is pale again once it dries, and nothing in it has changed. The window was warmed by a change of phase. A colored solution that only gets paler when water is added still holds the same colored particles, spread through more water. And a solid that was dissolved can come back out of a solution that cools or dries out, the same solid as before.

A solid that appears the moment two clear solutions are mixed is different. Nothing was cooled and nothing evaporated, so it is hard to explain without a new substance. That makes it strong evidence. Even so, it is evidence, not proof.

A chemist holds a loop of clean platinum wire in the blue flame of a laboratory burner. The wire glows orange. Out of the flame it stops glowing, and it is the same silvery wire as before. What do these observations show?

- A chemical change happened, because the hot platinum gave off orange light

<div slot="why">

Heat or light counts as evidence when the change itself gives it off. Here the heat was put in from outside, by the flame, and a metal that is hot enough glows. Once it is cool, the wire has the properties it started with: the same color and the same shine. So nothing points to a new substance in the wire.

The second option has the right label and the wrong reason. Platinum can react: a hot mixture of nitric acid and hydrochloric acid dissolves it.

## Building a case

A chemist does not ask "did I see a sign?" but "what is the simplest explanation of everything I saw?" The case for a chemical change is built by trying the explanations that need nothing new, and watching them fail.

<KeyIdea>

One sign is a reason to look further, not a verdict. For each thing you saw, ask what would have to be true if nothing new had formed. The case for a chemical change is strong when no such explanation fits.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Building the case for a chemical change">

A painter is making a lake pigment, a dye fixed onto a white powder. For a first test she leaves the dye out. She dissolves alum in one jar of warm water and washing soda in another, then leaves both to cool. At room temperature both solutions are still clear and colorless. When she pours the washing soda solution into the alum solution, the mixture froths, and it goes on frothing after she has stopped pouring. A white, jelly-like solid appears and slowly settles. Did a chemical change take place? Build the argument.

1. **List what was seen.** Bubbles of gas. A white solid that formed out of two clear solutions. No change of color: the white cloudiness is the solid itself. Nobody measured the temperature.

2. **Try to explain the bubbles with no new substance.** Bubbles appear when a liquid boils, when air that was already there is pushed out, as it was from the lump of soil, and when a gas that had been dissolved in a liquid leaves it again. None of these fits here. Nothing was being heated. Two clear solutions have no pores for air to hide in, and the frothing went on after the pouring stopped, so it was not air carried under by the pour. Neither jar held a fizzy liquid, either. Each held only water with a solid dissolved in it, standing open to the air, and the little air that water holds does not come out as froth when two such liquids are poured together.

3. **Try to explain the solid with no new substance.** A dissolved solid can come back out of a solution that cools or dries out. Neither fits. Both solutions had already cooled and stayed clear, the solid appeared within seconds, and mixing gave each dissolved solid more water, not less.

4. **Name a new substance that would account for each.** Washing soda is sodium carbonate, and a carbonate can give off carbon dioxide, a gas. Alum contains aluminum, and aluminum hydroxide is a white solid that does not dissolve in water. Neither carbon dioxide nor aluminum hydroxide was in either jar.

5. **Write the claim.** "A chemical change took place. Two kinds of evidence appeared together, a gas and a precipitate. Neither can be explained here without a new substance, and each matches a new substance that can be named: carbon dioxide and aluminum hydroxide."

</Worked>

A pigment maker pours a pink solution of a cobalt compound into a colorless solution of a phosphate compound. The mixture turns cloudy, and a violet solid settles. The liquid left above it is almost colorless. Which statement judges this evidence best?

- A solid formed and the pink left the liquid, which a new substance explains best
- The pink color only faded because one solution diluted the other, so nothing new formed
- There is no evidence of a chemical change, because no gas formed and no heat was felt
- The violet solid is the pink cobalt compound coming back out of its solution unchanged

<div slot="why">

There are two observations to explain: a solid formed, and the liquid lost its pink color. Dilution makes a color paler. It does not make a solid, and it does not turn pink into violet.

The solid is not the starting compound coming back, either. It appeared when water was added to that compound's solution, not when water was taken away. It is also a different color from the pink-red crystals that the pink solution leaves when it dries out at room temperature.

A reaction does not have to show all four signs, so the missing gas and heat count for nothing.

A new substance can be named that accounts for both observations: cobalt phosphate, a violet solid that does not dissolve in water. Painters know it as the pigment cobalt violet. The cobalt that colored the liquid pink is now in the solid.

## Trying to knock the case down

A careful chemist also tests the dull explanation, that the solid is something that was there all along.

>
A hydroponic grower keeps two clear, colorless fertilizer concentrates in separate tanks. One holds dissolved calcium nitrate, the other dissolved potassium phosphate. By mistake some of each goes into one bucket, and the mixture turns milky white. One dull explanation is that the white solid is only one of the two fertilizer salts coming back out of its solution. Which observation would best rule that out?

- The milky mixture has no color when a light is shone through it
- Left to stand, the white solid sinks slowly and forms a layer on the bottom
- The white solid, filtered off and rinsed, will not dissolve in fresh water
- Stirring the bucket hard for a minute spreads the cloudiness evenly through it

<div slot="why">

Both fertilizer salts dissolve easily in water. If the white solid were one of them, it would dissolve again in fresh water. It will not, so it cannot be either of them. A new substance shows itself by properties the starting substances did not have, and this is one.

A new substance can be named, too. Calcium from one tank and phosphate from the other can form calcium phosphate, which does not dissolve in water. That is why growers keep the two tanks apart.

The other three observations would be the same whether or not anything new had formed.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again when you judge what you saw:

- **Treating one observation as proof.** Each of the four signs has a look-alike in which nothing new forms.
- **Calling every bubble a new gas.** Boiling makes bubbles. So does air that was only trapped or pumped in, and so does a gas that had been dissolved in a liquid and leaves it again, with nothing new made.
- **Calling a paler color a color change.** Adding water makes a colored solution paler. The colored particles are the same ones, spread through more water.
- **Counting heat or light that was put in from outside.** A platinum wire glowing in a flame was heated. It did not react.
- **Expecting all four signs.** Many reactions show only one or two.
- **Stopping at the label.** The word "chemical" alone is not an argument. Say what you saw, why it cannot be explained without a new substance and, where you can, what the new substance could be.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A physical change alters a substance's properties but not its composition. The common ones are changes of phase and making or separating a mixture.
- A chemical change turns substances into new substances, which typically have a different composition.
- Four kinds of observation are possible evidence of a chemical change: heat or light given off, a gas formed, a precipitate formed, and a color change.
- Each of the four can also appear when no new substance forms, so one observation alone is not proof.
- To judge an observation, try to account for it with the substances that were already there. When every attempt fails, the evidence for a chemical change is strong. A solid that forms the moment two clear solutions are mixed is strong evidence, because nothing was cooled and nothing evaporated.
- A new substance has properties that the starting substances did not have.

</KeyPoints>
