Some factories make fine metal meshes without cutting anything. A thin sheet of nickel is printed with a pattern in a protective coating, then sprayed with a solution of iron(III) chloride. Wherever the nickel is bare, it dissolves, and what is left is the mesh. Nothing is worn away by force. Iron(III) ions are pulling electrons out of nickel atoms. This lesson is about keeping count of those electrons, because the count is what balances the equation.

## A redox reaction is two halves

In an oxidation–reduction reaction, or redox reaction for short, electrons pass from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced (topic 4.7). In the etching spray, nickel atoms lose electrons and become Ni²⁺ ions, which go into the solution. Fe³⁺ ions gain electrons and become Fe²⁺ ions. The chloride ions are spectators.

A **half-reaction** shows one of those two changes by itself, with the electrons written in. An electron is written e⁻.

Here is nickel's half, with the electrons it loses:

$$
\ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

And here is iron's half, with the electron it gains:

$$
\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e^- -> Fe^2+(aq)}
$$

In an **oxidation** half-reaction the electrons are a product, on the right. In a **reduction** half-reaction they are a reactant, on the left. Oxidation is a loss of electrons, and it shows as a rise in oxidation number: nickel goes from 0 to +2. Reduction is a gain of electrons, and it shows as a fall: iron goes from +3 to +2.

A half-reaction is balanced like any equation, in atoms and in charge (topic 4.2). Count each electron as a charge of −1. For nickel, the left side is 0, and the right side is

$$
(+2) + 2(-1) = 0
$$

For iron, the left side is

$$
(+3) + (-1) = +2
$$

and the right side is +2.

Neither half happens alone. Electrons do not pile up anywhere: every electron that one species loses, another species gains at once. The two always come together.

## Writing a simple half-reaction

Sometimes the half-reactions are given to you. A simple one you can write yourself, in three steps.

1. Write the species before and after.
2. Balance the atoms.
3. Add electrons to the more positive side until the two sides have the same charge.

Take a metal and its ion: cobalt atoms becoming cobalt(II) ions. There is one cobalt on each side, so the atoms are already balanced. The left side is 0 and the right side would be +2, so 2 electrons go on the right.

$$
\ce{Co(s) -> Co^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

Now a halogen and its ion: bromine becoming bromide ions. Bromine is Br₂, so the atoms come first.

$$
\ce{Br2(aq) -> 2Br^-(aq)}
$$

The left side is 0 and the right side is −2. The left is the more positive side, so 2 electrons go there.

$$
\ce{Br2(aq) + 2e^- -> 2Br^-(aq)}
$$

## Making the electrons cancel

The electrons lost in one half-reaction are the same electrons that are gained in the other. So the two numbers must be equal. That gives a routine for building the whole equation.

1. Write the two half-reactions, or take them as given.
2. Multiply one or both by the smallest whole numbers that make the electrons lost equal the electrons gained.
3. Add them. Cancel the electrons, and anything else that appears on both sides.
4. Check the result: the same atoms on both sides, the same total charge on both sides, and no electrons left.

<KeyIdea>

Electrons lost must equal electrons gained. Multiply the half-reactions until the electrons match, then add them, and the electrons cancel. A finished redox equation shows no electrons, and both its atoms and its charge balance.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Etching nickel with iron(III)">

Write the balanced equation for the reaction of the opening: nickel metal dissolves in a solution of iron(III) ions, forming Ni²⁺ and Fe²⁺.

1. **Write the oxidation half-reaction.** Nickel loses 2 electrons.

   $$
   \ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
   $$

2. **Write the reduction half-reaction.** An iron(III) ion gains 1.

   $$
   \ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e^- -> Fe^2+(aq)}
   $$

3. **Match the electrons.** Each nickel atom loses 2 electrons, and each Fe³⁺ ion gains only 1. So it takes two Fe³⁺ ions for each nickel atom. Multiply the reduction by 2.

   $$
   \ce{2Fe^3+(aq) + 2e^- -> 2Fe^2+(aq)}
   $$

4. **Add, and cancel the electrons.** There are two electrons on the right of one half and two on the left of the other. They cancel.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Ni(s) + 2Fe^3+(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ni^2+(aq) + 2Fe^2+(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Check.** Nickel: 1 and 1. Iron: 2 and 2. The charge on the left is 2(+3) = +6. On the right it is

   $$
   (+2) + 2(+2) = +6
   $$

   No electrons are left.

6. **Try it without the doubling.** Add the two half-reactions as first written, cross out the electrons as if they matched, and you get this:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Ni(s) + Fe^3+(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ni^2+(aq) + Fe^2+(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Its atoms balance, and that is what makes it tempting. But the charge is +3 on the left and +4 on the right. An equation whose charge does not balance is not balanced (topic 4.2). Here the missing charge is an electron that nickel lost and nothing gained: the nickel atom gave up two electrons, and the one Fe³⁺ ion took only one.

</Worked>

$$
\ce{Au^3+(aq) + 3e^- -> Au(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{Sn^2+(aq) -> Sn^4+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

In the balanced equation, written with the smallest whole-number coefficients, how many electrons pass from the tin(II) ions to the gold(III) ions?

<div slot="why">

Gold gains 3 electrons and tin loses 2. The smallest number that both 3 and 2 go into is 6. Multiply the gold half-reaction by 2, for 6 electrons gained, and the tin half-reaction by 3, for 6 electrons lost. Then add, and cancel the 6 electrons.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2Au^3+(aq) + 3Sn^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2Au(s) + 3Sn^4+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check the charge. On the left it is

$$
2(+3) + 3(+2) = +12
$$

and on the right it is 3(+4) = +12.

Now count the electrons in that equation. Each of the 3 Sn²⁺ ions loses 2, which makes 6 lost. Each of the 2 Au³⁺ ions gains 3, which makes 6 gained. So 6 electrons pass from tin to gold, though the finished equation does not show them.

An answer of 2 or 3 is the number of electrons in one half-reaction as written, before any multiplying. An answer of 5 adds those two numbers, and an answer of 1 subtracts them. An answer of 12 is the total charge on each side, not a number of electrons.

If you added the half-reactions as written and crossed out the electrons, the equation would have +5 on the left and +4 on the right: the gold ion would have taken 3 electrons where the tin ion gave up only 2.

## When oxygen and hydrogen are in the half-reaction

This part is **less common on the exam**, where half-reactions like the next one are usually given to you. It is shown once, so that you can follow where such a half-reaction comes from.

Many species that are oxidized or reduced in water hold oxygen or hydrogen atoms, and those atoms have to go somewhere. In an **acidic solution** there is plenty of H₂O and plenty of H⁺ to balance them with. The order is this:

1. Start with every element except oxygen and hydrogen, and get its atoms equal on the two sides.
2. Then oxygen. The side that is short of O atoms gets one H₂O for each atom it is missing.
3. Then hydrogen. The side that is short of H atoms gets one H⁺ for each atom it is missing.
4. Last, the charge. The more positive side gets electrons until the two sides have the same charge.

<Worked title="Chlorine taking iron out of well water">

Well water often carries dissolved iron(II) ions, Fe²⁺, which later leave orange stains on sinks and laundry. A water treatment plant adds chlorine, which forms hypochlorous acid, HOCl, in the water. HOCl is a weak acid, so it is written whole. The HOCl turns the Fe²⁺ into Fe³⁺ and is itself turned into chloride ions, Cl⁻. Write the balanced equation, balancing it the way it is done for acidic solution, with H₂O and H⁺.

1. **Write the simple half-reaction.** Iron(II) loses an electron.

   $$
   \ce{Fe^2+(aq) -> Fe^3+(aq) + e^-}
   $$

2. **Start the other with what is known.** HOCl becomes Cl⁻.

   $$
   \ce{HOCl -> Cl^-}
   $$

   Chlorine is already balanced, 1 and 1.

3. **Oxygen next, with water.** There is one O on the left and none on the right, so add one H₂O to the right.

   $$
   \ce{HOCl -> Cl^- + H2O}
   $$

4. **Then hydrogen, with H⁺.** There is one H on the left and there are two on the right, so add one H⁺ to the left.

   $$
   \ce{HOCl + H^+ -> Cl^- + H2O}
   $$

5. **Last, the charge, with electrons.** The left side is +1 and the right side is −1, so add 2 electrons to the left.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{HOCl(aq) + H^+(aq) + 2e^- ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Cl^-(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Both sides are now −1. A cross-check from topic 4.7: chlorine's oxidation number is +1 in HOCl and −1 in Cl⁻. That is a fall of 2, which is 2 electrons gained.

6. **Match the electrons.** The iron half-reaction loses 1 electron and this one gains 2, so multiply the iron half-reaction by 2.

   $$
   \ce{2Fe^2+(aq) -> 2Fe^3+(aq) + 2e^-}
   $$

7. **Add, and cancel the electrons.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{HOCl(aq) + H^+(aq) +} \\
   &\quad \ce{2Fe^2+(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Cl^-(aq) + H2O(l) + 2Fe^3+(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

8. **Check.** Chlorine: 1 and 1. Oxygen: 1 and 1. Hydrogen: 2 and 2. Iron: 2 and 2. The charge on the left is

   $$
   0 + (+1) + 2(+2) = +5
   $$

   and on the right it is

   $$
   (-1) + 0 + 2(+3) = +5
   $$

</Worked>

At the plant, the Fe³⁺ then comes out of the water as a rust-colored solid, which a filter catches.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Adding the half-reactions without matching the electrons.** Ni(s) + Fe³⁺(aq) → Ni²⁺(aq) + Fe²⁺(aq) has balanced atoms and an unbalanced charge, +3 against +4.
- **Putting the electrons on the wrong side.** They are a product in an oxidation and a reactant in a reduction.
- **Adding electrons before the atoms are balanced.** Two iodide ions make one I₂, so that half-reaction starts with 2 I⁻.
- **Multiplying only part of a half-reaction.** The multiplier applies to every species in it, H⁺ and H₂O included.
- **Leaving electrons in the final equation.** They must cancel completely.
- **Counting the atoms and calling it balanced.** Every atom in the wrong nickel equation at the top of this list balances. It is the charge, +3 against +4, that gives away the electrons that were never matched.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A half-reaction shows either the oxidation or the reduction by itself, with the electrons written in: on the right when they are lost, on the left when they are gained.
- A half-reaction is balanced in atoms and in charge, counting each electron as −1.
- Oxidation and reduction always happen together, so the electrons lost must equal the electrons gained.
- To build the full equation, multiply the half-reactions so that the electrons match, add them and cancel the electrons.
- A finished redox equation has the same atoms and the same total charge on both sides and shows no electrons.
- In acidic solution a half-reaction's oxygen is balanced with H₂O and its hydrogen with H⁺ before the electrons are added, a step the exam usually does for you.

</KeyPoints>
