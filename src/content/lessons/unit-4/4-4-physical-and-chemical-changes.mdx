Until the last minutes before a launch, the fuel tank of a hydrogen-burning rocket is kept topped up with liquid hydrogen at −253 °C. It needs to be, because the liquid boils the whole time, and the gas is piped away from the rocket. In a tank that boils without stopping, a great deal seems to be going on. Yet every hydrogen molecule that boils out leaves exactly as it went in, as H₂. Nothing was broken except the weak hold the molecules had on one another.

All that boiling, and nothing new is made. How much seems to be going on does not tell you what kind of change it is. What happened to the particles does.

## Between the particles, or inside them

Two kinds of attraction are at work in a sample of a molecular substance. Topic 3.1 drew the line between them.

- **Chemical bonds** hold the atoms together inside each molecule. (In a salt, the chemical bonds are the ionic bonds of topic 2.1, which hold the ions together.)
- **Attractions between particles** hold whole molecules near one another. They are far weaker.

The rule for naming a change rests on that difference, and it has two halves.

When a process breaks chemical bonds, forms them, or does both, it is typically classified as a **chemical** process. When a process changes only the attractions between particles, as a change of phase does, it is typically classified as a **physical** process.

Boiling liquid hydrogen is the second kind. H₂ molecules that were close together are now far apart, and the bond inside each one is untouched.

<KeyIdea>

Ask what changed at the level of the particles. If chemical bonds were broken or formed, the change is typically called chemical. If only the attractions between particles changed, it is typically called physical. The label is worth nothing without that reason.

</KeyIdea>

## From what you see to what the particles do

On the exam, you explain what is seen by what the particles do. Here are three things you can observe, each with its reason.

**How much energy the change takes.** Boiling a mole of liquid hydrogen takes about 0.90 kJ. Breaking the bonds in the same mole of H₂ molecules takes 436 kJ (topic 2.2), about 480 times as much. Attractions between molecules are weak and bonds are strong. That is why liquid hydrogen boils at a temperature far below any that would break its molecules into atoms. Topic 3.1 made the same comparison for water.

**Whether the same substance comes back.** Cool hydrogen gas enough, and it is liquid hydrogen again, because the molecules never changed. After bonds have been broken and new ones formed, reversing the conditions often does not bring the starting substance back.

**Whether the substance's own properties changed.** Color, smell, whether it dissolves, whether it burns: these depend mostly on which atoms are bonded to which. A new set of bonds is a new substance, with new properties.

None of these is a test that never fails, and that is why the rule says "typically".

<Check answer={3}>
A plant that makes vinyl chloride, the starting material of PVC, receives its chlorine as an amber liquid in a railroad tank car, and turns the liquid into gas before using it. Two measurements on chlorine: turning 1 mol of the liquid into gas takes 20.4 kJ, and breaking 1 mol of Cl₂ molecules into atoms takes 243 kJ.

A sample of the liquid absorbs 20.4 kJ for each mole and becomes a yellow-green gas with all the chemical properties of chlorine. What happened to the particles?

- The Cl–Cl bonds broke, and the gas is made of separate chlorine atoms
- Some of the Cl–Cl bonds broke, since energy was absorbed by the sample
- The molecules moved far apart, and every Cl–Cl bond stayed intact
- The molecules themselves grew larger as they took in the energy

<div slot="why">

The sample took in 20.4 kJ for each mole, exactly what it takes to separate the molecules from one another, and it did become a gas. Breaking the bonds would take 243 kJ for each mole on top of that, about 12 times as much. The gas still behaves as chlorine, so it is still made of Cl₂ molecules. The color is paler in the gas largely because the same Cl₂ molecules are spread far more thinly, not because they changed. Only the attractions between molecules were overcome, so this is classified as a physical process.

Notice the size of the gap, though. For chlorine it is about 12 times, far narrower than the 480 times for hydrogen, mainly because chlorine molecules hold on to one another much more strongly than hydrogen molecules do. The bond is still much the stronger of the two.

Molecules do not swell when they are heated. They move apart.

</div>
</Check>

<Worked title="Two changes in a potter's bowl">

A potter shapes a bowl from wet clay. Clay is made of tiny flat crystals of a mineral, with water between them.

**Change 1.** Left on a shelf for a week, the bowl dries hard. Dropped into a bucket of water, it falls apart into soft clay again.

**Change 2.** Fired in a kiln, a dry bowl gives off water vapor, although it was already dry, and it comes out as ceramic. No amount of soaking softens it. In the kiln, above about 500 °C, O–H groups that are part of the mineral itself come off as water. At higher temperatures still, the crystals join to one another through new bonds.

Classify each change, and explain it at the level of the particles.

1. **Drying: say what left.** Water molecules that sat between the crystals evaporated.

2. **Say which attractions changed.** The attractions between the water molecules and the crystals were overcome, and so were the attractions between one water molecule and the next. No bond inside a water molecule or inside the mineral was broken.

3. **Tie it to what is seen.** Put the water back, and the soft clay is back: the same substances, arranged as before. Only attractions between particles changed, so drying is classified as a physical change.

4. **Firing: say what broke and what formed.** O–H groups that were bonded into the mineral came off, so bonds were broken. Water molecules formed from them, and new bonds joined the crystals, so bonds were formed.

5. **Tie it to what is seen.** The water that comes off above about 500 °C was made in the kiln, from the mineral's own atoms. It is not water that was waiting in the bowl. The ceramic has properties the clay never had: it rings when it is tapped, and water no longer softens it. The mineral has become a different substance.

6. **Classify, with the reason.** Bonds were broken and formed, so firing is classified as a chemical change.

</Worked>

<Check answer={1}>
Two paint samples are left to dry on a balance. Sample 1, a watercolor wash, loses mass as it dries, and a wet brush turns it back into paint. Sample 2, a layer of oil paint, gains a little mass as it hardens over several days, and neither oil nor water softens it again. Which explanation fits the evidence?

- Water left sample 1, while in sample 2 new bonds formed, taking in atoms from the air
- Both are physical changes: in each sample a liquid evaporated and left the solid behind
- Both are chemical changes, because both paints ended up hard and dry to the touch
- Sample 2 is a physical change, because its mass went up where sample 1's went down

<div slot="why">

Evaporation takes molecules away, so the mass falls, and putting the water back restores the paint. Only the attractions between the water and the rest of the paint changed, so the drying of the watercolor is classified as a physical change.

A gain in mass cannot be evaporation. Atoms were added. Oxygen from the air bonds to the oil molecules and links them to one another. The solid that results is a new substance, which neither oil nor water softens, so the hardening of the oil paint is classified as a chemical change.

The oil in oil paint does not harden by evaporating. It reacts.

</div>
</Check>

## Dissolving a salt: one change, two arguments

At a lab bench, a student stirs white crystals of potassium nitrate, KNO₃, into a beaker of water, and they disappear.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{KNO3(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{K^+(aq) + NO3^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

What the particles do is not in doubt:

- In the crystal, K⁺ ions and NO₃⁻ ions are held together by ionic bonds.
- In the water, the ions are pulled apart from one another. Each one ends up surrounded by water molecules that are attracted to it. That is the ion–dipole attraction of topic 3.1.
- The covalent bonds inside each NO₃⁻ ion are untouched (topic 3.8), and so are the bonds inside each water molecule.

  caption="Potassium nitrate dissolving. The ionic bonds of the crystal are broken as ions leave it, and each ion is then held by the water molecules around it. The ions themselves do not change. The water molecules are drawn as in topic 3.8, with the rest of the water left out. What is new here is the crystal the ions came from."
/>

What to call the change is in doubt, and chemists argue it both ways.

**It looks chemical.**

- Ionic bonds are chemical bonds, and dissolving broke them. Every ion that left the crystal had to be pulled free of the oppositely charged ions that held it in place.
- The solution also does something that the crystal could not. It conducts electricity, because its ions are now free to move through the water.

**It looks physical.**

- A K⁺ ion is still a K⁺ ion, and each nitrate ion still has its three oxygen atoms bonded to its nitrogen.
- Water molecules now sit where other ions sat, held by an attraction between particles, not a bond. Take the water away, and the equation above runs backward: potassium nitrate crystals grow again.

Both arguments can be defended, and that, too, is why the rule says "typically". What you are expected to do is say exactly what happens to the particles, and to be able to make either argument from it. An answer that only says "physical" or only says "chemical" has not explained anything.

<Check answer={4}>
Sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄, is a white salt that dissolves in water. Which statement is true of the particles when it dissolves?

- Whole Na₂SO₄ units move apart from one another and spread through the water
- One Na₂²⁺ ion and one SO₄²⁻ ion separate, and each attracts water molecules around it
- Bonds inside each sulfate ion break, freeing sulfur and oxygen atoms into the water
- Two Na⁺ ions and one SO₄²⁻ ion separate, and each attracts water molecules

<div slot="why">

Sodium sulfate is ionic. When it dissolves, the ionic bonds between its Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions are broken, and the ions move apart:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Na2SO4(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2Na^+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The subscript 2 counts sodium ions. Each formula unit gives two separate Na⁺ ions, each with a charge of 1+, and there is no such ion as Na₂²⁺.

Each ion attracts the water molecules around it, which is an ion–dipole attraction. The covalent bonds inside the sulfate ion stay as they are.

Whichever name you give the change, physical or chemical, these are the facts that both arguments start from.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Judging a change by how much seems to be going on.** Liquid hydrogen boils without stopping and is a physical change; oil paint hardens quietly and is a chemical one.
- **Giving the label without the reason.** Say which bonds or which attractions changed.
- **Saying that boiling or melting breaks the bonds inside molecules.** Hydrogen gas is still H₂.
- **Calling water that comes off a sample proof of drying.** The kiln made new water from the clay mineral's O–H groups.
- **Treating the rule as settling every case.** Both halves say "typically": dissolving a salt breaks ionic bonds, yet the ions come through unchanged, and it can be argued either way.
- **Breaking up a polyatomic ion when a salt dissolves.** NO₃⁻ and SO₄²⁻ stay whole.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A process that breaks chemical bonds, forms them, or does both is typically classified as chemical.
- A process in which only the attractions between particles change, such as a change of phase, is typically classified as physical.
- Attractions between molecules are far weaker than bonds, which is why boiling a liquid takes far less energy than breaking its molecules into atoms.
- A classification is not complete without its reason at the level of the particles: which bonds or attractions changed, and whether a new substance formed.
- When a salt dissolves, its ionic bonds are broken and ion–dipole attractions form between the ions and water, while the ions themselves do not change.
- Dissolving a salt can therefore be argued to be chemical (bonds are broken) or physical (the ions come through unchanged), and you should be able to make both arguments.

</KeyPoints>
