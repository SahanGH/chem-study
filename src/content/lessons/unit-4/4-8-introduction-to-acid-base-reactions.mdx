Sodium hypochlorite, NaOCl, is the active ingredient of household bleach. A solution of sodium hypochlorite in water is basic: it holds far more hydroxide ions, OH⁻, than pure water does. Now look at the formula. Na, O, Cl. There is no hydrogen in it at all, so it has no OH to release.

The hydroxide ions come out of the water. To see how, you need a wider idea of what an acid and a base are.

## Acids give protons, bases take them

A **proton** here means a hydrogen ion, H⁺: a hydrogen atom without its one electron. Topic 4.7 called a reaction acid–base when a proton passes from one species to another. The two species have names.

A **Brønsted–Lowry acid** is a species that gives a proton to another species. A **Brønsted–Lowry base** is a species that takes one. You will also see them called a proton donor and a proton acceptor.

Neither works alone. A bare proton does not drift about in a solution. It goes straight from the species that gives it to a species that takes it. So wherever an acid is at work, a base is at work too.

Hydrogen cyanide, HCN, is a weak acid. Dissolve it in water, and a few of its molecules do this:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HCN(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O^+(aq) + CN^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

HCN gives a proton, so it is the acid. The water molecule takes it, so water is the base. What the water molecule becomes, H₃O⁺, is the **hydronium ion**. When an equation shows H⁺(aq), this is the ion it stands for.

The double arrow says that the reaction runs both ways and settles with reactants still present. Unit 7 is about reactions like that.

**Strong and weak.** Topic 4.2 used these two words to decide what is written as ions. They are about how far an acid goes in giving protons to water. In a **strong acid**, essentially every molecule gives a proton to water. Perchloric acid is one, and its equation has a single arrow:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HClO4(aq) + H2O(l) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O^+(aq) + ClO4^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

A **weak acid** gives up only some of its protons to water, for most weak acids a small fraction. In a solution of HCN, nearly all of the molecules are still HCN. Topics 8.2 and 8.3 put numbers on this.

Acids like these two are the molecular substances that topic 3.8 said react with water and form ions.

## Water does both

Now the hypochlorite of the opening. In water, sodium hypochlorite is present as Na⁺ ions and OCl⁻ ions, and the hypochlorite ions do this:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{OCl^-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{HOCl(aq) + OH^-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This time the water molecule gives the proton, so water is the acid. The hypochlorite ion takes it, so OCl⁻ is the base, and there is no OH anywhere in its formula. It becomes hypochlorous acid, HOCl, a weak acid. The hydroxide ion is what is left of the water molecule. That is where the hydroxide in the solution comes from.

OCl⁻ is a **weak base**: only a small fraction of the ions take a proton from water. A strong base such as sodium hydroxide is a different case. Its hydroxide ions are already there in the solid, and they are set free when it dissolves.

Why can water do both? Its oxygen atom has lone pairs (topic 2.5), and a lone pair can bond to an incoming proton. Its O–H bonds hold hydrogen atoms that can leave as protons. Give water an acid and it acts as a base. Give it a base and it acts as an acid.

<KeyIdea>

An acid gives a proton and a base takes one. Water can be either. It takes a proton from an acid and becomes H₃O⁺, or it gives a proton to a base and becomes OH⁻.

</KeyIdea>

## Conjugate pairs

Look again at HCN and the CN⁻ it turns into. They are the same species with and without one proton. Two species that differ by exactly one proton are a **conjugate acid–base pair**. The one with the proton is the acid of the pair. The one without it is its **conjugate base**. It works the other way too: when a base takes a proton, what it becomes is its **conjugate acid**.

Every proton transfer has two pairs, and each pair has one member on each side of the arrow. In the hypochlorite equation the pairs are OCl⁻ with HOCl, and H₂O with OH⁻.

<Figure
  src="/figures/4-8-conjugate-pairs.svg"
  alt="The equation for a hypochlorite ion and a water molecule forming hypochlorous acid and a hydroxide ion. A bracket above the equation joins the hypochlorite ion to hypochlorous acid and is labeled base and its conjugate acid. A dashed bracket below the equation joins the water molecule to the hydroxide ion and is labeled acid and its conjugate base."
  caption="Two conjugate pairs in one equation. The members of a pair differ by one proton and sit on opposite sides of the arrow."
/>

Writing a conjugate is bookkeeping, and the charge is part of it. A proton is one H with a charge of +1, and it carries that charge wherever it goes.

- When an acid gives up its proton, the charge leaves with it. What is left, the conjugate base, has one H fewer than the acid and a charge one lower.
- When a base takes a proton, the charge arrives with it. What forms, the conjugate acid, has one H more than the base and a charge one higher.

| Acid | Its conjugate base |
|---|---|
| HCN | CN⁻ |
| HOCl | OCl⁻ |
| H₃O⁺ | H₂O |
| H₂O | OH⁻ |

Water is in both columns: it is the conjugate base of H₃O⁺ and the conjugate acid of OH⁻.

## What an acid leaves behind

How strong an acid is means how readily it gives its proton to water. That one fact about the acid tells you something about its conjugate base. If the proton comes off easily, the base that is left has little pull on a proton. If the acid holds its proton tightly, the base that is left pulls hard to get one back.

Here are eight acids in order, the strongest at the top, each beside its conjugate base. Reading down, the acids hold their protons more and more tightly, and what each one leaves behind takes a proton back more and more readily.

| Acid | Its conjugate base |
|---|---|
| HClO₄ (strong) | ClO₄⁻ |
| H₃O⁺ | H₂O |
| HIO₃ | IO₃⁻ |
| HNO₂ | NO₂⁻ |
| HOCl | OCl⁻ |
| HCN | CN⁻ |
| CH₃NH₃⁺ | CH₃NH₂ |
| H₂O | OH⁻ |

So **of two acids, the stronger one has the weaker conjugate base**.

A strong acid sits above H₃O⁺. Its conjugate base here, ClO₄⁻, is so weak that it essentially never takes a proton from water. The same is true of Cl⁻ and NO₃⁻, the conjugate bases of the strong acids HCl and HNO₃. That is why ions like these are spectators when their acids meet a strong base and leave a soluble salt (topic 4.2). Every acid between H₃O⁺ and H₂O in the table is a weak acid. Iodic acid, HIO₃, is the strongest of them: in a dilute solution it gives up most of its protons, though not essentially all of them. At the bottom, OH⁻ is the strongest base in the table.

You do not need to memorize this order. A question gives you the relative strengths, or data that show them.

**Which way does a proton transfer go?** The stronger acid is the better giver and the stronger base is the better taker. So when equal amounts of the two are mixed, and water is not one of the four species in the equation, the proton ends up mostly on the stronger base. Now look at it from the other side. When that mixture has settled, it holds more of the weaker acid and the weaker base than of the stronger two. The two weaker species are always on the same side of the equation, because the weaker base is the conjugate of the stronger acid.

That side is the one the reaction **favors**. In a mixture like that one, the reaction goes more than halfway toward it. When water is one of the four species, on either side of the arrow, there is so much of it that the count is not that simple. Unit 7 makes the idea exact.

<KeyIdea>

Of two acids, the stronger one has the weaker conjugate base. A proton transfer favors the side that holds the weaker acid and the weaker base.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Why a gold mine keeps its cyanide solution basic">

A gold mine dissolves gold out of crushed ore with a solution of sodium cyanide. If that solution turned acidic, holding more H₃O⁺ ions than pure water does, its cyanide ions would become hydrogen cyanide, HCN, which leaves the solution as a deadly gas. So the mine adds slaked lime (calcium hydroxide, a strong base) to keep hydroxide ions in the solution.

Use the equation below to explain why that works. HCN is a weak acid, and it is a much stronger acid than water. The equation has a double arrow because the question is which side is favored.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HCN(aq) + OH^-(aq) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{CN^-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

1. **Find the proton that moved.** From left to right, HCN becomes CN⁻: it lost H⁺. OH⁻ becomes H₂O: it gained H⁺.

2. **Name the acid and the base on each side.** Going forward, HCN is the acid and OH⁻ is the base. Going backward, H₂O is the acid and CN⁻ is the base.

3. **Pair the conjugates.** HCN with CN⁻, and H₂O with OH⁻. Each pair differs by one proton and has one member on each side.

4. **Compare the strengths.** HCN is a stronger acid than H₂O. So its conjugate base, CN⁻, is a weaker base than OH⁻, the conjugate base of H₂O.

5. **Find the favored side.** The weaker acid, H₂O, and the weaker base, CN⁻, are both on the right, so the products are favored. With hydroxide ions around, nearly all of the HCN that forms hands its proton over and goes back to CN⁻, which stays dissolved.

6. **Say what an acid would do.** Add acid and the reaction is

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{H3O^+(aq) + CN^-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{HCN(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   H₃O⁺ is a far stronger acid than HCN, so this one favors its products too, and now the product is HCN. It favors them so strongly that, while there are cyanide ions left to take a proton, essentially every H₃O⁺ ion added gives one up. That is why this arrow is single.

   So the mine cannot let the solution turn acidic. It cannot even let it come close: while the solution is still slightly basic, holding fewer H₃O⁺ ions than pure water does, a good share of its cyanide is already HCN.

</Worked>

## Does a weak acid need less base?

A weak acid and a strong acid differ in where their protons are. Does that change how much base it takes to neutralize them?

<Check
  answer={3}
  misses={{
    1: "HY is a far stronger acid than water. What can a hydroxide ion do when it meets an HY molecule?",
    2: "Go through each kind of particle in flask B and ask whether it has a proton to give to OH⁻.",
    4: "The X⁻ and Y⁻ ions have no proton to give. Count the particles that do.",
  }}
>
Picture two flasks as particle drawings, with the water molecules left out. In flask A, eight units of a strong acid, HX, were dissolved: you would see 8 H₃O⁺ ions and 8 X⁻ ions. In flask B, eight units of a weak acid, HY, were dissolved: you would see 7 HY molecules, 1 H₃O⁺ ion and 1 Y⁻ ion.

Sodium hydroxide solution, a strong base, is added to each flask until the acid is just neutralized. How many OH⁻ ions does each flask need?

- A needs 8 and B needs 1, because OH⁻ reacts only with the H₃O⁺ ions
- A needs 8 and B needs 7, because only the HY molecules still hold a proton
- A needs 8 and B needs 8, because OH⁻ takes protons from HY molecules too
- A needs 16 and B needs 9, because every particle pictured uses up one OH⁻

<div slot="why">

In flask A each of the 8 H₃O⁺ ions gives its proton to an OH⁻, so 8 are needed.

In flask B the one H₃O⁺ ion does the same. The hydroxide ion also takes the proton straight off each of the 7 HY molecules. It can, because HY is a far stronger acid than water. That makes OH⁻ a far stronger base than Y⁻, as well as a far stronger base than water. So flask B needs 1 + 7 = 8.

The X⁻ and Y⁻ ions have no proton to give. Essentially every proton the acid had to give ends up on a hydroxide ion, whether it was waiting on a water molecule or still on the acid.

So the base needed depends on how much acid was dissolved, and on how many protons each unit of it has to give: HX and HY have one each, and topic 4.6 met acids with two. It does not depend on how strong the acid is. The amount of acid is what a titration measures (topic 4.6).

</div>
</Check>

A weak acid such as HOCl keeps nearly all of its protons while it is alone in water, because water is only a very weak base. Hydroxide is a far stronger base than water. It is a far stronger base than OCl⁻ too, because HOCl is a far stronger acid than water. So it takes the proton from the weak acid's molecules directly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HOCl(aq) + OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{OCl^-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The arrow is single, as in topics 4.2 and 4.6. The products are favored so strongly that, with enough hydroxide, essentially all of the weak acid reacts. The same is true of HCN with hydroxide. The worked example drew that equation with a double arrow only because its question was which side is favored.

With a strong acid such as HClO₄ the protons are already on water molecules, and the reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H3O^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) of topic 4.2, with the water molecule that carries the proton written in. It is one reaction, written two ways.

**Beyond the exam:** a still wider definition, due to G. N. Lewis, calls an acid any species that accepts a pair of electrons and a base any species that donates one, with no proton needed. The exam does not test it.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Looking for OH in the formula to find a base.** OCl⁻, CN⁻ and N₂H₄ have none. A base is whatever takes the proton.
- **Leaving water out because it is "only the solvent".** In the equation for HCN in water, water is the base. In the equation for the hypochlorite ion in water, it is the acid.
- **Pairing the two reactants as conjugates.** A conjugate pair is one species with and without a proton, one on each side of the arrow.
- **Forgetting the charge when writing a conjugate.** HPO₄²⁻ plus a proton is H₂PO₄⁻, not H₂PO₄²⁻.
- **Giving the stronger acid the stronger conjugate base.** It is the other way round: HCN is a stronger acid than CH₃NH₃⁺, so CN⁻ is a weaker base than CH₃NH₂.
- **Counting only the H₃O⁺ ions when a base is added.** The flask of weak acid showed 1 H₃O⁺ ion and still needed 8 hydroxide ions, because each of its 7 HY molecules can give a proton too.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A Brønsted–Lowry acid gives a proton, H⁺, to another species, and a Brønsted–Lowry base takes one, so a base needs no OH in its formula.
- Water can do either: it takes a proton from an acid and becomes H₃O⁺, or it gives one to a base and becomes OH⁻.
- A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by one proton. Every proton transfer has two pairs, each with one member on each side of the arrow.
- A proton carries its +1 charge with it, so a conjugate base has one H fewer than its acid and a charge one lower, and a conjugate acid has one H more than its base and a charge one higher.
- Of two acids, the stronger one has the weaker conjugate base, and a proton transfer favors the side with the weaker acid and the weaker base.
- In a strong acid essentially every molecule gives a proton to water, and a weak acid gives up only some of its protons. Even so, equal amounts of a strong acid and a weak acid that each have one proton to give need equal amounts of hydroxide.

</KeyPoints>
