In Rome in the 1500s, long before anyone could make ice to order, a host chilled wine by standing the flask in a tub of water and stirring saltpeter into the water. The water turned cold as the salt dissolved. Saltpeter is potassium nitrate, and it does today what it did then: it dissolves freely, and it takes energy in from the water as it goes. Topic 6.1 promised an explanation for a change that takes energy in and happens anyway. For a salt, here it is.

## Three changes, each with two signs

Topics 3.10 and 6.1 took dissolving apart into three parts and followed the energy. Now each part gets two signs. One is for its enthalpy change: energy taken in is +, and energy released is −, as in Unit 6. The other is for its entropy change: more ways for the particles and their energy to be arranged is +, and fewer is −, as in topic 9.1.

For a salt in water, the three parts are these:

1. **The ions leave the crystal.** The attractions between cations and anions are overcome, which takes a great deal of energy in: enthalpy +, large. The ions go from fixed places in a crystal to the whole volume of the solution: entropy +.
2. **Water molecules move apart and rearrange to make room for each ion.** Some attractions between water molecules are overcome: enthalpy +, small beside part 1. The water molecules around the space rearrange to fit it and have fewer ways to be arranged: entropy −, a sign that is hard to separate from part 3's.
3. **Ions and water molecules attract.** Each ion ends up held in a shell of water molecules turned toward it, by the ion–dipole attractions of topic 3.1. Forming attractions releases energy: enthalpy −, large. An ion and the water molecules holding one another move less freely than they would apart: entropy −.

| Part | Enthalpy | Entropy |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Ions leave the crystal | +, large | + |
| 2. Water makes room | +, small | − |
| 3. Ions and water attract | −, large | − |

The entropy lost in parts 2 and 3 is one loss seen from two sides, water molecules and ions holding one another in place; an experiment measures only the total.

<KeyIdea>

Dissolving a salt is three changes at once. Pulling the ions out of the crystal takes energy in and raises the entropy. Making room in the water takes a little energy in. The attraction between ions and water releases energy, and the water held around each ion lowers the entropy.

</KeyIdea>

By Coulomb's law, the attraction between two charges is stronger when the charges are larger. Compared with potassium nitrate, what do you expect of the enthalpy changes of part 1 and part 3 for barium sulfate?

- Part 1 takes in more energy, and part 3 releases about the same
- Part 1 takes in about the same energy, and part 3 releases more

<div slot="why">

The same larger charges attract the partial charges on water molecules more strongly, so more energy is released when the ions and the water come together (part 3).

Both parts grow, and they have opposite signs. That is why the charges alone cannot tell you which salt has the larger total: for barium sulfate the total is +26.3 kJ/mol and for potassium nitrate +34.8 kJ/mol, close together, although each part is several times larger for barium sulfate.

## What is left when the parts nearly cancel

For a typical salt, part 1 takes in many hundreds of kilojoules per mole, sometimes thousands, and part 3 gives nearly all of it back. The enthalpy change of dissolving that a calorimeter measures (topic 6.4) is what is left over: usually a few tens of kilojoules per mole, with either sign. The entropy changes behave the same way: a gain from part 1, a loss from the held water, and a total that can have either sign.

This is why the topic stops short of prediction. Part 1 and part 3 each run to hundreds of kilojoules per mole, so an estimate of either that is off by a few percent is off by as much as the whole total, and the total could come out with the wrong sign. A salt's formula does not settle whether it dissolves. A question on this topic gives data (a temperature change, a ΔH°, a ΔS°, a $K_{sp}$) or asks about the sign and the size of one part.

What you can always do is put the pieces into topic 9.3's equation:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ
$$

Each quantity is now the total for the dissolving, per mole of salt. The more negative ΔG° is, the further the dissolving goes.

<Worked title="What 380 grams in a kilogram of water says">

At 25 °C a kilogram of water dissolves about 380 g of potassium nitrate, which is close to 3.8 mol:

$$
\ce{KNO3(s) -> K^+(aq) + NO3^-(aq)}
$$

For this dissolving ΔH° = +34.8 kJ/mol. How much potassium nitrate would dissolve if the enthalpy change were the whole of ΔG°? What does the measured solubility then say about the entropy change, and which of the three parts accounts for it?

1. **Read the enthalpy.** ΔH° is positive: parts 1 and 2 take in more energy than part 3 releases. The enthalpy term raises ΔG° and works against the dissolving.

2. **Test the enthalpy by itself.** If the entropy did not change at all, ΔG° would equal ΔH°, +34.8 kJ/mol. In joules:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &+34.8\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
   &\quad = +34{,}800\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Topic 9.5 turns that into a $K$. The top is in joules per mole, $R$ is 8.314 J/(mol·K) and $T$ is 298 K:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   -\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= -\frac{34{,}800}{(8.314)(298)} \\
   &= -14.05 \\
   K &= e^{-14.05} \\
   &= 7.9 \times 10^{-7}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Turn that K into a concentration.** For this equation $K$ is $[\ce{K+}][\ce{NO3-}]$, the $K_{sp}$ of topic 7.11, and each ion's concentration would be the square root:

   $$
   \sqrt{7.9 \times 10^{-7}} = 8.9 \times 10^{-4}\ M
   $$

   Less than a thousandth of a mole in a liter.

4. **Compare with the measurement.** The real solution holds several moles per liter, thousands of times more. So the entropy change cannot be zero, and it cannot be negative, which would make things worse. The term −TΔS° must be negative, and about as large in size as +34.8 kJ/mol.

5. **Check against the measured value.** ΔS° = +115.8 J/(mol·K). In kilojoules:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &+115.8\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)} \\
   &\quad = +0.1158\ \text{kJ/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The entropy term, in kJ/mol:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   T\Delta S^\circ &= (298)(0.1158) \\
   &= 34.5\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The entropy term cancels almost the whole of the enthalpy term. That leaves ΔG° close to zero and $K$ near 1, and for a salt a $K$ near 1 means a saturated solution with something like a mole of each ion in a liter, not a thousandth of a mole.

6. **Find the part responsible.** In the table, part 1 is the one that raises the entropy: the ions leave their fixed places for the whole solution. K⁺ and NO₃⁻ are large ions with single charges, and they hold the water around them weakly, so the loss in parts 2 and 3 is small and the gain wins.

</Worked>

Now warm the water. In topic 9.3's equation the temperature multiplies only the entropy term. For potassium nitrate ΔH° and ΔS° are both positive, the case topic 9.3 found favored at high temperature: at 25 °C the two terms nearly cancel, and the hotter the water, the larger the entropy term grows beside the enthalpy cost. 100 g of water dissolves about 13 g of potassium nitrate at 0 °C, about 38 g at 25 °C and about 246 g at 100 °C. Many salts are like it: they take energy in as they dissolve, have a positive ΔS°, and dissolve better in hot water. Not every salt has those two signs, as the next section shows.

A negative or zero ΔS° would leave ΔG° positive. And ΔH° alone does not decide: that was topic 6.1's point.

## When the water loses more than the ions gain

Part 1 always raises the entropy. Whether the total is positive depends on how much water each ion holds in place, and how firmly.

The words beneath read held in place. On the right, six water molecules with no ion lie at uneven distances and point in six different directions. The words beneath read free to turn and move."
  caption="Six water molecules next to a small ion with a charge of 2+, and six on their own. The ion holds its neighbors turned toward it, which leaves them far fewer ways to be arranged. Shaded circles are oxygen atoms and small open circles are hydrogen atoms."
/>

A small ion, or an ion with a charge of 2+ or 3+, has a strong pull on the water molecules near it. They turn one end toward the ion and stay. Each one has lost most of the ways it could be arranged, and the effect can reach a second layer of water.

For salts of such ions, the entropy change of dissolving can be negative: the water loses more than the ions gain. Here are two salts, with their measured values:

| Dissolving | ΔH° (kJ/mol) | ΔS° (J/(mol·K)) |
|---|---|---|
| KNO₃(s) → K⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) | +34.8 | +115.8 |
| MgSO₄(s) → Mg²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) | −91.3 | −209.6 |

Size matters inside one charge, too. Li⁺ is much smaller than K⁺, so it holds water more strongly, and lithium chloride warms the water as it dissolves (ΔH° = −37.0 kJ/mol; topic 6.1 had it warming an insulated cup).

## Ksp and ΔG° are one statement

The dissolving equation of a salt has an equilibrium constant, and topic 7.11 named it: $K_{sp}$. Topic 9.5 ties any $K$ to a ΔG°. So for a salt, ΔG° of dissolving is this:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K_{sp}
$$

A sparingly soluble salt has a $K_{sp}$ far below 1 and therefore a large positive ΔG° of dissolving: the two are the same fact. The more positive ΔG° is, the smaller $K_{sp}$ is.

This lets you take a $K_{sp}$ apart. $K_{sp}$ gives ΔG°; a calorimeter or a table gives ΔH°; the difference is TΔS°.

<Worked title="What keeps silver iodide out of solution">

Topic 7.11 gave $K_{sp} = 8.52 \times 10^{-17}$ for silver iodide at 25 °C:

$$
\ce{AgI(s) <=> Ag^+(aq) + I^-(aq)}
$$

ΔH° for this dissolving is +112.2 kJ/mol. Find ΔG° at 298 K, then TΔS° and ΔS°, and say which term keeps silver iodide from dissolving.

1. **Predict the sign.** $K_{sp}$ is far below 1, so ΔG° is positive and large.

2. **Find ΔG°.** With $R$ = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and $T$ = 298 K, the answer comes out in joules per mole:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta G^\circ ={} & -(8.314)(298) \\
   & \times \ln(8.52 \times 10^{-17}) \\
   ={} & -(2{,}477.6)(-37.00) \\
   ={} & +91{,}670\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Convert.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &+91{,}670\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
   &\quad = +91.7\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Find the entropy term.** Rearrange ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. Both values are in kJ/mol:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   T\Delta S^\circ &= \Delta H^\circ - \Delta G^\circ \\
   &= 112.2 - 91.7 \\
   &= +20.5\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Find ΔS°.** Divide by the temperature in kelvins:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta S^\circ &= \frac{20.5}{298} \\
   &= +0.0688\ \text{kJ/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   In joules:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &+0.0688\ \text{kJ/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)} \\
   &\quad = +69\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Read it.** The entropy change is positive: entropy works for this dissolving, to the amount of 20.5 kJ/mol. The enthalpy change works against it, and at +112.2 kJ/mol it is more than five times as large. Silver iodide stays out of solution because pulling Ag⁺ and I⁻ apart costs far more energy than their attraction to water gives back, not because of entropy.

</Worked>

What does a negative ΔG° mean for a salt? For a salt that gives one cation and one anion, $K_{sp} = 1$ would mean a saturated solution that is 1 M in each ion. So a ΔG° of dissolving below zero does not mean "dissolves", and one above zero does not mean "does not dissolve": every salt dissolves until $Q$ reaches $K_{sp}$ (topic 7.11), and ΔG° tells how far that is. Thallium(I) chloride, at +21.3 kJ/mol, still puts about 0.014 mol into a liter. How the acidity of the water changes a salt's solubility is topic 8.11, the other half of the story of solubility.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes come up on this topic:

- **"It takes energy in, so it should not dissolve."** The entropy term can cancel a positive ΔH° or outweigh it. It cancels nearly all of potassium nitrate's at 25 °C, and it outweighs xylitol's.
- **"Dissolving always raises the entropy."** The ions gain room, but water held around small or highly charged ions loses freedom, and the total can be negative, as it is for magnesium sulfate and barium sulfate.
- **Losing the double minus in −TΔS°.** When ΔS° is negative, the term is positive and works against the change.
- **Leaving ΔS° in joules** beside a ΔH° in kilojoules.
- **Predicting solubility from part 1 alone,** or from the charges alone. Parts 1 and 3 grow together and nearly cancel.
- **Dividing two ΔG° values to compare two $K_{sp}$ values.** Differences in ΔG° become ratios of $K$: 5.7 kJ/mol for each factor of ten at 298 K.
- **Reading a positive ΔG° of dissolving as "nothing dissolves".** It means that $K_{sp}$ is below 1.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Dissolving a salt is three changes. The ions leave the crystal (enthalpy +, entropy +). The water makes room (enthalpy +, small; entropy −). Ions and water attract (enthalpy −; entropy −, from the water held around each ion).
- The totals are what is left when large parts of opposite sign nearly cancel. So a salt's formula does not settle whether it dissolves, and a question gives data or asks about one part.
- An endothermic dissolving goes far when ΔS° is positive and TΔS° is about as large as ΔH° or larger. For potassium nitrate at 298 K the two nearly cancel: 34.5 against 34.8 kJ/mol.
- Small or highly charged ions hold water firmly, which can make ΔS° of dissolving negative, as for magnesium sulfate and barium sulfate.
- ΔG° of dissolving is ΔH° minus TΔS°, and it is also −RT times the natural logarithm of $K_{sp}$. So a small $K_{sp}$ and a large positive ΔG° are one statement.
- With $K_{sp}$ and ΔH° known, ΔH° minus ΔG° gives TΔS°, which shows which term keeps a salt out of solution.

</KeyPoints>
