A sugar-free mint sweetened with xylitol feels cool as it dissolves on your tongue: the dissolving takes energy in, and it happens by itself all the same. Topic 6.1 left that as a puzzle and said that energy is not the only thing that decides whether a change happens. The other thing is called entropy, and this lesson is about what it measures and how to tell whether it goes up or down.

## What entropy measures

**Entropy**, $S$, is a measure of how widely the matter and the energy of a system are spread out. The more ways the particles can be arranged, and the more ways their energy can be shared among them, the higher the entropy.

The system is what topic 6.1 said it is: the particles you are watching. The change in its entropy is written $\Delta S$, and like every $\Delta$ it is final minus initial:

$$
\Delta S = S_\text{final} - S_\text{initial}
$$

So $\Delta S$ is positive when the system's entropy rises and negative when it falls.

Now the mint. In the crystal each xylitol molecule is held in one place; dissolved, the same molecules are scattered through the liquid, with far more places to be, so the entropy of the xylitol goes up. Dissolving a salt has a twist, because water molecules are held around each ion, and topic 9.6 deals with it.

You may have seen entropy described as "disorder"; the question to ask is never how untidy something looks but how many ways its particles and their energy can be arranged.

There are two things to watch, the room the particles have and the way their energy is shared, and this lesson takes them in that order. Numbers wait for topic 9.2: here you find only the sign of $\Delta S$ and which of two changes is larger.

<KeyIdea>

Entropy, $S$, measures how widely the matter and the energy of a system are spread out. More ways to arrange the particles, or more ways to share the energy among them, means more entropy. $\Delta S$ is positive when the entropy of the system rises and negative when it falls.

</KeyIdea>

## Solid, liquid, gas

Topic 3.3 drew one substance three ways. In a solid each particle has a fixed place and only vibrates there. In a liquid the particles still touch but slide past one another, so each has many more places it can be. In a gas the particles are far apart and each one can be anywhere in the container.

  src="/figures/9-1-three-states.svg"
  alt="Three boxes of the same size, each holding the same 12 particles. In the box labeled solid, the particles sit touching in a neat block of three rows of four on the floor of the box, with the words least room. In the box labeled liquid, they still touch but lie in a lower, wider pile of staggered rows of five, four and three, with the words a little more. In the box labeled gas, they are scattered far apart through the whole box, with the words far more room."
  caption="The same 12 particles as a solid, a liquid and a gas. The more room each particle has, the more ways there are to arrange them, and the higher the entropy. A real gas is far more spread out than a drawing can show."
/>

So for one substance the entropy runs solid, then liquid, then gas, from lowest to highest. Melting raises it ($\Delta S > 0$). Vaporizing raises it. So does subliming, solid straight to gas. Each of the reverse changes, freezing, condensing and depositing, lowers it ($\Delta S < 0$).

The two steps are not the same size. Topic 3.3 showed that a mole of liquid takes up nearly the same volume as a mole of the solid, while a mole of gas near room conditions takes up about a thousand times as much. So the step from liquid to gas is far larger than the step from solid to liquid.

## A gas given more room

A gas does not have to change phase to gain room.

<Figure
  src="/figures/9-1-gas-given-room.svg"
  alt="Two drawings of a cylinder closed at its left end, with a sliding piston. In the upper drawing, labeled piston pushed in, 10 gas particles fill the space between the closed end and the piston. In the lower drawing, labeled piston pulled out, the piston stands twice as far from the closed end, and the same 10 particles are spread through twice the volume."
  caption="Pull the piston out and the gas spreads through all the space it is given. The particles and the temperature are the same, but the volume has doubled, so the entropy of the gas is higher."
/>

A gas is trapped in a cylinder by a piston that can slide. Pull the piston out until the gas has twice the volume, and wait for the gas to come back to the temperature of the room. Nothing about the particles has changed and the temperature is what it was, but each particle can now be found anywhere in a space twice as large, so there are more ways to arrange them: $\Delta S > 0$.

The reverse holds too: push the piston back in, let the gas return to the same temperature, and its entropy falls.

<KeyIdea>

Matter that gains room gains entropy. For one substance, entropy rises from solid to liquid and rises much more from liquid to gas. A gas that expands at a constant temperature gains entropy, and a gas that is compressed loses it.

</KeyIdea>

## Counting moles of gas in a reaction

A reaction can make gas or use it up. Because a mole of gas has so much more room than a mole of any solid or liquid, the gas decides the matter. As a rule, a reaction that ends with more moles of gas than it started with has $\Delta S > 0$, and a reaction that ends with fewer has $\Delta S < 0$.

The method has three short steps:

1. Find the substances marked (g).
2. Add up their coefficients on each side.
3. Compare the two totals.

Solids and liquids are left out of the count: beside a change in the moles of gas, what they add is small.

<KeyIdea>

To find the sign of $\Delta S$ for a reaction, count the moles of gas on each side first. More moles of gas among the products means the entropy rises. Fewer means it falls.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Four changes, four signs">

Find the sign of $\Delta S$ for each change.

1. **A block of camphor shrinking in a closet.** Camphor is a white, waxy solid that people hang in closets to keep moths away, and a block of it slowly disappears without ever melting: it sublimes.

   $$
   \ce{C10H16O(s) -> C10H16O(g)}
   $$

   No gas before, 1 mol of gas after: $\Delta S$ is positive. Each molecule leaves a fixed place for the whole closet.

2. **White smoke.** Hold the open mouths of a bottle of ammonia solution and a bottle of concentrated hydrochloric acid near each other and a white smoke forms where the two gases meet. The smoke is tiny crystals of ammonium chloride.

   $$
   \ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}
   $$

   Gas before: 1 + 1 = 2 mol. Gas after: none. $\Delta S$ is negative.

3. **Iron rusting.** Taking rust as iron(III) oxide:

   $$
   \ce{4Fe(s) + 3O2(g) -> 2Fe2O3(s)}
   $$

   Gas before: 3 mol. Gas after: none. $\Delta S$ is negative. The 4 mol of solid iron and the 2 mol of solid oxide are not counted.

4. **Nitrous oxide breaking down.** This is the reaction of topic 6.6.

   $$
   \ce{2N2O(g) -> 2N2(g) + O2(g)}
   $$

   Gas before: 2 mol. Gas after: 2 + 1 = 3 mol. $\Delta S$ is positive.

Each time, the reason fits in a phrase: "a solid becomes a gas", "2 mol of gas become a solid", "3 mol of gas are used up", "2 mol of gas become 3". An exam answer needs the sign and that phrase.

</Worked>

## When the count comes out even

Now try a reaction where counting does not finish the job.

<Check
  answer={3}
  misses={{

    2: "The count is right: 2 moles of gas on each side. But are the four gases the same substance, with the same entropy for each mole?",
    4: "Start with what you can count. How many moles of gas are on each side, and what does that say about the size of the change?",

>
In bright light, methane and chlorine react. The first products are chloromethane and hydrogen chloride:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH4(g) + Cl2(g) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{CH3Cl(g) + HCl(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Without a table of data, what can you say about $\Delta S$ for this reaction?

- It is large and positive, because two new substances are formed
- It is exactly zero, because 2 moles of gas become 2 moles of gas
- It is small, and counting moles of gas cannot give its sign
- Nothing can be said about its size or its sign without a table

<div slot="why">There are 2 mol of gas on each side, so the particles end with about as much room as they started with, and no large change in entropy is expected. But the four gases are four different substances, and a mole of one does not hold exactly the same entropy as a mole of another, so the change is not exactly zero either. Which side comes out a little higher cannot be seen from the equation. A table settles it (topic 9.2): for this reaction ΔS turns out to be small and positive.</div>
</Check>

When a reaction has the same number of moles of gas on both sides, or is among solids and liquids only, $\Delta S$ is small, and its sign cannot be found by counting; it takes the values of topic 9.2. On the exam, say exactly that: "the moles of gas do not change, so $\Delta S$ is small and its sign cannot be predicted without data."

<Worked title="Which change is larger?">

Two comparisons, with no numbers needed for either.

1. **Melting or vaporizing.** Mercury melts at −39 °C and boils at 357 °C. Which has the larger $\Delta S$: melting 1 mol of solid mercury, or vaporizing 1 mol of liquid mercury?

   $$
   \ce{Hg(s) -> Hg(l)}
   $$

   $$
   \ce{Hg(l) -> Hg(g)}
   $$

   Both are positive. In melting, the atoms go from touching in fixed places to touching and moving: a little more room. In vaporizing, they go from touching to far apart: more than a thousand times the volume. Vaporizing has the larger $\Delta S$, by a wide margin. The same holds for other substances: the step that makes the gas is the big one.

2. **Two reactions that make gas.** Nitrous oxide breaking down:

   $$
   \ce{2N2O(g) -> 2N2(g) + O2(g)}
   $$

   And liquid hydrazine burning:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{N2H4(l) + O2(g) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{N2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Count the gas. Nitrous oxide: 2 mol before, 3 mol after, a gain of 1 mol. Hydrazine: 1 mol before (the oxygen; the hydrazine is a liquid), 3 mol after, a gain of 2 mol. Both have $\Delta S > 0$, and the hydrazine reaction has the larger increase, because it gains more moles of gas.

As a rule, the larger the change in the moles of gas, the larger the change in entropy; tables bear this pair out.

</Worked>

## Energy with more ways to be shared

So far only the matter has spread out. Energy spreads too. Topic 3.5 said that the kelvin temperature of a sample measures the average kinetic energy of its particles, and topic 5.5 showed that the particles do not all have the average: their kinetic energies are spread over a range.

<Figure
  src="/figures/9-1-energy-spread.svg"
  alt="A graph of the fraction of particles against kinetic energy for a gas at two temperatures. The solid curve, labeled 300 K: narrower, rises steeply to a tall peak at low energy and falls away quickly. The dashed curve, labeled 600 K: wider, has a peak half as tall, a little farther to the right, and a long tail that stretches about twice as far along the energy axis. The two curves cross once."
  caption="The same gas at 300 K and at 600 K. The areas under the two curves are equal, because the number of particles is the same. At the higher temperature the particles are spread over a wider range of kinetic energies."
/>

At 300 K most of the particles have kinetic energies in a narrow band near the low end. At 600 K the curve is half as tall and twice as wide. The area under each curve is the same, because the number of particles is the same, but at the higher temperature the particles are spread over a wider range of kinetic energies. There is more energy to share and there are more ways to share it. That is a rise in entropy.

So warming a substance raises its entropy and cooling lowers it, even when its volume and its phase do not change. The exam's course description states this for a gas; it is true of liquids and solids as well.

<KeyIdea>

Energy that is shared in more ways means more entropy. Warming a sample gives its particles more energy to share and more ways to share it, so its entropy rises with temperature.

</KeyIdea>

## A fall in entropy does not forbid a change

Look back at the iron. Rusting lowers the entropy of the iron and the oxygen, and iron rusts by itself all the same. So a change whose $\Delta S$ is negative can happen. The xylitol showed the other half: a change that takes energy in can happen too.

Neither the energy alone nor the entropy alone decides. Rusting releases a great deal of energy, and the xylitol's dissolving raises its entropy. Topic 9.3 puts the two together in one quantity and shows how each is weighed. Until then, an entropy change tells you one thing only: whether the matter and energy of the system end up more spread out or less.

<Trap>

Six mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Calling entropy "messiness".** Ask how many ways the particles and their energy can be arranged, not how untidy something looks.
- **Counting every mole instead of the moles of gas.** 4 mol of solid iron becoming 2 mol of solid oxide is not what makes rusting's $\Delta S$ negative; the 3 mol of oxygen gas are.
- **Looking at how much gas is made instead of the change.** 10 mol of gas made from 9 mol is a gain of 1.
- **Writing $\Delta S = 0$ when the moles of gas are equal.** It is small, not zero, and its sign needs data.
- **Saying a gas in a rigid container cannot change its entropy.** Warming it spreads its energy more widely.
- **Saying a change with a negative $\Delta S$ cannot happen.** Iron rusts; ammonia and hydrogen chloride make smoke.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Entropy, $S$, measures how widely the matter and the energy of a system are spread out. $\Delta S$ is positive when it rises and negative when it falls.
- For one substance, entropy rises from solid to liquid and rises far more from liquid to gas, so vaporizing has a larger $\Delta S$ than melting. A gas that expands at constant temperature gains entropy.
- For a reaction, count the moles of gas on each side first. More moles of gas among the products means $\Delta S > 0$, fewer means $\Delta S < 0$, and as a rule the larger the change in the moles of gas, the larger $\Delta S$.
- When the moles of gas in a reaction do not change, $\Delta S$ is small and its sign cannot be predicted without data.
- Warming a sample spreads its particles over a wider range of kinetic energies, so entropy rises with temperature.
- A change that lowers the entropy of the system can still happen. Topic 9.3 shows how entropy and energy are weighed together.

</KeyPoints>
