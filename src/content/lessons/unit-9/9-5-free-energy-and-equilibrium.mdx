Topic 7.5 said that at 25 °C the equilibrium constant for carbon dioxide falling apart into carbon monoxide and oxygen is about 10⁻⁹⁰, so small that you would need tens of millions of liters of the gas to expect to find one CO molecule. Nobody found that number by counting molecules. It was worked out from a table of free energies, with one equation. This lesson is about that equation.

## Which side of 1

Topic 9.3 gave the rule: a reaction is thermodynamically favored when ΔG° is below zero. The little circle matters here. ΔG° is about one particular mixture: every substance in its standard state, which means every gas at 1 bar, which is very nearly 1 atm, and every solute at 1 M (topic 9.3). Put those numbers into the reaction quotient of topic 7.3 and every term is 1. So that mixture has $Q = 1$.

"Favored" says that this mixture, the one with $Q = 1$, reacts in the forward direction. Now recall topic 7.3: a mixture reacts until $Q$ equals $K$, and it runs forward when $Q$ is below $K$. So a favored reaction has $K$ above 1.

The other two cases go the same way. If ΔG° is above zero, the standard mixture runs backward, so $K$ is below 1. If ΔG° is exactly zero, the standard mixture is already at equilibrium, so $K = 1$.

| ΔG° | The mixture with Q = 1 | So K is |
|---|---|---|
| below zero | runs forward | above 1 |
| zero | is at equilibrium | exactly 1 |
| above zero | runs backward | below 1 |

In topic 7.5's terms: with $K$ above 1 the top of the $K$ fraction, the products, is the larger at equilibrium, and with $K$ below 1 the bottom is.

So "thermodynamically favored" and "$K$ is greater than 1" are one statement. This is the first thing to check in any calculation: the sign of ΔG° tells you the side of 1 that $K$ must land on.

<KeyIdea>

The sign of ΔG° tells which way the standard mixture, which has $Q = 1$, has to go to reach equilibrium, and so it fixes the side of 1 on which $K$ lies. ΔG° below zero puts $K$ above 1, ΔG° above zero puts $K$ below 1, and ΔG° = 0 puts $K$ at exactly 1.

</KeyIdea>

## From one to the other

The sign gives the side. One equation gives the size.

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K
$$

It is on the equation sheet. $R$ is the gas constant in its energy form, 8.314 J/(mol·K), $T$ is in kelvins, and $\ln$ is the natural logarithm, the "ln" key of a calculator.

Read the signs off it. $R$ and $T$ are positive, and $\ln K$ is positive when $K$ is above 1 and negative when $K$ is below 1. So the minus sign in front makes ΔG° negative exactly when $K$ is above 1, as the table says.

Often you have ΔG° and want $K$. Divide both sides by $-RT$, then undo the logarithm with the $e^x$ key:

$$
K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ/RT}
$$

This form is not on the equation sheet.

**The unit trap of this topic.** $R$ is in joules, so $RT$ is in joules per mole, and ΔG° must be in joules per mole too. But tables and topic 9.3 give ΔG° in kilojoules, and nothing on the equation sheet reminds you. Convert on a line of its own, every time: into joules before ΔG° goes into the exponent, and back into kilojoules after it comes out of $-RT\ln K$.

**Which K.** The $K$ of this equation is the one written with pressures in atm for gases ($K_p$) and molarities for solutes ($K_c$), as topic 7.3 writes them, because those are the standard states that ΔG° is defined for. A question says which one it is. $K$ has no unit.

Some reactions have only pure solids and pure liquids in them. Topic 7.3 leaves pure solids and liquids out of a $K$ expression, so such a reaction has nothing in its $K$ expression, and there is no $K$ to compute for it. Its ΔG° still tells which direction is favored. Never report a $K$ for a reaction like that.

<Worked title="K from ΔG°">

Phosphorus trichloride and chlorine combine to make phosphorus pentachloride, all three as gases:

$$
\ce{PCl3(g) + Cl2(g) <=> PCl5(g)}
$$

At 298 K, ΔG° = −37.2 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$. What is $K_p$ at 298 K?

1. **Check the sign first.** ΔG° is negative, so $K_p$ must come out above 1.

2. **Convert to joules.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &-37.2\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
   &\quad = -37{,}200\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Find the exponent.** The minus sign of the equation and the minus sign of ΔG° make a plus.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= \frac{37{,}200}{(8.314)(298)} \\
   &= \frac{37{,}200}{2{,}477.6} \\
   &= 15.01
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The units cancel: joules per mole over joules per mole.

4. **Take $e$ to that power.**

   $$
   K_p = e^{15.01} = 3.3 \times 10^{6}
   $$

5. **Check it.** Above 1, as predicted, and large: at equilibrium the pressure of PCl₅ is far greater than the product of the other two pressures.

- About 3 × 10⁴⁰, so essentially all ethane at equilibrium

<div slot="why">

The sign first: ΔG° is negative, so $K_p$ is above 1. Then put ΔG° in joules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-100.4\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
&\quad = -100{,}400\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

The exponent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= \frac{100{,}400}{(8.314)(298)} \\
&= 40.52
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_p = e^{40.52} = 4 \times 10^{17}
$$

Topic 7.5 said this $K$ is "greater than 10¹⁷", and this is where that came from.

The first option has the sign of the exponent reversed, which gives $1/K$. The second leaves ΔG° in kilojoules, so the exponent is 0.0405 and $K$ comes out next to 1. The third takes 40.5 as the power of ten: it is the power of $e$.

The solid is left out, so $K_p$ is the pressure of the vapor. At 25 °C that pressure is 4.1 × 10⁻⁴ atm. What is ΔG° for the change at 298 K?

1. **Write $K$.**

   $$
   K_p = 4.1 \times 10^{-4}
   $$

2. **Predict the sign.** $K_p$ is below 1, so ΔG° must come out positive.

## How far from 1

The equation has ΔG° divided by $RT$, so what counts is the size of ΔG° beside $RT$. At 298 K:

$$
RT = (8.314)(298) = 2{,}478\ \text{J/mol}
$$

That is about 2.5 kJ/mol, and it is a small energy. A ΔG° of a few kilojoules is "about $RT$". A ΔG° of a hundred kilojoules is "forty times $RT$".

Keep two cases in mind. When ΔG° is within a kilojoule or two of zero, the exponent is a small number and $K$ is near 1. When the size of ΔG° is many times $RT$, $K$ is far from 1: enormous if ΔG° is negative, tiny if it is positive.

The tie is exponential. Each time ΔG° falls by another 5.70 kJ/mol at 298 K, $K$ is multiplied by ten. Here is where the 5.70 comes from: a factor of ten in $K$ adds $\ln 10 = 2.3026$ to $\ln K$, and that many times $RT$, in kJ/mol, is

$$
2.3026 \times 2.4776 = 5.70
$$

So a ΔG° of −5.7 kJ gives a $K$ of 10, −11.4 kJ gives 100, −17.1 kJ gives 1,000, and +5.7 kJ gives 0.1.

Topic 7.5 had a rule of thumb: above about 10³, mostly products. At 25 °C a $K$ of 10³ is only −17 kJ/mol. Many reactions have a ΔG° far larger in size than that, which is why so many reactions go, for practical purposes, all the way or not at all.

This is also the way to estimate without a calculator: divide ΔG° in kJ by 5.7 for the power of ten of $K$ at 298 K, with the sign reversed.

</KeyIdea>

## A positive ΔG° is not a zero

The explorer at +5 kJ/mol held 12 particles of B in every 100, and the iodine jar holds vapor. "Thermodynamically unfavored" does not mean that no product forms. It means that $K$ is below 1: starting from reactants alone, the reaction runs forward until $Q$ reaches $K$, and the net change ends there, with the top of the $K$ fraction smaller than the bottom.

How much smaller depends on the size of ΔG° beside $RT$. At +5 kJ/mol the product is about an eighth of the mixture. At +60 kJ/mol it is a trace too small to find: 60 ÷ 5.7 is between 10 and 11 factors of ten, so $K$ is near 10⁻¹¹.

The mirror statement holds too: "thermodynamically favored" does not mean "goes to completion". At −5 kJ/mol, 12 particles in 100 stayed A. As topic 7.5 put it, $K$ is never infinite and never zero.

## ΔG° does not change as the reaction runs

ΔG° is a property of the equation and the temperature, exactly as $K$ is. It compares reactants and products in their standard states. Nothing that happens in the flask changes it: not the starting amounts, not how far the reaction has run, not reaching equilibrium.

So at equilibrium ΔG° is not zero, unless $K$ happens to be 1. The iodine jar of the second worked example has ΔG° = +19.3 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ when the crystals first go in, while the violet is still deepening, and after the vapor has settled at 4.1 × 10⁻⁴ atm.

**Beyond the exam:** the quantity that does change as a reaction runs is written ΔG, without the circle. For a mixture with reaction quotient $Q$ it is

$$
\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q
$$

It is negative while $Q$ is below $K$, and it reaches zero at equilibrium, where $Q = K$. Setting it to zero there gives $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$. This equation is not on the equation sheet.

## K at another temperature

$K$ changes with temperature (topic 7.9), and now you can say by how much. $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ holds at any temperature, as long as the ΔG° is the one for that temperature.

Formation values give ΔG° at 25 °C only. For any other temperature, topic 9.3's second route gives it:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ
$$

with ΔH° and ΔS° taken as the same at every temperature.

Two steps, then: find ΔG° at the new temperature, then find $K$ from it, with the new $T$ in $RT$ as well.

<Worked title="The same reaction at 600 K">

For the reaction of the first worked example,

$$
\ce{PCl3(g) + Cl2(g) <=> PCl5(g)}
$$

the enthalpy and entropy changes are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H^\circ &= -87.9\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
\Delta S^\circ &= -170.3\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Find $K_p$ at 600 K.

1. **Convert the entropy change.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &-170.3\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)} \\
   &\quad = -0.1703\ \text{kJ/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Find the entropy term.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   T\Delta S^\circ &= (600)(-0.1703) \\
   &= -102.2\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Find ΔG° at 600 K.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta G^\circ &= -87.9 - (-102.2) \\
   &= +14.3\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   It is +14.28 before rounding.

4. **Predict.** ΔG° is now positive, so $K_p$ must come out below 1.

5. **Convert to joules.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &+14.28\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
   &\quad = +14{,}280\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Find $K$.** The $T$ in $RT$ is 600 K now, not 298 K.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= \frac{-14{,}280}{(8.314)(600)} \\
   &= \frac{-14{,}280}{4{,}988.4} \\
   &= -2.863
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   K_p = e^{-2.863} = 0.057
   $$

7. **Read it.** From 3.3 × 10⁶ at 298 K to 0.057 at 600 K. The reaction is exothermic, and topic 7.9 said that the $K$ of an exothermic reaction falls on heating. The numbers agree. For this reaction you can watch it happen in ΔG°: ΔS° is negative, so the term −TΔS° is positive and grows with $T$, and ΔG° climbs from −37.2 to +14.3 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$. That is this reaction, not the rule: an exothermic reaction with a positive ΔS° has a ΔG° that falls on heating, and its $K$ falls all the same, because $K$ goes by ΔG° divided by $RT$, and $RT$ grows too. It passes through zero at ΔH° divided by ΔS° (topic 9.3):

   $$
   T = \frac{87.9}{0.1703} = 516\ \text{K}
   $$

   That is the temperature at which $K = 1$.

</Worked>

For this reaction ΔH° = −136.4 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ and ΔS° = −120.8 $\text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}$. Chemical plants make ethene from ethane at temperatures near 1,000 K. What is $K_p$ for the reaction as written at 1,000 K?

<div slot="why">

ΔS° in kilojoules first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-120.8\ \text{J/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)} \\
&\quad = -0.1208\ \text{kJ/(mol}_\text{rxn}\text{·K)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The entropy term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T\Delta S^\circ &= (1{,}000)(-0.1208) \\
&= -120.8\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

ΔG° at 1,000 K:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -136.4 - (-120.8) \\
&= -15.6\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

In joules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-15.6\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
&\quad = -15{,}600\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Negative, so $K_p$ is above 1.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT} &= \frac{15{,}600}{(8.314)(1{,}000)} \\
&= 1.876
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K_p = e^{1.876} = 6.5
$$

At 298 K this $K_p$ was 4 × 10¹⁷. At 1,000 K it is 6.5, small enough that the reverse reaction goes a good part of the way: a good share of the ethane breaks up into ethene and hydrogen, which is why the plants run hot.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Eight mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Leaving ΔG° in kilojoules inside $e^{-\Delta G^\circ/RT}$.** $R$ is in joules. With kilojoules the exponent comes out a thousand times too small and $K$ lands next to 1, whatever the reaction.
- **Reporting the joules that $-RT\ln K$ gives as kilojoules.** Convert on a line of its own.
- **Losing the sign.** Decide from the sign of ΔG° which side of 1 the $K$ must be on before you compute, and from $K$ which sign ΔG° must have.
- **Using the log key for ln,** or reading $\ln K$ as the power of ten.
- **Saying ΔG° = 0 at equilibrium.** ΔG° is fixed for an equation at a temperature. It is zero only if $K = 1$.
- **Reading a positive ΔG° as "no reaction".** It means $K$ below 1: some product forms, and how much depends on the size of ΔG° beside $RT$.
- **Reading a negative ΔG° as "goes to completion" or as "fast".** $K$ is never infinite, and speed is topic 9.4's subject.
- **Using the 298 K value of ΔG° at another temperature,** or 298 in $RT$ when $K$ is wanted at another temperature.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A thermodynamically favored reaction (ΔG° below zero) has $K$ above 1, a thermodynamically unfavored one (ΔG° above zero) has $K$ below 1, and ΔG° of zero goes with $K$ of exactly 1.
- ΔG° equals $-RT\ln K$, so $K$ is $e$ raised to the power $-\Delta G^\circ/RT$, with $R$ = 8.314 J/(mol·K), $T$ in kelvins and ΔG° in joules per mole. The $K$ is $K_p$, with pressures in atm, for gases and $K_c$ for solutes.
- What counts is the size of ΔG° beside $RT$, about 2.5 kJ/mol at 298 K. Near zero, $K$ is near 1, and at 298 K every 5.7 kJ/mol is a factor of ten in $K$.
- A positive ΔG° means a small $K$, not zero, so some product still forms. A negative ΔG° means a large $K$, not an infinite one.
- ΔG° belongs to the equation and the temperature and does not change as the mixture reacts, so it is not zero at equilibrium unless $K$ is 1.
- For $K$ at another temperature, find ΔG° there from ΔH° minus TΔS°, and use that temperature in $RT$.

</KeyPoints>
