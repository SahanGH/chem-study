Turn over the button cell from a watch and you will often find a number stamped into the steel: 1.55 V. Topic 9.8 showed where the cell's current comes from: zinc gives up electrons, silver oxide takes them, and the two are kept apart so that the electrons must go through the watch. This lesson is about the number: what a volt measures, why this pair of substances gives about one and a half of them, and what that has to do with ΔG°.

## What a volt measures

Electrons in the wire of a galvanic cell go from the anode to the cathode (topic 9.8) because the cell's reaction is thermodynamically favored. The **cell potential**, $E_\text{cell}$, measures how hard the reaction pushes them: it is the energy that each coulomb of charge gives up on its way through the wire from the anode to the cathode. Its unit is the volt, and one volt is one joule per coulomb. The equation sheet prints that line. A meter joined to the two electrodes reads the cell potential.

The potential depends on the concentrations, which is topic 9.10's subject, so chemists agree on one set of conditions for the tables. The **standard cell potential**, $E^\circ_\text{cell}$, is the cell potential when every substance is in its standard state: every solute at 1 M, every gas at 1 bar, which is very nearly 1 atm, and every solid and liquid pure. The values in this lesson are for 25 °C. The little circle means what it meant on ΔH° and ΔG° (topics 6.8 and 9.3).

## A table of reductions

A meter can only compare two half-cells. No half-reaction runs alone (topic 4.9), so no half-cell has a potential that can be measured alone. Chemists therefore chose one half-reaction as the zero of the scale, the reduction of hydrogen ions to hydrogen gas, and gave every other half-reaction the potential it shows against that one. The result is the **standard reduction potential**, $E^\circ$, of a half-reaction: a number in volts that says how strongly the half-reaction pulls toward reduction, on a scale where hydrogen's is 0.00 V by definition.

Tables write the half-reaction as a reduction, electrons on the left, whichever way it will run in your cell. These are the values this lesson uses. An exam question gives the ones it needs.

| Half-reaction, written as a reduction | $E^\circ$ (V) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Ag^+(aq) + e^- -> Ag(s)}$ | +0.80 |
| $\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Cu(s)}$ | +0.34 |
| $\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2e^- -> H2(g)}$ | 0.00 |
| $\ce{Sn^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Sn(s)}$ | −0.14 |
| $\ce{Co^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Co(s)}$ | −0.28 |
| $\ce{Cd^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Cd(s)}$ | −0.40 |
| $\ce{Cr^3+(aq) + 3e^- -> Cr(s)}$ | −0.74 |

Here is what the order means. The higher a half-reaction stands, the more strongly it pulls toward reduction. Ag⁺ at the top takes electrons more readily than any other ion in this table. Chromium metal at the bottom is the one whose half-reaction is most easily pushed backward: of these metals it gives up electrons most readily. A negative value is not a verdict on the half-reaction. It only says the half-reaction stands below hydrogen's.

Put two half-cells together and they compete for the electrons. **The half-reaction with the higher reduction potential wins: it runs as written, as the reduction, at the cathode. The other is forced to run backward, as the oxidation, at the anode.** That is the rule for a galvanic cell at standard conditions.

The standard cell potential is the gap between the two.

$$
E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}
$$

Both values go in exactly as the table prints them, as reduction potentials, signs included. Do not change the sign of the anode's value before you subtract: the minus sign in the equation already accounts for that half-reaction running backward. This equation is not on the equation sheet.

Take cobalt and tin. Tin's half-reaction is the higher of the two, −0.14 V against −0.28 V, so Sn²⁺ is reduced and cobalt is oxidized:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\circ_\text{cell} &= -0.14 - (-0.28) \\
&= +0.14\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

Two negative potentials still make a cell with a positive one.

<KeyIdea>

In a galvanic cell at standard conditions, the half-reaction with the higher standard reduction potential is the reduction, at the cathode. The standard cell potential is the cathode's value minus the anode's, both taken from the table as they stand.

</KeyIdea>

## From volts to free energy

A volt is a joule per coulomb, so a cell potential times the charge that passes is an energy. For one mole of reaction the charge is $n$ moles of electrons, and each mole of electrons carries 96,485 C. That number is **Faraday's constant**, $F$: the charge of one mole of electrons, 96,485 C/mol. So $nF$ is the charge in coulombs, and $nFE^\circ$ is the electrical energy the reaction can deliver, in joules. The free energy of the chemicals falls by that much.

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ
$$

This equation is on the equation sheet, and so is the value of $F$.

There are three things to get right.

- **$n$ is counted once the electrons match.** It is the number of moles of electrons that pass from the anode to the cathode for one mole of reaction: the number that canceled when the two half-reactions were added (topic 4.9). A half-reaction on its own may have been multiplied first, so its electron count can be too small.
- **The answer is in joules.** Volts times coulombs are joules. ΔG° values are quoted in $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$, so the conversion is a line of its own.
- **The minus sign.** A positive $E^\circ$ gives a negative ΔG°: a cell that runs by itself runs a thermodynamically favored reaction. A negative $E^\circ$ gives a positive ΔG°.

**A potential is never multiplied.** To make the electrons cancel you often multiply a half-reaction by 2 or 3. Its $E^\circ$ stays as the table printed it. A potential is energy per coulomb. Doubling a half-reaction doubles the coulombs and doubles the joules, and the joules per coulomb do not change, just as the price per kilogram does not change when you buy two kilograms. The amount of reaction does matter to the energy, and it comes in through $n$: that is what $n$ is for.

<KeyIdea>

$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$. Balancing the electrons may mean multiplying a half-reaction, and its $E^\circ$ stays as the table printed it. The size of the reaction enters once, through $n$, the moles of electrons in the balanced overall equation.

</KeyIdea>

   $$

   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Cd(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Cd^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Check: the charge is +2 on each side.

4. **Count the electrons.** Two electrons canceled, so $n = 2$.

5. **Find ΔG°, in joules first.** With $F$ in coulombs per mole of electrons and $E^\circ$ in volts, the answer is in joules for each mole of reaction.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta G^\circ &= -(2)(96{,}485)(1.20) \\
   &= -231{,}564
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Convert, and check the sign.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &-231{,}564\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
   &\quad = -232\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The potential is positive and ΔG° is negative, as they must be for a cell that runs by itself.

## Potential, free energy and K on one scale

Topic 9.5 tied ΔG° to the equilibrium constant:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K
$$

This lesson ties ΔG° to the cell potential. So for a redox reaction the three numbers cannot be chosen separately: fix one and the other two follow.

The three rulers below are drawn for a reaction in which 2 mol of electrons pass, at 298 K, and any vertical line across them picks out a matching $E^\circ_\text{cell}$, ΔG° and $K$.

Read the figure outward from its dashed line. To the right of the line, the half-reaction written as the reduction has the higher reduction potential, so cathode minus anode is positive. Then $nFE^\circ$ is a positive number of joules, energy the reaction can deliver, so ΔG° is negative, and topic 9.5 puts $K$ above 1. To the left, the half-reaction written as the reduction has the lower potential. Cathode minus anode is negative, $nFE^\circ$ is energy that would have to be supplied, ΔG° is positive and $K$ is below 1. On the line itself the two half-reactions pull equally hard, as two identical half-cells would: nothing is gained in either direction, and $K$ is 1.

**K from a cell potential** takes the two steps you already have, and no new equation: $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ gives ΔG°, and ΔG° gives $K$. For the cadmium and silver cell, ΔG° is −231,564 $\text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}$. Keep the joules, because $R$ is in joules.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln K &= \frac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT} \\
&= \frac{231{,}564}{(8.314)(298)} \\
&= 93.46
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
K = e^{93.46} = 4 \times 10^{40}
$$

That is far off the right-hand end of the figure's bottom ruler: a potential of little more than a volt belongs to a reaction that goes, for every practical purpose, to completion. This $K$ is $K_c$, written with the molarities of the two ions. Topic 7.3 leaves the solid metals out.

A large $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ says the reaction is strongly favored. It says nothing about how fast the reaction goes (topic 9.4).

## When the potential is negative

Write a cell reaction in the direction that is not favored and the subtraction says so. Take the explorer's first cell, tin with silver, and write its equation backward:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2Ag(s) + Sn^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2Ag^+(aq) + Sn(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

As written, silver is oxidized, so now silver's half-reaction is the anode's and tin's is the cathode's.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\circ_\text{cell} &= -0.14 - 0.80 \\
&= -0.94\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, in joules for each mole of reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -(2)(96{,}485)(-0.94) \\
&= +181{,}392
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&+181{,}392\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
&\quad = +181\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reaction as written is thermodynamically unfavored. Compare the explorer's readout for the forward cell, 0.94 V and −181 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$. Writing a reaction backward changes the sign of $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ and the sign of ΔG°, and it leaves the size of each as it was.

A reaction with a negative $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ cannot run a galvanic cell at standard conditions. It can be made to go in an electrolytic cell (topic 9.8), where a power supply pushes the electrons the way the reaction will not send them. The supply must provide more than the size of the negative potential: here, more than 0.94 V at standard conditions.

<KeyIdea>

A positive $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ means a negative ΔG° and a $K$ greater than 1: a favored reaction, which can run a galvanic cell. A negative $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ means a positive ΔG° and a $K$ less than 1: an unfavored reaction, which cannot run a galvanic cell at standard conditions. A power supply can drive it in an electrolytic cell.

</KeyIdea>

A student writes this equation for a cell:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Sn(s) + Cd^2+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Sn^2+(aq) + Cd(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The standard reduction potentials are −0.14 V for Sn²⁺/Sn and −0.40 V for Cd²⁺/Cd. Which statement about the reaction as written, at standard conditions, is correct?

- E° is +0.26 V: favored, so it could run a galvanic cell as written
- E° is −0.26 V, so ΔG° is negative and K is greater than 1
- E° is −0.26 V: unfavored, but a power supply could drive it
- E° is −0.26 V: favored, but too slow to give a measurable current

<div slot="why">

As written, tin is oxidized and Cd²⁺ is reduced, so cadmium's half-reaction is the cathode's:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\circ &= -0.40 - (-0.14) \\
&= -0.26\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

A negative potential means a positive ΔG°. In joules for each mole of reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -(2)(96{,}485)(-0.26) \\
&= +50{,}172
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&+50{,}172\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
&\quad = +50\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $K$ is below 1, and the reaction is unfavored as written. An electrolytic cell with a supply of more than 0.26 V could force it.

The first option subtracts the wrong way around: it is the potential of the reverse reaction. The second forgets the minus sign in $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$. The fourth mixes up favored with fast: a potential says nothing about rate.

</div>

A fuel cell makes electricity from methanol and oxygen:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3OH(l)} + \tfrac{3}{2}\,\ce{O2(g)} \ce{->} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For this reaction ΔG° = −702.0 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ at 25 °C. In it the oxidation number of carbon goes from −2 to +4. What is the standard cell potential, in V? ($F$ = 96,485 C per mole of electrons.)

<div slot="why">

One carbon atom reacts in each mole of reaction and its oxidation number rises by 6, so 6 mol of electrons pass: $n = 6$. The oxygen agrees: three oxygen atoms each go from 0 to −2.

ΔG° must be in joules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-702.0\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn} \\
&\quad = -702{,}000\ \text{J/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranged, the equation gives the potential in volts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\circ &= \frac{-\Delta G^\circ}{nF} \\
&= \frac{702{,}000}{(6)(96{,}485)} \\
&= 1.21
\end{aligned}
$$

It is 1.2126 before rounding. A favored reaction, a negative ΔG°, a positive potential.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Multiplying a potential by a coefficient.** The half-reaction gets the coefficient. Its $E^\circ$ keeps the table's value, because a potential is energy per coulomb.
- **Changing the sign of the anode's value and then subtracting it as well.** Both values go into cathode minus anode exactly as the table prints them.
- **Using one half-reaction's electron count as $n$.** Chromium's half-reaction shows 3 electrons and tin(II)'s shows 2, but the equation they add up to passes 6 for each mole of reaction.
- **Leaving $-nFE^\circ$ in joules** and calling it kilojoules, or putting kilojoules into $-RT\ln K$.
- **Reading a positive $E^\circ$ as a positive ΔG°.** The signs are opposite: a positive potential is a favored reaction.
- **Taking the beaker on the left for the anode.** The anode is where oxidation happens, wherever it stands.
- **Reading a large potential as a fast reaction.**

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A standard reduction potential, $E^\circ$, says how strongly a half-reaction pulls toward reduction, on a scale where the reduction of H⁺(aq) to H₂(g) is 0.00 V. Tables write every half-reaction as a reduction.
- In a galvanic cell at standard conditions the half-reaction with the higher $E^\circ$ is the reduction, at the cathode. $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ is the cathode's value minus the anode's, with both values as the table prints them.
- Multiplying a half-reaction to balance the electrons never changes its potential, because a volt is a joule per coulomb.
- $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$, where $n$ is the moles of electrons in the balanced overall equation and $F$ is 96,485 C per mole of electrons. The answer is in joules.
- $E^\circ_\text{cell}$, ΔG° and $K$ of a redox reaction fix one another. When the cathode's reduction potential is the higher one, $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ is positive, ΔG° is negative and $K$ is greater than 1. $K$ comes from $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ in two steps, through ΔG°.
- A reaction with a negative $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ is thermodynamically unfavored: it cannot run a galvanic cell at standard conditions, and a power supply can drive it in an electrolytic cell.

</KeyPoints>
