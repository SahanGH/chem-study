Magnesium hydride is a light gray powder, a solid that is part hydrogen, and engineers study it as a way to carry hydrogen without a high-pressure tank. Sealed in a dry can at room temperature, it stays magnesium hydride. Heat it enough and the hydrogen comes back out as a gas, leaving magnesium metal behind. Nothing was added, and the reaction takes in the same energy hot or cold. This lesson builds the one number that says which way a change is favored, and shows why that number can change its sign when the temperature does.

## One number for two tendencies

Two things work for a change. One is releasing energy: a negative ΔH° (Unit 6). The other is an increase in entropy: a positive ΔS° (topics 9.1 and 9.2). When both point the same way, the verdict is easy. Often they disagree. The xylitol of topic 6.1 takes energy in as it dissolves, and dissolves anyway. Magnesium hydride giving up its hydrogen takes energy in and gains entropy, because a solid gives a gas. Something has to weigh the two.

The **Gibbs free energy**, $G$, is a property of a system that combines its enthalpy and its entropy, and what matters is its change. The **standard free energy change**, $\Delta G^\circ$, is the change in free energy when the reactants turn into the products with every substance in its standard state. From here on, this lesson just says "free energy".

The circle means what it meant in topic 6.8: each substance pure and at 1 bar (very nearly 1 atm). One thing is added here: for a dissolved substance, the standard state is a concentration of 1 M. Tables give values for 25 °C.

Now the rule. A process is **thermodynamically favored** when $\Delta G^\circ < 0$. It is **thermodynamically unfavored** when $\Delta G^\circ > 0$, and then the reverse process is the favored one, because reversing a process reverses the sign of its $\Delta G^\circ$, as it does for ΔH° (topic 6.9).

What does "favored" mean? Starting from everything in its standard state, the change can go forward by itself, with nothing driving it from outside. It does not mean that the change is fast: topic 9.4 takes that up. And it does not mean that the reactants are all used up: how far a favored change goes is topic 9.5's question.

Older books call a thermodynamically favored process "spontaneous". The word means the same thing, but it sounds like "sudden", and ΔG° says nothing about speed, so this course and the exam say "thermodynamically favored". Some exam questions write "thermodynamically favorable", which means exactly the same.

Like ΔH°, ΔG° goes with the equation as written, so its unit is $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$ (topic 6.6). Tables, questions and the answer boxes in this lesson shorten that to kJ/mol, and J/(mol·K) is short for joules per kelvin for each mole of reaction: this is the shortening topic 6.6 described. The sign of ΔG° is part of it, and a plus sign is written.

<KeyIdea>

$\Delta G^\circ < 0$: thermodynamically favored. $\Delta G^\circ > 0$: thermodynamically unfavored, and the reverse is favored. The sign tells you the direction. It does not tell you the speed.

</KeyIdea>

## ΔG° from a table

The first way to get ΔG° is the way topic 6.8 got ΔH°. The **standard free energy of formation**, $\Delta G_f^\circ$, of a compound is the free energy change for forming one mole of it from its elements in their standard states. Tables list it in kJ/mol at 25 °C.

An element in its standard state has $\Delta G_f^\circ = 0$, for the reason its $\Delta H_f^\circ$ is zero: forming it from itself changes nothing. It goes into the sum as zero. This is not true of its $S^\circ$, which is never zero at 25 °C (topic 9.2).

<KeyIdea>

The standard free energy change of a reaction is the products' total minus the reactants' total:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ ={} & \textstyle\sum n\,\Delta G_f^\circ(\text{products}) \\
& - \textstyle\sum n\,\Delta G_f^\circ(\text{reactants})
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $n$ is each substance's coefficient in the balanced equation.

</KeyIdea>

The equation sheet prints this sum without the $n$, as it does the enthalpy sum, and the coefficients are yours to put in. No value of $\Delta G_f^\circ$ is on the sheet: a question gives each one it needs.

One limit: tables of $\Delta G_f^\circ$ are for 25 °C, so this route gives ΔG° at 25 °C and at no other temperature.

<Worked title="Magnesium hydride from the table">

Is this reaction thermodynamically favored at 25 °C?

$$
\ce{MgH2(s) -> Mg(s) + H2(g)}
$$

$\Delta G_f^\circ$ of MgH₂(s) is −35.9 kJ/mol.

1. **Write a value under every substance.** All are in kJ/mol.

   - MgH₂(s): −35.9
   - Mg(s): 0, an element in its standard state
   - H₂(g): 0, the same

2. **Add up the products, with coefficients.**

   $$
   1(0) + 1(0) = 0\ \text{kJ}
   $$

3. **Add up the reactants, with coefficients.**

   $$
   1(-35.9) = -35.9\ \text{kJ}
   $$

4. **Products minus reactants.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \Delta G^\circ &= 0 - (-35.9) \\
   &= +35.9\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Read the sign.** It is positive: at 25 °C, magnesium hydride giving up its hydrogen is thermodynamically unfavored. The reverse, magnesium taking up hydrogen to make the hydride, is the favored direction at this temperature, with $\Delta G^\circ$ of −35.9 $\text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}$.

</Worked>

## ΔG° from ΔH° and ΔS°

The second way shows where free energy comes from.

$$
\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ
$$

Here $T$ is the temperature in kelvins. This equation is on the equation sheet.

Read it as two terms. $\Delta H^\circ$ is the enthalpy term: it is negative when the reaction releases energy, and a negative value pulls ΔG° down. $-T\Delta S^\circ$ is the entropy term: when ΔS° is positive this term is negative, and it too pulls ΔG° down. So the equation says what the first section said in words: releasing energy works for a change, and so does gaining entropy. When the two disagree, the larger term wins.

"Exothermic" is not "favored". A reaction can release energy and still be unfavored, if it loses enough entropy and the temperature is high enough. One can take energy in and still be favored. The sign that decides is the sign of ΔG°.

There is a trap in the units. Tables give ΔH° in kilojoules and S° in joules per kelvin, so ΔS° comes out in J/(mol·K). The two terms cannot be subtracted until they are in the same unit, and nothing on the equation sheet reminds you. **Turn ΔS° into kJ/K first, on a line of its own, before you do anything else with it:** divide by 1,000.

<KeyIdea>

$\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$, with $T$ in kelvins and both terms in kilojoules. Convert ΔS° from J/K to kJ/K before you multiply by $T$.

</KeyIdea>

## Heat it, and only one term moves

Look at the equation again. $T$ multiplies ΔS° and nothing else. Over the temperatures of a problem, ΔH° and ΔS° themselves change very little, and this course treats them as constant. That is an approximation. So when the temperature goes up, the enthalpy term stays where it is and the entropy term grows.

For magnesium hydride that is the whole story of the opening. Its entropy term works for the reaction. Cold, that term is too small to beat the +75.3 kJ of the enthalpy term. Hot enough, it is not.

The route by formation values cannot follow this: its table is for 25 °C. $\Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$ works at any temperature.

The temperature at which the verdict changes is the temperature at which $\Delta G^\circ = 0$. Set the equation to zero and solve:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ \\
T &= \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{\Delta S^\circ}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both must be in the same energy unit, so that the answer is in kelvins.

## Four combinations of signs

ΔH° can be negative or positive, and so can ΔS°. That makes four combinations, and the equation settles each one. You do not need to memorize them. Reason each one out from a single fact: temperature multiplies only the entropy term.

**Both terms negative** (ΔH° negative, ΔS° positive). The reaction releases energy and gains entropy. Nothing works against it. ΔG° is negative at every temperature.

**Both terms positive** (ΔH° positive, ΔS° negative). The reaction takes in energy and loses entropy. Nothing works for it. ΔG° is positive at every temperature, and the reverse reaction is the favored one at every temperature.

**ΔH° positive and ΔS° positive.** The enthalpy term works against the reaction, the entropy term works for it, and heating strengthens the entropy term. The reaction is favored at high temperature, above ΔH°/ΔS°. Magnesium hydride is this case.

**ΔH° negative and ΔS° negative.** The enthalpy term works for the reaction, the entropy term works against it, and heating strengthens the entropy term. The reaction is favored at low temperature, below ΔH°/ΔS°. The white smoke of ammonium chloride is this case.

A word of care: "at every temperature" is a statement about the equation, with ΔH° and ΔS° taken as constant and each substance in the state the equation gives it.

The ratio ΔH°/ΔS° means something only when the two signs match. With unlike signs it comes out negative, and no temperature in kelvins is negative: that is the equation's way of saying that the sign of ΔG° never changes.

<KeyIdea>

Temperature multiplies only the entropy term, so heating strengthens whichever way entropy points. If ΔS° is positive, heating helps the reaction. If ΔS° is negative, heating hurts it.

</KeyIdea>

## Choosing a route

Look at what the question hands you. A table of free energies of formation and a temperature of 25 °C call for the sum. Anything else calls for $\Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$: a given ΔH° and ΔS°, enthalpies of formation with standard entropies, or a temperature other than 25 °C. At 25 °C the two routes agree up to the rounding in the tables. For magnesium hydride both gave +35.9. For other reactions they can differ by a tenth or two.

A positive ΔG° does not mean that nothing can be done. The reverse is favored, a different temperature may change the sign, and topic 9.7 shows how an unfavored reaction can be driven.

**Beyond the exam:** the energy a reaction releases spreads out into the surroundings and raises their entropy, by $-\Delta H^\circ/T$. Add the system's own ΔS° to that and you get $-\Delta G^\circ/T$, so a negative ΔG° is another way of saying that the entropy of the system and its surroundings together goes up.

\begin{aligned}

Line 4: ΔG° is negative, so the white smoke is favored at 298 K.

Which is the first line that has a mistake in it?

- Line 1, where ΔS° is put into kJ/K
- Line 2, where the entropy term is found
- Line 3, where the two terms are combined
- Line 4, where the sign of ΔG° is read

<div slot="why">

Line 1 is right: −285.1 J/K is −0.2851 kJ/K. Line 2 is where it goes wrong. The entropy term is −TΔS°, and ΔS° is negative, so the term is a negative times a negative:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-T\Delta S^\circ &= -(298)(-0.2851) \\
&= +84.96\ \text{kJ}
\end{aligned}
$$

It is not −84.96. You can see that it must be positive without any arithmetic: two moles of gas become a solid, entropy is lost, and a loss of entropy works against a reaction.

Line 3 adds the two terms it was given without a slip, and line 4 reads a negative number correctly, so neither is the first mistake. With line 2 put right:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -176.2 + 84.96 \\
&= -91.2\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the classmate's verdict came out right all the same, since −261.2 and −91.2 are both negative. A right verdict does not show that the number behind it is right.

<Trap>

Eight mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Subtracting joules from kilojoules.** ΔS° comes out of the tables in J/K. Turn it into kJ/K on its own line, and go back to that line if the entropy term comes out hundreds of times larger than ΔH°.
- **Putting $T$ in °C.** The equation needs kelvins: 25 °C is 298 K.
- **Losing the double negative.** When ΔS° is negative, −TΔS° is positive and works against the reaction.
- **Reading "exothermic" as "favored".** The sign of ΔG° decides, and the entropy term can outweigh ΔH°.
- **Entering an element's $\Delta G_f^\circ$ as anything but zero, or leaving out a coefficient.** Elements in their standard states go in as zero, and every value is multiplied by its coefficient.
- **Using a table of $\Delta G_f^\circ$ at another temperature.** Those values are for 25 °C. Use $\Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$ elsewhere.
- **Dividing ΔH° by ΔS° when their signs differ.** The answer is negative and means nothing: the sign of ΔG° never changes.
- **Reading "favored" as "fast".** ΔG° says nothing about speed (topic 9.4).

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The standard free energy change, $\Delta G^\circ$, is the free energy change with every substance in its standard state: pure at 1 bar, and solutes at 1 M. A process is thermodynamically favored when $\Delta G^\circ < 0$ and thermodynamically unfavored when $\Delta G^\circ > 0$, in which case its reverse is favored.
- At 25 °C, ΔG° is the sum of $n\,\Delta G_f^\circ$ for the products minus the sum for the reactants, with every element in its standard state entered as zero.
- At any temperature, ΔG° is ΔH° minus $T\Delta S^\circ$, with $T$ in kelvins and ΔS° turned from J/K into kJ/K before it is multiplied by $T$.
- Temperature multiplies only the entropy term, so heating helps a reaction whose ΔS° is positive and hurts one whose ΔS° is negative.
- When ΔH° and ΔS° are taken as constant, a reaction with a negative ΔH° and a positive ΔS° is favored at every temperature, and one with a positive ΔH° and a negative ΔS° is favored at none.
- When ΔH° and ΔS° have the same sign, ΔG° changes sign at the temperature ΔH° divided by ΔS°: the reaction is favored above that temperature if both are positive, and below it if both are negative.

</KeyPoints>
