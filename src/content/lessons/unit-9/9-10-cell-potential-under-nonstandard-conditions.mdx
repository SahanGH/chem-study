Build the tin and silver cell of topic 9.9 with both solutions at 1.0 M, and the meter reads 0.94 V, as the table of reduction potentials says it should. Build it again with the same two strips, the same solution of tin(II) ions, and a silver nitrate solution of only 0.010 M, and the meter reads about 0.82 V. Nothing about the metals or the reaction has changed. This lesson is about where the missing 0.12 V went, and what a cell's reading says about the mixture inside it.

## E° is the reading at Q = 1

Here is the cell's reaction, as topic 9.9 found it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Sn(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Sn^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Two moles of electrons pass for each mole of reaction, so $n = 2$, and the standard cell potential is cathode minus anode:

$$
E^\circ_\text{cell} = 0.80 - (-0.14) = 0.94\ \text{V}
$$

Like any reaction, this one has a reaction quotient, $Q$ (topics 7.3 and 7.10): products over reactants, each concentration raised to its coefficient, with solids left out. For this cell:

$$
Q = \frac{[\ce{Sn^2+}]}{[\ce{Ag+}]^2}
$$

The two metal strips are solids, so they are not in it. The equation sheet does not say how to write $Q$. Write it as topic 7.3 writes $K$.

Standard conditions mean every solute at 1 M. Put 1.0 in for both ions:

$$
Q = \frac{1.0}{(1.0)^2} = 1
$$

So $E^\circ$ is the potential of the cell at one particular mixture, the standard one, which has $Q = 1$.

A real cell is rarely that mixture, so it needs a symbol of its own. The **cell potential**, $E$ or $E_\text{cell}$, written without the little circle, is the potential of the cell as it is now, whatever its concentrations. $E^\circ$ is one value of $E$: the value at $Q = 1$. At 25 °C a reaction has one $E^\circ$. A cell running that reaction can have many values of $E$.

<KeyIdea>

$E^\circ$ is the cell potential when $Q = 1$. $E$, with no circle, is the cell potential at whatever $Q$ the cell has now.

</KeyIdea>

## The potential says how far there is to go

Every reaction has an equilibrium constant. For the tin and silver cell, topic 9.9's two steps give it. First the potential gives the free energy change:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -nFE^\circ \\
&= -181\ \text{kJ/mol}_\text{rxn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ gives a $K$ of about 6 × 10³¹ at 25 °C, close to 10³². A mixture with $Q = 1$ is about 32 powers of ten short of equilibrium.

Now recall topic 7.10's rule: a mixture with $Q$ less than $K$ reacts forward, and $Q$ rises until it equals $K$. A cell is that same reaction with its electrons sent through a wire. The reading on the meter is the size of the push on the electrons at this moment. A mixture with $Q$ far below $K$ still has a long way to go, and the push is big. A mixture with $Q$ close to $K$ has almost nowhere left to go, and the push is small.

So to predict what a changed concentration does to the reading, find what it does to $Q$ and see whether $Q$ ends up nearer to $K$ than before or farther from it. For a galvanic cell, $Q$ is below $K$. A change that raises $Q$ takes the cell nearer equilibrium and lowers $E$. A change that lowers $Q$ takes it farther from equilibrium and raises $E$.

You may want to reach for Le Châtelier's principle here (topic 7.9). It does not fit. That principle is about a system that is at equilibrium and is then disturbed. A cell that shows a potential is not at equilibrium: it is on its way there. The argument that works for a mixture on its way is the one topic 7.10 gave, through $Q$.

<KeyIdea>

The reading on a cell's meter is large while the mixture has far to go to reach equilibrium and small when it has little left to go. A change that raises $Q$ toward $K$ lowers the potential. A change that lowers $Q$ away from $K$ raises it.

</KeyIdea>

## As the cell runs

Leave the tin and silver cell joined through its meter. Every second, tin atoms become Sn²⁺ ions and Ag⁺ ions become silver metal. The top of $Q$ grows and the bottom shrinks, so $Q$ climbs toward $K$ all the time the cell runs, and $E$ falls.

It passes through a filled circle at Q = 1 and E = 0.94 V, labeled Standard, and ends at an open square on the axis, where E = 0 and Q is near 10 to the power 32, labeled Equilibrium.   caption="The tin and silver cell at 25 °C. Its potential is E° at Q = 1, higher where Q is below 1, and falls to zero as Q climbs to K, which is about 10³²."
/>

The horizontal scale counts powers of ten of $Q$, and against that scale the potential falls along a straight line, from 0.94 V at $Q = 1$ to zero at $Q = K$.

The fall is slow at first. Suppose the cell starts with equal volumes of the two 1.0 M solutions. When 99 percent of the silver ions have reacted, [Ag⁺] is 0.010 M and [Sn²⁺] is 1.495 M, $Q$ is about 1.5 × 10⁴, and the reading is still about 0.82 V. Nearly all of the fall comes at the very end, as the last traces of Ag⁺ go.

At $Q = K$ the reaction is at equilibrium. Nothing pushes it either way, no electrons flow, and $E = 0$. That is what a run-down cell is: a mixture that has reached equilibrium.

$E^\circ$ has not changed. It belongs to the reaction, at $Q = 1$, and it is 0.94 V for this reaction at 25 °C whatever the cell in front of you is doing. Build the cell afresh from 1.0 M solutions and it reads 0.94 V again. What ran down is $E$.

## One metal, two concentrations

Now a cell that standard potentials say should do nothing. Put a cobalt strip into each of two beakers of cobalt(II) nitrate solution, one 1.0 M and deep pink, the other 0.0010 M and so pale it looks almost colorless (topic 7.9 met the pink ion). Join the strips through a meter and the solutions with a salt bridge. Both half-cells have the same half-reaction, so the two values in cathode minus anode are the same:

$$
E^\circ_\text{cell} = -0.28 - (-0.28) = 0\ \text{V}
$$

The meter still shows a small potential. Such a cell is a **concentration cell**: a cell whose two half-cells differ only in concentration.

Why does it read anything? Argue through $Q$. With $E^\circ = 0$, $\Delta G^\circ = 0$ and $K = 1$ (topic 9.9). The only change this cell can make is to move Co²⁺ from one solution to the other, in effect:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Co^2+}(\text{concentrated}) \ce{->} \\
&\quad \ce{Co^2+}(\text{dilute})
\end{aligned}
$$

So the product is the ion in the dilute solution and the reactant is the ion in the concentrated one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{[\ce{Co^2+}]_\text{dilute}}{[\ce{Co^2+}]_\text{concentrated}} \\[0.6em]
&= \frac{0.0010}{1.0} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}
\end{aligned}
$$

$Q$ is less than $K$, so the reaction runs forward until the two concentrations are equal.

Here is what that means at each strip. No cobalt ion crosses from one beaker to the other. In the dilute half-cell, cobalt atoms of the strip become ions:

$$
\ce{Co(s) -> Co^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
$$

That is oxidation, so this strip is the anode. It loses mass, and its solution becomes more concentrated. The electrons go through the wire to the other strip, where

$$
\ce{Co^2+(aq) + 2e^- -> Co(s)}
$$

That is reduction, so that strip is the cathode. It gains mass, and its solution becomes more dilute. In the salt bridge of potassium nitrate, NO₃⁻ moves toward the anode and K⁺ toward the cathode: anions toward the anode, as in every cell (topic 9.8).

The cell stops when the two concentrations are equal: $Q = 1 = K$ and $E = 0$. The potential is small throughout, 0.089 V at the start for these two solutions, because a thousandfold difference in concentration is only three powers of ten in $Q$.

<KeyIdea>

In a concentration cell the electrons flow in the direction that brings the two concentrations together. The half-cell with the lower concentration is the anode.

</KeyIdea>

## The same story as an equation

Everything above fits in one line, which the equation sheet prints:

$$
E_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cell} - \dfrac{RT}{nF}\ln Q
$$

$R$ is 8.314 J/(mol·K), $T$ is in kelvins, $n$ is the moles of electrons in the balanced equation, and $F$ is 96,485 C per mole of electrons (topic 9.9). It is called the **Nernst equation**. On the exam it is mostly a tool for reasoning: which way $E$ moves, and why.

Read it without a calculator. There are three cases:

- When $Q = 1$, $\ln Q = 0$ and $E = E^\circ$.
- When $Q$ is greater than 1, $\ln Q$ is positive, something is subtracted, and $E$ is below $E^\circ$.
- When $Q$ is less than 1, $\ln Q$ is negative, subtracting a negative adds, and $E$ is above $E^\circ$.

These are the three results of the worked example above.

The equation also says why the changes are small. The logarithm grows slowly, and it is multiplied by a small number: at 298 K, $RT/F$ is 0.0257 V, and it is divided by $n$.

And it says what happens at equilibrium. Put in $E = 0$ and $Q = K$, and the equation becomes

$$
E^\circ = \dfrac{RT}{nF}\ln K
$$

which is topic 9.9's link between $E^\circ$ and $K$, arrived at from the other side.

One calculation with the equation follows. It is here so that "the change is small" has a number behind it.

<Worked title="The opening cell, in numbers">

The tin and silver cell has [Sn²⁺] = 1.0 M and [Ag⁺] = 0.010 M at 25 °C. $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ = 0.94 V and $n$ = 2. What is the cell potential?

1. **Find $Q$.**

   $$
   Q = \frac{1.0}{(0.010)^2} = 1.0 \times 10^{4}
   $$

2. **Find $RT/nF$.** The numbers go in without their units, and the answer is in volts, because joules per coulomb are volts.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{RT}{nF} &= \frac{(8.314)(298)}{(2)(96{,}485)} \\[0.6em]
   &= 0.01284
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Multiply by $\ln Q$.**

   $$
   \ln(1.0 \times 10^{4}) = 9.210
   $$

   $$
   (0.01284)(9.210) = 0.118
   $$

   That is 0.118 V, which is 0.12 V to the two decimal places $E^\circ$ has.

4. **Subtract.**

   $$
   E = 0.94 - 0.12 = 0.82\ \text{V}
   $$

5. **Check the direction and the size.** $Q$ is greater than 1, so $E$ should be below $E^\circ$, and it is. A change of ten thousand in $Q$ moved the potential by about 0.12 V. The explorer showed this state as 0.822 V: it keeps a third decimal place so that small changes can be seen.

</Worked>

<Trap>

Seven mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Saying that $E^\circ$ falls as a cell runs.** It is $E$ that falls. $E^\circ$ belongs to the reaction at $Q = 1$ and stays put.
- **Explaining a change with Le Châtelier's principle.** That principle is for a system at equilibrium, and a working cell is not at equilibrium. Say where the species sits in $Q$, what happens to $Q$, and so what happens to $E$.
- **Putting a working cell's concentrations into the expression for $K$.** They give $Q$. They fit $K$ only when the meter reads zero.
- **Putting an electrode into $Q$.** The metals are solids, and a larger strip changes nothing.
- **Forgetting the exponents in $Q$.** Diluting Ag⁺ tenfold changes the tin and silver cell's $Q$ a hundredfold.
- **Making the concentrated half-cell the anode of a concentration cell.** The dilute side is where ions must be made, so oxidation happens there.
- **Predicting when a cell will stop from how much reactant is left.** The stopping point is $Q = K$. For the tin and silver cell that is long after 99 percent of the silver ions have reacted.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- $E^\circ$ is the cell potential at standard conditions, where $Q = 1$. $E$ is the cell potential at whatever $Q$ the cell has now. $Q$ is written as in topic 7.3, with the solid electrodes left out.
- A cell's potential is large while its mixture has far to go to equilibrium and small when little is left to go. A change that raises $Q$ toward $K$ lowers $E$. A change that lowers $Q$ raises $E$, above $E^\circ$ when $Q$ is below 1.
- As a cell runs, $Q$ rises and $E$ falls. At $Q = K$ the cell is at equilibrium and $E = 0$. $E^\circ$ has not changed.
- A working cell is not at equilibrium, so reason through $Q$, not through Le Châtelier's principle: where the species sits in $Q$, what happens to $Q$, and so what happens to $E$.
- In a concentration cell $E^\circ = 0$ and $K = 1$. The half-cell with the lower concentration is the anode, and the cell stops when the two concentrations are equal.
- The Nernst equation says all of this in one line: the cell potential is $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ minus $RT/nF$ times $\ln Q$. The changes it gives are small beside $E^\circ$.

</KeyPoints>
