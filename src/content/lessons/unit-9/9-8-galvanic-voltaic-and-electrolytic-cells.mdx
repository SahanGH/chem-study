Topic 4.7 left a promise. It was about the silver oxide battery in a watch, and it said that Unit 9 would explain how that battery's reaction runs the watch. This lesson is about the arrangement that does it: why keeping two reactants apart turns a reaction into an electric current, what each part of such a cell is for, and how the same parts, run backward by a power supply, make reactions go that would never go by themselves.

## From a collision to a current

Start with the two reactants together. Lower a strip of cobalt metal into a colorless solution of silver nitrate. Gray crystals of silver grow on the cobalt, and the solution slowly turns pink, the color of Co²⁺ ions in water.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Co(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Co^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The nitrate ions are spectators.

This is a redox reaction (topic 4.7): each cobalt atom loses 2 electrons, and each silver ion gains 1. It is thermodynamically favored (topic 9.3).

Follow one silver ion. It wanders through the water until it strikes the surface of the strip, and in that collision an electron hops from the metal onto the ion. The ion is now a silver atom, and it stays where it landed. After two such collisions a cobalt atom at the surface has given up 2 electrons, and it slips into the water as a Co²⁺ ion.

The hop itself is about the width of an atom, and the electron never leaves the strip and the silver growing on it, so there is no place to put anything in its path. The energy the reaction releases ends up in faster-moving particles: the solution gets a little warmer, and that is all.

Now make that collision impossible. Stand the cobalt strip in a beaker that holds no silver ions, put the silver ions in another beaker with a strip of silver for them to land on, and join the two strips with a wire.

A silver ion that strikes the silver strip can still take an electron from the metal, and a cobalt atom can still leave its strip as an ion. But every electron taken from the silver strip has to be made good by one released at the cobalt strip, and the wire is the only link between the two. Electrons moving along a wire are an electric current (topic 2.4), and whatever is wired into their path is run by them: a lamp, a motor, a watch.

(Two beakers and a wire are not quite the whole cell. The next section adds the one part still missing.)

An **electrochemical cell** is a device in which a redox reaction and an electric current are tied together like this. A **galvanic cell** is an electrochemical cell in which a thermodynamically favored reaction pushes electrons through a wire. (It is also called a voltaic cell: the two names mean the same thing.) A battery is one or more galvanic cells in a package.

<KeyIdea>

In a galvanic cell the two half-reactions of a favored redox reaction take place in separate places, so the electrons one of them releases can reach the other only along a wire. The reaction is the same as in the beaker. What changes is the path of the electrons, and with it what the energy can do.

</KeyIdea>

## The parts of a galvanic cell

  caption="A galvanic cell of cobalt and silver. Electrons go through the wire from the anode to the cathode, and the ions of the salt bridge keep both solutions neutral."

Take the parts one at a time.

A **half-cell** is one of the two places: here, a strip of cobalt standing in a solution of cobalt(II) nitrate, and a strip of silver standing in a solution of silver nitrate. One half-reaction happens in each.

An **electrode** is the solid conductor in a half-cell, where electrons pass between the wire and the chemicals. Here the electrodes are the two metal strips.

The **wire** joins the two electrodes and carries the electrons. Somewhere along it sits either a meter, which measures the push on the electrons in volts (topic 9.9), or the device the cell is running.

The **salt bridge** joins the two solutions. It is a tube filled with a solution of a salt whose ions react with nothing in the cell, here potassium nitrate, and plugged so that the liquids do not pour through. Ions can move through it, and that is its whole job. More on that below.

The two electrodes get their names from what happens at them. **The anode is the electrode where oxidation happens. The cathode is the electrode where reduction happens.** Those definitions hold in every electrochemical cell, whatever its kind. Nothing else about an electrode decides its name: not its metal, not its side of the drawing.

Electrons are released at the anode, by the oxidation, and used up at the cathode, by the reduction. So in the wire, **electrons travel from the anode to the cathode.**

**What the salt bridge is for.** Set up the two half-cells and the wire but leave the bridge out, and the meter reads nothing. Count the ions to see why.

The cobalt solution began with exactly two nitrate ions for every Co²⁺ ion. The first few cobalt atoms to be oxidized add Co²⁺ ions that have no nitrate ions to match them, so that beaker is left with a little extra positive charge. In the other beaker the first few Ag⁺ ions to be reduced leave their nitrate ions unmatched: a little extra negative charge.

Extra positive charge around the cobalt strip holds back the electrons that should leave it, and extra negative charge around the silver strip pushes back the ones that should arrive. A very small imbalance is enough to hold both half-reactions still.

Now lower the bridge into the two solutions, and the meter comes to life. For every 2 electrons that go through the wire, in the simplest picture 2 nitrate ions move out of the bridge into the cobalt half-cell and 2 potassium ions move out of it into the silver half-cell. Neither solution ever builds up a charge, so the cell keeps running. In general: **anions move toward the anode, and cations move toward the cathode.**

Two kinds of charge carrier are at work (topics 2.3 and 2.4): electrons in the metal parts, ions in the solutions and the bridge. Electrons do not travel through the solutions, and ions do not travel through the wire. Together they make one complete loop.

<KeyIdea>

In every electrochemical cell, oxidation happens at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrons go from the anode to the cathode through the wire. In the solutions, anions move toward the anode and cations toward the cathode.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Reading a cell from what you see">

A student builds the cell of the figure: a cobalt strip in pink cobalt(II) nitrate solution, a silver strip in colorless silver nitrate solution, and a salt bridge of potassium nitrate. She reads the meter, then takes it out and joins the two strips directly with the wire. After the cell has run for a day, the cobalt strip weighs 0.295 g less than it did and the silver strip weighs 1.080 g more.

Write the half-reaction at each strip, name the anode and the cathode, give the direction of the electrons and of the ions in the salt bridge, and write the overall equation. Then show that the two changes in mass agree with it.

1. **Turn each observation into a half-reaction.** The cobalt strip got lighter: cobalt atoms left it for the solution, as ions.

   $$
   \ce{Co(s) -> Co^2+(aq) + 2e^-}
   $$

   The silver strip got heavier: silver ions came out of the solution onto it, as atoms.

   $$
   \ce{Ag^+(aq) + e^- -> Ag(s)}
   $$

2. **Name the electrodes by the process.** Cobalt loses electrons, which is oxidation, so the cobalt strip is the anode. Silver ions gain electrons, which is reduction, so the silver strip is the cathode.

3. **Send the electrons.** From the anode to the cathode through the wire: from the cobalt strip to the silver strip.

4. **Send the ions.** The cobalt solution is gaining Co²⁺ ions, so nitrate ions move into it from the bridge. The silver solution is losing Ag⁺ ions, so potassium ions move into it from the bridge. Anions toward the anode, cations toward the cathode.

5. **Write the overall equation.** Each cobalt atom gives up 2 electrons and each silver ion takes 1, so the silver half-reaction is doubled (topic 4.9) and the electrons cancel.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Co(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Co^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   It is the reaction of the beaker, unchanged.

6. **Check it against the masses.** Moles of cobalt lost, from grams and grams per mole:

   $$
   \frac{0.295}{58.93} = 0.00501
   $$

   Moles of silver gained:

   $$
   \frac{1.080}{107.87} = 0.01001
   $$

   Twice as many moles of silver as of cobalt, as the equation says. The two strips do not change by equal masses: they change by amounts set by the electrons, 2 for each cobalt atom and 1 for each silver atom.

</Worked>

## At the two surfaces

Both half-reactions of a cell happen at the surfaces of its electrodes, where metal meets solution.

<Figure
  src="/figures/9-8-two-surfaces.svg"
  alt="Two panels showing the surfaces of the two electrodes as particles. In the left panel, labeled cobalt strip, anode, a block of 14 cobalt atoms has one empty place at its surface, and an arrow leads from that place to 1 cobalt 2 plus ion in the solution; 2 electrons are left in the metal, labeled 2 electrons to the wire. In the right panel, labeled silver strip, cathode, 2 silver ions in the solution each have an arrow pointing to the surface of a block of 15 silver atoms; 2 electrons are in the metal, labeled 2 electrons from the wire."
  caption="At the anode a cobalt atom goes into the solution as an ion and leaves 2 electrons in the strip. At the cathode those 2 electrons turn 2 silver ions into silver atoms. Water molecules and nitrate ions are not shown."
/>

At the anode's surface, a cobalt atom leaves the metal and goes into the solution as a Co²⁺ ion. Its 2 electrons stay behind in the strip, and electrons already in the metal are pushed along the wire. The strip has one atom fewer: this is why an anode made of the metal being oxidized loses mass.

At the cathode's surface, a silver ion arrives from the solution, takes 1 electron from the strip, and stays on the surface as a silver atom, part of the metal now. The strip has one atom more: this is why a cathode gains mass when a metal is formed on it. For the 2 electrons one cobalt atom leaves behind, 2 silver ions are reduced.

Electrons never enter the solution. They cross from the metal to an ion, or from an atom to the metal, only at the surface.

**Not every electrode takes part.** An electrode can be a conductor that only hands electrons over and is not itself changed, such as a rod of graphite or a strip of platinum. Then the species oxidized or reduced at it comes from the solution, and the product may be a gas that bubbles away or a dissolved ion. The names do not change: the electrode where oxidation happens is the anode.

**What you can see, and what it tells you.**

| What you see | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| A metal electrode gets lighter | Its atoms are being oxidized to ions: it is the anode |
| An electrode gets heavier as a coating of metal grows on it | Metal ions are being reduced to atoms there: it is the cathode |
| Bubbles of gas at an electrode | A gas is the product of the half-reaction there: write the half-reaction to see whether electrons are lost or gained |
| A solution's color deepens or fades | A colored ion is being made or used up in that half-cell |

Take the third row. Hydrogen gas forms where H⁺ ions gain electrons:

$$
\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2e^- -> H2(g)}
$$

That is a reduction, so those bubbles mark a cathode. A gas formed by a loss of electrons marks an anode, and the next section has one.

Now the promise of topic 4.7 can be kept. In the watch battery, the zinc is the anode and the silver oxide is the cathode. They are pressed into one small steel can with a layer between them soaked in potassium hydroxide solution, whose ions do the job of the salt bridge. The watch is the device on the wire.

## The electrolytic cell

A galvanic cell lets a favored reaction push electrons through a wire. Turn that around: push electrons through a cell from outside, and a reaction can be made to go that is thermodynamically unfavored.

An **electrolytic cell** is an electrochemical cell in which a power supply drives an unfavored redox reaction. Doing so is called **electrolysis**. This is one of the two ways of topic 9.7 to make an unfavored change happen: energy from outside.

The supply does one thing: it pulls electrons out of one electrode and pushes them into the other. At the electrode it pushes electrons into, something in the cell has to take them: a reduction happens there, so that electrode is the cathode. At the electrode it pulls electrons out of, something has to give them up: an oxidation happens there, so that electrode is the anode. The definitions have not changed, and in the wire the electrons still go from the anode to the cathode, now through the supply.

Often an electrolytic cell is one container with no salt bridge. If the two products do not have to be kept in separate solutions, the liquid itself carries the ions between the electrodes.

| | Galvanic cell | Electrolytic cell |
|---|---|---|
| The overall reaction is | thermodynamically favored | thermodynamically unfavored |
| Electrons move because | the reaction pushes them | a power supply pushes them |
| Oxidation happens at | the anode | the anode |
| Electrons in the wire go | from anode to cathode | from anode to cathode |

The cells differ in what makes the electrons move. They do not differ in where oxidation happens or in which way the electrons go through the wire.

<Worked title="Magnesium from a molten salt">

One industrial way of making magnesium metal is the electrolysis of molten magnesium chloride. The salt melts at 714 °C, and the cell is run at about 750 °C. A steel electrode and a graphite electrode dip into the melt and are joined to a power supply. Silvery liquid magnesium collects at the steel electrode, and a yellow-green gas, chlorine, bubbles off the graphite.

Write the half-reaction at each electrode, name the anode and the cathode, say which way the ions and the electrons move, and write the overall reaction.

1. **Say what is in the cell.** A molten ionic compound is ions that are free to move (topic 2.3): Mg²⁺ ions and Cl⁻ ions, and nothing else. There is no water.

2. **Write the half-reaction that makes each product.** Magnesium metal comes from magnesium ions.

   $$
   \ce{Mg^2+ + 2e^- -> Mg(l)}
   $$

   Chlorine gas comes from chloride ions.

   $$
   \ce{2Cl^- -> Cl2(g) + 2e^-}
   $$

3. **Name the electrodes by the process.** Mg²⁺ gains electrons: reduction, so the steel electrode is the cathode. Cl⁻ loses electrons: oxidation, so the graphite electrode is the anode. Neither electrode is itself used up: each only hands electrons over.

4. **Send the ions and the electrons.** Cations, Mg²⁺, move through the melt toward the cathode, and anions, Cl⁻, toward the anode: the same rule as in a salt bridge. The supply pulls electrons from the graphite anode and pushes them into the steel cathode.

5. **Add the halves.** Each has 2 electrons, so they cancel as they stand.

   $$
   \ce{MgCl2(l) -> Mg(l) + Cl2(g)}
   $$

6. **Say why it needs the supply.** Magnesium burns in chlorine to give magnesium chloride: that direction is the thermodynamically favored one. The cell runs the reverse, which is unfavored, and it goes only while the supply keeps pushing electrons. For the same reason the cell is built to keep the two products apart: where liquid magnesium and chlorine meet, they react and undo the work.

</Worked>

**Beyond the exam:** chemists have a shorthand for a galvanic cell, called cell notation. The cobalt and silver cell is written Co(s) | Co²⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s): the anode's half-cell on the left, the cathode's on the right, a single line wherever a solid meets a solution, and a double line for the salt bridge. The exam describes a cell in words or draws it.

**Beyond the exam:** the two electrodes of a cell are also marked positive and negative, as on any battery. Which is which is not the same in a galvanic cell as in an electrolytic cell, so the signs are a poor way to tell electrodes apart, and the exam does not ask for them. Name an electrode by what happens at it.

<Trap>

Six mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Naming an electrode by its metal or by its place in the drawing.** The anode is wherever oxidation happens: cobalt was the anode in one cell of this lesson and the cathode in another.
- **Sending electrons through the salt bridge or the solution.** Electrons take the wire; ions carry the charge through the liquids.
- **Sending the bridge's ions the wrong way.** Anions move toward the anode, where positive ions are building up; cations move toward the cathode.
- **Thinking an electrolytic cell reverses the definitions.** Oxidation is at the anode in both kinds of cell; what differs is whether the reaction pushes the electrons or a supply does.
- **Expecting the two electrodes to change by equal masses.** The electrons are equal; the moles and the masses follow the half-reactions.
- **Taking bubbles of gas as the mark of one kind of electrode.** Write the half-reaction: hydrogen forms by reduction, at a cathode, and chlorine by oxidation, at an anode.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A galvanic (voltaic) cell splits a thermodynamically favored redox reaction between two half-cells, so the electrons released at the anode reach the cathode only through a wire, where their energy can do work.
- Each half-cell has an electrode, and in every electrochemical cell oxidation happens at the anode and reduction at the cathode.
- Electrons travel through the wire from the anode to the cathode. In the salt bridge and the solutions, anions move toward the anode and cations toward the cathode, which keeps each solution neutral.
- An anode made of the metal being oxidized loses mass, and a cathode on which a metal forms gains mass. A gas or a change of color at an electrode also shows which half-reaction is happening there.
- An electrolytic cell uses a power supply to push electrons through a cell and drive a thermodynamically unfavored reaction, as in making magnesium from molten magnesium chloride.
- In an electrolytic cell the names and the direction of the electrons follow the same rules, and only the source of the push is different.

</KeyPoints>
