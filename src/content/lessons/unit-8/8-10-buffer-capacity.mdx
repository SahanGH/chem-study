Bacteria growing in a flask of sugary broth give off acids as they feed. Microbiologists put a buffer in the broth to hold its pH, and for a while it does. Then, if the buffer was made too weak, the pH falls quickly and the culture stops growing. The buffer did not stop being a buffer by accident: it ran out. How much acid or base can a buffer take, and what decides that?

## One pH, two sizes of buffer

A buffer has two members. Its acid form reacts with added OH⁻, and its base form reacts with added H₃O⁺ (topic 8.8). Its pH follows the ratio of the two forms (topic 8.9):

$$
\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\dfrac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

Both forms are in the same solution, so the ratio of their concentrations is the ratio of their moles (topic 8.9).

Look at what that equation holds. The amounts of the two forms are in it only as a ratio. A liter holding 0.20 mol of each form and a liter holding 0.020 mol of each have the same ratio, 1, and the same pH, which is the p$K_a$. Put a pH meter in each and the two readings match.

An addition can. Each mole of OH⁻ that goes in turns one mole of acid form into base form (topic 8.8). Take 0.015 mol of OH⁻. From 0.20 mol of acid form it removes 7.5 percent, less than a tenth, and the ratio barely moves. From 0.020 mol it removes 75 percent, three-quarters, and the ratio moves a long way.

That difference has a name. The **buffer capacity** of a buffer is the amount of strong acid or strong base it can take before its pH changes sharply. More moles of both forms means more capacity.

Add water to a buffer and its pH hardly changes, because both forms are diluted alike (topic 8.8), and the moles of each form in the flask are unchanged. What falls is the amount in each liter you pour from it.

<KeyIdea>

A buffer's pH follows the ratio of base form to acid form. How much it can take is a matter of how many moles of the two forms it holds. More of both at the same ratio means the same pH, and more acid or base taken up before the pH changes much.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Thirty-five millimoles of base, into a buffer and into water">

A liter of buffer holds 140 mmol of propanoic acid, HC₃H₅O₂, and 60 mmol of sodium propanoate, NaC₃H₅O₂. The p$K_a$ of propanoic acid is 4.87. A chemist dissolves 35 mmol of solid sodium hydroxide (1.40 g) in it, so the volume stays 1.00 L. Find the pH before and after, and compare with what the same 35 mmol does to 1.00 L of pure water at 25 °C. Write HA for propanoic acid and A⁻ for the propanoate ion.

1. **Before.** The ratio of base form to acid form is 60 ÷ 140 = 0.429.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= 4.87 + \log(0.429) \\
   &= 4.87 - 0.37 \\
   &= 4.50
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The buffer holds more acid form than base form, and its pH is below the p$K_a$ (topic 8.9).

2. **React first, in moles.** Sodium hydroxide is a strong base, so its OH⁻ reacts to completion with the acid form (topic 8.8):

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{HC3H5O2(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{C3H5O2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Here is the table of moles. Every value is in mmol. It is not an ICE table: nothing in it is at equilibrium yet (topic 8.4).

   | | HA | OH⁻ | A⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Before | 140 | 35 | 60 |
   | Change | −35 | −35 | +35 |
   | After | 105 | 0 | 95 |

3. **Then the new ratio gives the pH.** The ratio is now 95 ÷ 105 = 0.905.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= 4.87 + \log(0.905) \\
   &= 4.87 - 0.04 \\
   &= 4.83
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **The same 35 mmol in water.** In 1.00 L of water nothing reacts with the OH⁻, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ is 0.035 M. At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14.00.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pOH} &= -\log(0.035) \\
   &= 1.46
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= 14.00 - 1.46 \\
   &= 12.54
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Compare.** The buffer went from 4.50 to 4.83, a change of 0.33. The water went from 7.00 to 12.54, a change of 5.54. The buffer's ratio went from 0.429 to 0.905, a little more than double. The water's $[\ce{OH-}]$ went from 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M to 0.035 M, up 350,000 times.

</Worked>

**Beyond the exam:** the exam rarely asks you to calculate the pH of a ready-made buffer after an addition, as steps 2 and 3 did. It asks which reaction happens, which way the pH moves, and which of two buffers moves less. The numbers are here because "the ratio barely changes" is easier to believe once you have seen it.

The order of the steps is what matters everywhere in this unit. The strong base reacts completely first, in moles; only then does an equilibrium expression come in (topic 8.4).

## Room for base, room for acid

A buffer does not have to hold equal amounts of its two forms, and when it does not, its capacity is lopsided. The reason is in the two reactions (topic 8.8). Added OH⁻ is met by the acid form and by nothing else. Added H₃O⁺ is met by the base form and by nothing else.

Both buffers are propanoic acid with propanoate ion, and both hold 200 mmol in all. The first holds 150 mmol of acid form and 50 mmol of base form. It could use up 150 mmol of OH⁻ before its acid form was gone, and only 50 mmol of H₃O⁺ before its base form was gone. The second, with 50 mmol of acid form and 150 mmol of base form, is the reverse.

The two do not have the same pH. The first is below the p$K_a$ and the second above it (topic 8.9):

$$
\begin{aligned}
4.87 + \log\frac{50}{150} &= 4.39 \\[4pt]
4.87 + \log\frac{150}{50} &= 5.35
\end{aligned}
$$

A buffer with equal amounts, at pH = p$K_a$, has the same room on both sides. For a given total amount of the two forms, that is also where a small addition moves the pH least. So when a buffer must guard against both acid and base, it is made with a ratio near 1.

<KeyIdea>

The acid form is what meets added base, and the base form is what meets added acid. A buffer with more acid form than base form has more capacity for added base than for added acid. With more base form, it is the other way around.

</KeyIdea>

## When one member runs out

A buffer works only while both members are there. Keep adding strong base and the acid form is used up, mole for mole, until none is left. From then on, added OH⁻ has nothing to react with, and it stays in the solution as OH⁻.

What the solution is depends on how far you have gone, and topic 8.4 sorted the cases by comparing the moles of OH⁻ with the moles of acid form. With less OH⁻ than acid form, both members remain and the ratio sets the pH. With exactly as much, only the base form is left, a weak base in water, and at 25 °C its pH is above 7. With more, the OH⁻ left over sets the pH, and the weak base beside it hardly counts.

The same happens on the other side: add more H₃O⁺ than there is base form, and the H₃O⁺ left over sets the pH.

The pH does not wait for the last mole. As a member gets scarce, the ratio runs away from 1. Once there is ten times as much base form as acid form, the pH is already a full unit above the p$K_a$ (topic 8.7), and each further millimole of base moves it more than the one before. That is why capacity is defined by a sharp change in pH, and not by the last mole.

So the pH of a buffer is never quite fixed. How well it resists a change depends on how much of each member it holds, and when one member is gone the resistance goes with it.

## Choosing a buffer for a wanted pH

On paper, any conjugate pair can be pushed to almost any pH by choosing the ratio. But a ratio far from 1 means one member is scarce, and a scarce member is no capacity on that side. So a buffer is useful only when both forms are present in comparable amounts: a ratio between about 1 to 10 and 10 to 1, which is a pH within about one unit of the p$K_a$ (topic 8.7).

That gives a method in two steps:

1. Choose the pair whose p$K_a$ is nearest the wanted pH.
2. Find the ratio from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation (topic 8.9), then make the amounts as large as the job needs.

These are the pairs this lesson chooses from, all at 25 °C.

| Acid form, with its base form | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|
| Formic acid, with formate ion | 3.75 |
| Propanoic acid, with propanoate ion | 4.87 |
| H₂PO₄⁻, with HPO₄²⁻ | 7.21 |
| Conjugate acid of Tris, with Tris | 8.07 |

The p$K_a$ in the last row belongs to the conjugate acid of Tris. A buffer made from a weak base is worked with the p$K_a$ of its conjugate acid (topic 8.9).

<Worked title="A buffer for pH 8.60">

A biochemist needs a buffer at pH 8.60. Choose a pair from the table and find the ratio of its two forms.

1. **Find the nearest p$K_a$.** The wanted pH is 4.85 above formic acid's p$K_a$, 3.73 above propanoic acid's, 1.39 above that of H₂PO₄⁻ and 0.53 above that of the conjugate acid of Tris. Tris is the only pair within one unit.

2. **Find the ratio.** Write B for Tris and HB⁺ for its conjugate acid.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \log\frac{[\ce{B}]}{[\ce{HB+}]} &= 8.60 - 8.07 \\
   &= 0.53
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{[\ce{B}]}{[\ce{HB+}]} &= 10^{0.53} \\
   &= 3.4
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Turn the ratio into amounts.** For every 0.050 mol of the conjugate acid, the buffer needs 3.4 × 0.050 = 0.17 mol of Tris.

4. **Check the answer against what you know.** The wanted pH is above the p$K_a$, so there should be more base form than acid form, and there is.

5. **Why not the phosphate pair?** It would need a ratio of $10^{1.39}$, about 25 to 1. Only 1 part in 26 of the phosphate would be in the acid form, about 4 percent, so the buffer would have almost nothing to meet added base with.

</Worked>

With 3.4 times as much base form as acid form, this buffer has more room for added acid than for added base. If the experiment is going to make base, use larger amounts of both.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Reading capacity from the pH.** Two buffers with the same pH can differ tenfold in capacity. Count the moles of each form.
- **Saying that extra acid form helps against added acid.** The acid form meets added base; the base form meets added acid.
- **Letting the added hydroxide join an equilibrium table.** It reacts with the acid form until one of them runs out, and that is counted in moles. Only what is left goes into an equation with a $K$ or a logarithm.
- **Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation after a member has run out.** With no acid form left there is no ratio; what is left over sets the pH.
- **Believing a buffer holds its pH constant.** The pH moves a little with every addition, and it moves a lot once one member is nearly gone.
- **Choosing a pair by its name and not by its p$K_a$.** The p$K_a$ must be within about one unit of the wanted pH, or one member will be scarce.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A buffer's pH follows the ratio of base form to acid form. Its buffer capacity, the amount of strong acid or strong base it can take before its pH changes sharply, follows the amounts of the two forms.
- Two buffers with the same ratio have the same pH, and the one holding more moles of both forms has the larger capacity.
- Added base is met by the acid form and added acid by the base form, so a buffer with more acid form has more capacity for base, and one with more base form has more capacity for acid.
- An added strong acid or base reacts completely first, in moles. When it is more than the member it reacts with, that member runs out and the strong acid or base left over sets the pH.
- A buffer is useful within about one pH unit of its p$K_a$, so choose the pair whose p$K_a$ is nearest the wanted pH and then set the ratio.
- For a buffer made from a weak base, the p$K_a$ is that of its conjugate acid.

</KeyPoints>
