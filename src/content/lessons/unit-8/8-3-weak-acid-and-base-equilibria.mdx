Glycolic acid is the acid in many skin-care peels. Make a 0.30 M solution of it, put a pH meter in, and the meter reads close to 2.2. If every molecule of the acid had given its proton to water, the hydronium concentration would be 0.30 M and the meter would read 0.52. A reading near 2.2 says that only about 2 molecules in every 100 have done so. Topic 4.8 said that a weak acid gives up "only some" of its protons, and promised numbers. Here they are.

## A weak acid's equilibrium has its own constant

A weak acid in water is a reaction that runs both ways and settles with most of the acid still whole (topic 4.8). Write a weak acid as HA and its conjugate base as A⁻:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

It is an equilibrium, so it has a $K$ (topic 7.3). Water is a pure liquid and is left out of it:

$$
K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

This is the **acid ionization constant**, $K_a$: the equilibrium constant for a weak acid giving its proton to water. The expression is on the equation sheet. The values are not: a question gives the $K_a$ it needs.

Here are the acids this lesson uses. The values are for 25 °C.

| Acid | Formula | $K_a$ |
|---|---|---|
| Iodic acid | HIO₃ | 0.17 |
| Chloroacetic acid | HC₂H₂ClO₂ | 1.4 × 10⁻³ |
| Formic acid | HCHO₂ | 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ |
| Glycolic acid | HC₂H₃O₃ | 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ |
| Butanoic acid | HC₄H₇O₂ | 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ |

Read it this way: the larger the $K_a$, the further the ionization goes and the stronger the acid. Most of the values are very small, so the equilibrium lies far toward the un-ionized acid (topic 7.5). Iodic acid, at the top, is the exception, and a question below shows how far it goes. A strong acid has no place in such a table: its ionization is essentially complete, and there is no equilibrium to describe.

Because $K_a$ values spread over so many powers of ten, they are often given as logarithms, as $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is:

$$
\text{p}K_a = -\log K_a
$$

For butanoic acid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{p}K_a &= -\log(1.5 \times 10^{-5}) \\
&= 4.82
\end{aligned}
$$

p$K_a$ runs on the same backward scale as pH. **A stronger acid has a bigger $K_a$ and a smaller p$K_a$.** Each step of one in p$K_a$ is a factor of ten in $K_a$. If a question hands you p$K_a$, undo the logarithm just as you did for a pH: $K_a = 10^{-\text{p}K_a}$.

## From Ka to pH

You have solved this problem before. In topic 7.7 you started with the reactant alone, let some of it react, and put the last row of the table into the $K$ expression. A weak acid in water is that problem. The constant has a new name, $K_a$, and the unknown $x$ turns out to be the $[\ce{H3O+}]$ you want.

One thing about the table's starting row: the starting $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is written as 0. Water's own 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M (topic 8.1) is there, but it is too small to matter beside what the acid makes.

<Worked title="Glycolic acid">

Find the pH of 0.30 M glycolic acid, HC₂H₃O₃, at 25 °C, and the concentration of every species in the solution. $K_a$ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴.

1. **Write the equilibrium.** HA stands for glycolic acid and A⁻ for its conjugate base, the glycolate ion, C₂H₃O₃⁻.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
   &\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Table, as in topic 7.7.** Every value is in M. Water gets no column.

   | | HA | H₃O⁺ | A⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | I | 0.30 | 0 | 0 |
   | C | −x | +x | +x |
   | E | 0.30 − x | x | x |

3. **$K_a$ in terms of $x$.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   K_a &= \frac{x^2}{0.30 - x} \\
   &= 1.5 \times 10^{-4}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Drop the $x$ beside 0.30,** as topic 7.7 does for a small $K$. $0.30 - x$ is very nearly 0.30.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   x^2 &= (1.5 \times 10^{-4})(0.30) \\
   &= 4.5 \times 10^{-5} \\
   x &= 6.7 \times 10^{-3}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Check that dropping $x$ was fair.**

   $$
   \frac{6.7 \times 10^{-3}}{0.30} \times 100\% = 2.2\%
   $$

   Under 5 percent, so it holds.

6. **Take the pH.** $x$ is $[\ce{H3O+}]$.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= -\log(6.7 \times 10^{-3}) \\
   &= 2.17
   \end{aligned}
   $$

7. **List every species.** The hydronium ion and the glycolate ion are each at $x$, 6.7 × 10⁻³ M. For the acid still whole:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{HA}] &= 0.30 - 0.0067 \\
   &= 0.29\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   And from $K_w$, which is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{OH-}] &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{6.7 \times 10^{-3}} \\
   &= 1.5 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

8. **Check.**

   $$
   \frac{(6.7 \times 10^{-3})^2}{0.29} = 1.5 \times 10^{-4}
   $$

   The answers give $K_a$ back.

</Worked>

Solving the equation without the shortcut gives $x$ = 6.6 × 10⁻³ M and a pH of 2.18. The shortcut's 2.17 is off by less than 0.01 of a pH unit, which is what passing the check buys.

## How much of the acid ionizes

The **percent ionization** of a weak acid is the share of its molecules that have given up their proton: $[\ce{H3O+}]$ at equilibrium divided by the starting concentration of the acid, times 100 percent.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{percent ionization} \\
&= \frac{[\ce{H3O+}]_\text{eq}}{[\ce{HA}]_\text{start}} \times 100\%
\end{aligned}
$$

It is not on the equation sheet.

For the 0.30 M glycolic acid you have already calculated it, 2.2 percent, in step 5: for a weak acid alone in water, the check of the approximation and the percent ionization are the same division, as long as the check passes. About 2 molecules in 100 are ionized, which is the opening's number.

**What does adding water do?** Reason with $Q$ (topic 7.10). Double the volume of a weak acid solution and, for an instant, every concentration is halved. $Q$ has two concentrations on top and one underneath, so $Q$ falls to half of $K_a$. With $Q$ below $K$ the reaction runs forward: more of the acid ionizes. Dilution raises the percent ionization. $K_a$ itself has not changed: diluting leaves a $K$ exactly as it was (topic 7.9).

- Its percent ionization goes down and its pH goes up

<div slot="why">

Just after the water goes in, $Q$ is below $K_a$, so more of the acid ionizes and the percent ionization rises: from 2.2 percent to 6.8 percent. But those ions are now spread through ten times the volume, and the extra ionization does not make up for that. $[\ce{H3O+}]$ falls, from 6.6 × 10⁻³ M to 2.0 × 10⁻³ M, so the pH rises, from 2.18 to 2.69. (These four numbers are from the full equation, because at 0.030 M the shortcut does not pass its check.)

Two measures are moving, and they answer different questions. The share of the acid that is ionized went up. The number of ions in each liter went down, because the acid itself is now so spread out. $K_a$ did not move.

<KeyIdea>

Diluting a weak acid raises its percent ionization and lowers its $[\ce{H3O+}]$, so the pH rises. $K_a$ does not change. At one concentration, the acid with the larger $K_a$ is more ionized and has the lower pH, and a weak acid's pH is always above that of a strong acid at the same concentration.

</KeyIdea>

**When is the shortcut allowed?** What step 4 showed is what topic 7.7 said: what decides is how large the starting concentration is beside $K$. A concentrated solution of an acid with a small $K_a$ passes easily; a dilute solution, or an acid with a large $K_a$, may not. No cutoff is worth memorizing. Do the division every time and put the percentage in your answer, as topic 7.7 asked. If the check fails, the equation has to be solved with $x$ left in. With $C$ for the starting concentration, it is

$$
x^2 + K_a x - K_a C = 0
$$

**Beyond the exam:** that takes the quadratic formula, which topic 7.7 uses once. Exam questions are nearly always written so that $x$ is small, and a question that asks you to justify the shortcut wants to see the percentage.

## Ka from a measured pH

The same table runs the other way. A pH meter gives the pH of a weak acid solution of known concentration, and the pH gives $x$ (topic 8.1):

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}
$$

The table's equilibrium row then holds three known numbers: $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is $x$, $[\ce{A-}]$ is $x$, and $[\ce{HA}]$ is the starting concentration minus $x$. Put them into the $K_a$ expression. Nothing has to be left out and nothing has to be checked, because $x$ was measured, not solved for.

The pH gives $x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 10^{-5.70} \\
&= 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &= \frac{x^2}{0.040} \\
&= \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{-6})^2}{0.040} \\
&= 1.0 \times 10^{-10}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Weak bases: x is the hydroxide

A weak base takes a proton from water, and only a small fraction of its molecules do (topic 4.8). Write a weak base as B and its conjugate acid as HB⁺:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{B(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{HB+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Its constant is the **base ionization constant**, $K_b$: the equilibrium constant for a weak base taking a proton from water.

$$
K_b = \frac{[\ce{HB+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{B}]}
$$

It is on the equation sheet, and so is

$$
\text{p}K_b = -\log K_b
$$

Many weak bases are compounds of nitrogen, and take the proton onto the nitrogen atom (topic 8.6 returns to them).

The table is the same table. **The one difference decides the answer: what the base makes is OH⁻, so $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$.** Its negative logarithm is the pOH. The pH takes one more step, which at 25 °C is

$$
\text{pH} = 14.00 - \text{pOH}
$$

A solution of a base must end up with a pH above 7 at 25 °C: if yours does not, you stopped at the pOH.

## An acid and its conjugate base

The conjugate base of a weak acid is itself a weak base: A⁻ can take a proton back from water. So one conjugate pair has two equilibria, the acid's with $K_a$ and the base's with $K_b$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{A-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{HA(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Add the two equations. HA is on both sides and cancels, and so does A⁻. What is left is water's own equilibrium:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

When equations are added, their constants multiply (topic 7.6):

$$
K_a \times K_b = K_w
$$

This is on the equation sheet, and at 25 °C the product is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.

<Figure
  src="/figures/8-3-conjugate-pair-equations.svg"
  alt="Two equations written one under the other and added. The first is HA plus water giving hydronium plus A minus, with the constant K a. The second is A minus plus water giving HA plus hydroxide, with the constant K b. HA is struck through in both equations with a solid stroke and A minus is struck through in both with a dashed stroke.   caption="Add the equation of an acid to the equation of its own conjugate base, and the acid and the base cancel. What is left is water's equilibrium, so the two constants multiply to Kw."

Here are two uses. For the conjugate base of butanoic acid, the butanoate ion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_b &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.5 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= 6.7 \times 10^{-10}
\end{aligned}
$$

For the conjugate acid of ethanolamine:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_a &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{3.2 \times 10^{-5}} \\
&= 3.1 \times 10^{-10}
\end{aligned}
$$

This puts a number on topic 4.8's rule that the stronger acid has the weaker conjugate base: the two constants of a pair multiply to a fixed number, so the larger one is, the smaller the other must be. In logarithms, at 25 °C:

$$
\text{p}K_a + \text{p}K_b = 14.00
$$

**Only for a conjugate pair.** The equations cancel to water's only when the base is the acid's own conjugate. The $K_a$ of butanoic acid and the $K_b$ of ethanolamine are two unrelated numbers; their product, 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁰, means nothing.

What the pH is when a conjugate base is dissolved by itself, as a salt, is topic 8.4's question.

<KeyIdea>

For an acid and its own conjugate base, $K_a \times K_b = K_w$, which is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.

</KeyIdea>

The two species must be an acid and its own conjugate base, which differ by exactly one proton (topic 4.8). Take one proton off phenol, C₆H₅OH, and what is left is C₆H₅O⁻: that is a pair.

Take one proton off formic acid, HCHO₂, and what is left is CHO₂⁻, not the glycolate ion C₂H₃O₃⁻. Take one proton off (CH₃)₃NH⁺ and what is left is trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N, not ethanolamine; and the conjugate acid of trimethylamine is (CH₃)₃NH⁺, not HOC₂H₄NH₃⁺. Options 3 and 4 each pair an acid with a base, and each uses the right kind of species, but the two belong to different pairs.

</div>

<Trap>

Eight mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Reading $[\ce{H3O+}]$ from the bottle.** A weak acid's concentration is not its hydronium concentration. The table and $K_a$ give that.
- **Stopping at the pOH.** For a weak base $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$. $-\log x$ is the pOH. Subtract it from 14.00 at 25 °C to get the pH.
- **Dropping $x$ without a test.** Divide $x$ by the starting concentration, and put the percentage in your answer.
- **Using an $x$ that failed its check.** An $x$ larger than the starting concentration is a sign, not an answer.
- **Thinking that dilution lowers a weak acid's pH because more of it ionizes.** The percent ionization rises, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ falls, and the pH rises.
- **Thinking that a larger percent ionization means a larger $K_a$.** Dilution changes the percent ionization; only temperature changes $K_a$.
- **Multiplying a $K_a$ and a $K_b$ that do not belong to one conjugate pair.** The product is $K_w$ only for an acid and its own conjugate base.
- **Forgetting the square root.** $K_a$ times the concentration is $x^2$, not $x$.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A weak acid's ionization is an equilibrium, in which HA and water give $\ce{H3O+}$ and $\ce{A-}$, with $K_a = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]/[\ce{HA}]$. The stronger the acid, the larger its $K_a$ and the smaller its p$K_a$.
- The pH of a weak acid comes from the table of topic 7.7, where $x$ is $[\ce{H3O+}]$. Leaving $x$ out beside the starting concentration is allowed only if $x$ turns out under 5 percent of it. Show the percentage.
- Percent ionization is $[\ce{H3O+}]$ at equilibrium over the starting concentration, times 100 percent. Dilution raises it while it lowers $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and raises the pH, with $K_a$ unchanged.
- A measured pH gives $x$, and $x$ in the table gives $K_a$.
- For a weak base, in which B and water give $\ce{HB+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ with $K_b$, $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$, so find the pOH first and then the pH.
- For an acid and its own conjugate base, $K_a \times K_b = K_w$.

</KeyPoints>
