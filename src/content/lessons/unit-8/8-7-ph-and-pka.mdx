Potassium sorbate is on the label of many fruit drinks, where it keeps mold and yeast from growing. Stir the same amount into a food that is not acidic and it does very little. Nothing about the sorbate has changed, only the pH around it. This lesson is about what the pH of a solution does to a weak acid dissolved in it.

## Two forms of one acid

A weak acid in solution is always present in two forms. One is the acid itself, HA, which this unit calls the **acid form**. The other is its conjugate base, A⁻, the **base form**. The two forms differ by one proton, and they can differ in much else: in color, in smell, in how they act on a living cell.

So far in this unit the acid set the pH. Now turn it around. Suppose something else fixes the pH of the solution, such as a strong acid, a buffer, or a food full of other acids, and a small amount of a weak acid is dissolved in it. Which form does the weak acid take?

The $K_a$ expression answers at once:

$$
K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

Divide both sides by $[\ce{H3O+}]$:

$$
\frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} = \frac{K_a}{[\ce{H3O+}]}
$$

The ratio of base form to acid form depends on one thing only: how $[\ce{H3O+}]$ compares with $K_a$. There are three cases.

- When $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals $K_a$, the ratio is 1: the two forms are equal. Take minus the logarithm of both sides, and that is when pH = p$K_a$.
- When the solution is more acidic than that, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is larger than $K_a$, the ratio is less than 1, and the acid form has the higher concentration.
- When the solution is less acidic than that, the base form has the higher concentration.

Each pH unit is a factor of ten in $[\ce{H3O+}]$ (topic 8.1), so each pH unit away from the p$K_a$ is a factor of ten in the ratio. Here is the ratio at five pH values:

| pH of the solution | [H₃O⁺] is | A⁻ : HA |
|---|---|---|
| p$K_a$ − 2 | 100 × $K_a$ | 1 : 100 |
| p$K_a$ − 1 | 10 × $K_a$ | 1 : 10 |
| p$K_a$ | $K_a$ | 1 : 1 |
| p$K_a$ + 1 | $K_a$ ÷ 10 | 10 : 1 |
| p$K_a$ + 2 | $K_a$ ÷ 100 | 100 : 1 |

It makes sense without the algebra. A solution with a low pH is crowded with H₃O⁺ ions that give protons away, so the acid keeps its proton. A solution with a high pH has few, and the acid loses its proton.

This ties back to topic 8.3. A weak acid alone in water, at a concentration many times its $K_a$, makes a solution whose pH is well below its own p$K_a$. That is the same fact as "only a small part of it is ionized".

<KeyIdea>

Compare the pH of the solution with the p$K_a$ of the acid. Below the p$K_a$, the acid form has the higher concentration. Above it, the base form has. At pH = p$K_a$ the two are equal, and each pH unit away is a factor of ten.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Why a preservative needs an acidic food">

Sorbic acid, HC₆H₇O₂, has a p$K_a$ of 4.76. Nearly all of its effect on mold and yeast comes from its acid form. The same small amount of potassium sorbate is dissolved in a fruit drink at pH 2.76 and in a milk pudding at pH 6.76. In each, what is the ratio of the two forms, and what percent of the sorbate ends up in the acid form?

1. **Compare the pH with the p$K_a$, for the drink.** 2.76 is 2.00 below 4.76. The pH is below the p$K_a$, so the acid form has the higher concentration.

2. **Turn the difference into a ratio.** Two pH units below the p$K_a$ means $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is 100 times $K_a$.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} &= \frac{K_a}{[\ce{H3O+}]} \\
   &= \frac{10^{-4.76}}{10^{-2.76}} \\
   &= 10^{-2.00}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   That is 1 sorbate ion for every 100 sorbic acid molecules.

3. **Turn the ratio into a percent.** The total is both forms together: 100 + 1 = 101 particles. Out of every 101 particles, 100 are in the acid form.

   $$
   \frac{100}{101} = 0.990
   $$

   So 99.0 percent of the sorbate is in the acid form.

4. **Do the same for the pudding.** 6.76 is 2.00 above 4.76, so the base form has the higher concentration, by 100 to 1. The acid form is 1 particle in 101.

   $$
   \frac{1}{101} = 0.0099
   $$

   That is 0.99 percent.

5. **Say what it means.** The same amount of preservative gives 100 times as much of the working form in the drink as in the pudding (99.0 ÷ 0.99 = 100). That is why sorbate is used in acidic foods. It made no difference that the sorbate went in as the potassium salt, which is the base form: the pH of the food decides how it is shared between the two forms.

</Worked>

**A base follows the same rule.** A weak base, B, and its conjugate acid, HB⁺, are a pair in the same way. HB⁺ is the acid form and B is the base form, so compare the pH with the p$K_a$ of HB⁺. ## An indicator is a weak acid with two colors

Topic 4.6 called an indicator a dye that has one color in acid and another in base. Now the reason: an **indicator** is a weak acid whose acid form and base form have different colors. Call the two forms HIn and In⁻. Only a few drops go into the flask, far too little to change the pH. The indicator does not set the pH. It reports it.

Bromothymol blue is the indicator of topic 4.6. Its acid form is yellow and its base form is blue, and its p$K_a$ is about 7.1. More than about a pH unit below 7.1, the yellow form outnumbers the blue by more than ten to one, and all you see is yellow. Far enough above 7.1, the blue form outnumbers the yellow in the same way, and all you see is blue. In between you see both at once, which the eye mixes to green. At pH 7.1, with the two forms equal, the solution is green.

So an indicator does not change at one pH. It changes over a range of roughly two pH units centered near its p$K_a$: about p$K_a$ ± 1. The ranges in tables are the ones people actually see, and they are not exactly symmetric, because the eye picks up one color more easily than the other.

The exam gives a table like this one whenever it is needed, so there is nothing in it to memorize:

| Indicator | Acid form to base form | Changes over pH | p$K_a$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bromocresol green | yellow to blue | 3.8 to 5.4 | about 4.7 |
| Bromothymol blue | yellow to blue | 6.0 to 7.6 | about 7.1 |
| Thymol blue | yellow to blue | 8.0 to 9.6 | about 8.9 |

All three happen to go from yellow to blue, through green. What separates them is where on the pH scale they do it. Thymol blue has an earlier color change as well, near pH 2, which this lesson does not use.

<KeyIdea>

An indicator is a weak acid whose two forms differ in color. Its color tells you whether the pH is below its range, inside it or above it, and nothing more.

</KeyIdea>

## Choosing an indicator for a titration

In a titration the endpoint, the color change you watch for, should come at the equivalence point, to within a drop (topic 4.6). A drop from a buret is about 0.05 mL. Topic 8.5 showed what makes that possible: near the equivalence point the pH leaps by several units for a fraction of a milliliter of titrant.

The rule: choose an indicator whose p$K_a$ is close to the pH at the equivalence point. Then its range holds that pH, the whole range lies on the steep part of the curve, and the color goes all the way from one form's to the other's within a drop or two. An indicator whose range lies on a flat part of the curve changes slowly, over many milliliters, and at the wrong volume.

Naming an indicator is never the whole answer to a question. The reason is the answer: the pH at the equivalence point, and the range that holds it.

<Worked title="The right indicator and the wrong one">

Topic 8.5's flask holds 20.0 mL of 0.150 M lactic acid (3.00 mmol; p$K_a$ 3.86), titrated with 0.200 M NaOH. The equivalence point is at 15.0 mL, where the pH is 8.40. Which of the three indicators in the table marks it, and how far off would bromocresol green be?

1. **Start from the pH at the equivalence point.** It is 8.40 (topic 8.5), above 7, because the flask then holds the lactate ion, a weak base.

2. **Find the range that holds it.** Thymol blue changes from pH 8.0 to pH 9.6, and 8.40 lies inside. Its p$K_a$, about 8.9, is the closest of the three to 8.40. Thymol blue is the choice.

3. **Ask where bromocresol green would finish changing.** It is fully blue at pH 5.4. Use the first section's ratio for lactic acid at that pH. The hydronium concentration, in M:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{H3O+}] &= 10^{-5.4} \\
   &= 4.0 \times 10^{-6}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The acid's constant:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   K_a &= 10^{-3.86} \\
   &= 1.4 \times 10^{-4}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The ratio of lactate ion, A⁻, to lactic acid, HA:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} &= \frac{1.4 \times 10^{-4}}{4.0 \times 10^{-6}} \\
   &= 35
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Turn the ratio into a volume.** With 35 lactate ions for each lactic acid molecule, 35 parts in 36 of the acid have reacted.

   $$
   \frac{35}{36} = 0.972
   $$

   Each lactate ion was made by one OH⁻ ion, and 15.0 mL of the base reacts with all of the acid. So the base added, in mL, is

   $$
   0.972 \times 15.0 = 14.58
   $$

5. **Compare.** With bromocresol green the flask is fully blue at 14.58 mL, 0.42 mL before the equivalence point, and it began to turn long before that. A student who stopped there would report 2.8 percent too little lactic acid (0.42 ÷ 15.0).

</Worked>

<Trap>

Seven mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Comparing the pH with 7.** Whether a solution is acidic says nothing about which form a weak acid is in. Compare the pH with that acid's own p$K_a$.
- **Turning the rule around.** Below the p$K_a$ the acid form wins: a low pH means plenty of H₃O⁺, so the acid keeps its proton.
- **Reading 10 to 1 as 90 percent.** 10 to 1 is 10 parts in 11, about 91 percent.
- **Using a p$K_b$ for a base.** Compare the pH with the p$K_a$ of the base's conjugate acid.
- **Choosing bromothymol blue because "the equivalence point is at pH 7".** That is true of a strong acid with a strong base, at 25 °C, and not when either one is weak, as in this unit's titrations. Find the pH at this equivalence point first.
- **Naming an indicator with no reason.** Give the pH at the equivalence point and the range that holds it.
- **Thinking an indicator changes at the equivalence point by its nature.** It changes when the pH crosses its range, wherever on the curve that happens.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A weak acid in solution is shared between its acid form, HA, and its base form, A⁻. The $K_a$ expression gives their ratio, base form to acid form, as $K_a$ divided by $[\ce{H3O+}]$.
- When the pH of a solution is below the p$K_a$ of an acid, the acid form has the higher concentration. When it is above, the base form has. At pH = p$K_a$ the two are equal.
- Each pH unit away from the p$K_a$ is a factor of ten in the ratio. One unit below it, the acid is 10 parts in 11 in its acid form, about 91 percent.
- For a weak base, compare the pH with the p$K_a$ of its conjugate acid.
- An indicator is a weak acid whose two forms differ in color. It changes color over a range of about two pH units around its own p$K_a$.
- A good indicator for a titration has a p$K_a$ close to the pH at the equivalence point, so that its whole range lies on the steep part of the curve.

</KeyPoints>
