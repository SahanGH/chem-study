Honey is acidic, and most of its acid is gluconic acid, which an enzyme from the bees makes from the sugar glucose. One molecule of gluconic acid, C₆H₁₂O₇, has twelve hydrogen atoms, and six of them are bonded to oxygen atoms. Yet a base takes only one of the twelve.

How can you tell, from a drawing of a molecule, which hydrogen comes off and how easily? That is what structure is for.

## Look at what is left behind

An acid's strength is how readily it gives a proton to water (topic 4.8). Topic 8.3 put a number on it: the larger the $K_a$, and so the lower the p$K_a$, the stronger the acid. Here is the ionization of any weak acid, HA:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

What decides how far this goes is mostly the anion on the right. When the proton leaves, it leaves its bonding electrons behind, and A⁻ has to carry a negative charge. If A⁻ carries that charge easily, it is low in energy, or **stable**, and the ionization goes further. If the charge sits badly on A⁻, the ion takes its proton back and the acid is weak.

This is topic 4.8's rule seen from the inside: the stronger acid has the weaker conjugate base, because a stable anion has little pull on a proton.

Three things help an anion to carry a negative charge, and this lesson takes them in turn:

- The charge sits on an **electronegative atom**.
- Other electronegative atoms nearby pull some of the charge toward themselves.
- The charge is shared by several atoms through **resonance**.

A fourth thing belongs to the acid itself and not to the anion: how strong the bond to the hydrogen is.

<KeyIdea>

To judge an acid, look at its conjugate base. The better the conjugate base carries its negative charge, the more stable it is and the stronger the acid.

</KeyIdea>

## Which hydrogen comes off

Not every hydrogen in a molecule is acidic. Look at what each one is bonded to.

Carbon and hydrogen attract shared electrons almost equally (electronegativities 2.6 and 2.2, topic 1.7). So a C–H bond is barely polar, and if the proton left, the charge would sit on a carbon atom, which carries it badly. The hydrogen atoms of a CH₃ or CH₂ group do not come off in water.

Oxygen is far more electronegative (3.4). An O–H bond is polar, with the hydrogen at its positive end, and an oxygen atom carries a negative charge much better.

So in the molecules of this unit, look for hydrogen on oxygen first. That is why chemists often write an acid's formula with its acidic hydrogen in front: HC₃H₅O₃ for lactic acid says that one hydrogen is acidic and five are not. The polarity of the bond is the first thing to look at, and it is not the only one: a later section meets acids whose bond to hydrogen is no more polar than a C–H bond.

But an O–H group alone does not make much of an acid. Ethanol, C₂H₅OH, has one, and a solution of ethanol in water is not acidic: its p$K_a$ is about 16. Acetic acid, CH₃COOH, also has one O–H, and its p$K_a$ is 4.76. Those eleven units mean a $K_a$ roughly a hundred billion times larger.

The difference is the second oxygen atom. A **carboxylic acid** is a molecule with the group –COOH: a carbon atom with a double bond to one oxygen atom and a single bond to an O–H group. When the proton leaves, what remains is a **carboxylate ion**, –COO⁻.

Two Lewis diagrams can be drawn for a carboxylate ion, with the double bond to one oxygen atom or to the other, and the real ion is the blend of the two (topic 2.6). Each oxygen atom carries half of the negative charge, and the two carbon–oxygen bonds are alike. A charge shared by two atoms is carried more easily than a charge on one. In the ethoxide ion, C₂H₅O⁻, there is no second oxygen, and one oxygen atom carries all of it.

Carboxylic acids are the most common weak acids you will meet: acetic acid, lactic acid, and most of the weak acids in the calculations of this unit. Those with no halogen atom in them mostly have a p$K_a$ between about 3 and 5.

<Worked title="The one acidic hydrogen of gluconic acid">

Gluconic acid is HOCH₂(CHOH)₄COOH: a chain of six carbon atoms with a –COOH group at one end. Which of its twelve hydrogen atoms does it give to a base, and why that one?

1. **Sort the hydrogen atoms by what they are bonded to.** The carbon atom at the far end holds two hydrogen atoms, and each of the next four holds one: 2 + 4 = 6 C–H hydrogens. Each of those five carbon atoms also holds an O–H group: five O–H hydrogens. One more is in the –COOH group. That is 6 + 5 + 1 = 12.

2. **Rule out the six on carbon.** A C–H bond is barely polar, and a carbon atom carries a negative charge badly.

3. **Compare the six on oxygen by the anions they would leave.** Take the proton from any of the five O–H groups along the chain, and the negative charge sits on that one oxygen atom, as in ethoxide. Take it from the –COOH group, and the charge is shared by two oxygen atoms through resonance.

4. **Choose the most stable anion.** The –COOH hydrogen is the acidic one. The ion left is the gluconate ion, HOCH₂(CHOH)₄COO⁻, or C₆H₁₁O₇⁻.

5. **Check against what is known.** The p$K_a$ of gluconic acid is 3.86, in the usual range for a carboxylic acid. And calcium gluconate, a calcium supplement, is Ca(C₆H₁₁O₇)₂: two gluconate ions, each with a charge of 1−, for one Ca²⁺ ion. Each acid molecule gave up one proton and kept the other eleven hydrogen atoms.

</Worked>

## A neighbor that pulls

An electronegative atom does not have to carry the charge itself to help. Put one near the –COO⁻ group and it pulls electron density toward itself through the bonds between them. That draws a little of the negative charge off the two oxygen atoms and spreads it over more of the ion. This pull through bonds is the **inductive effect**.

The pull adds up: trichloroacetic acid, with three chlorine atoms on that carbon, is a stronger acid than chloroacetic acid, with one. And it fades with distance: a halogen farther along a carbon chain from the –COOH group does less.

<Worked title="Ranking four acids from their structures">

Acetic acid is CH₃COOH. In fluoroacetic acid, bromoacetic acid and iodoacetic acid, one hydrogen atom of the CH₃ group is replaced by a halogen atom: FCH₂COOH, BrCH₂COOH and ICH₂COOH. The electronegativities are 2.2 for hydrogen, 2.7 for iodine, 3.0 for bromine and 4.0 for fluorine. Rank the four acids from weakest to strongest, then compare with their p$K_a$ values.

1. **Find what is the same and what differs.** All four have the same –COOH group, and all four leave a carboxylate ion that shares its charge between two oxygen atoms. Only the atom on the neighboring carbon differs.

2. **Say what that atom does to the anion.** It pulls electron density toward itself and away from the –COO⁻ end. The stronger the pull, the more the charge is spread and the more stable the anion.

3. **Rank the pull.** By electronegativity: hydrogen (2.2), then iodine (2.7), then bromine (3.0), then fluorine (4.0).

4. **Turn that into a ranking of the acids.** Weakest to strongest: acetic acid, iodoacetic acid, bromoacetic acid, fluoroacetic acid.

5. **Compare with measurement.** The p$K_a$ values are 4.76, 3.18, 2.90 and 2.59, in that order: each lower than the one before, so each acid is stronger than the one before. Even iodine, the weakest puller of the three halogens, lowers the p$K_a$ by more than one and a half units (4.76 − 3.18 = 1.58).

</Worked>

## Down a group, the bond decides

Now a family in which the acidic hydrogen is bonded to a different atom each time: hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, hydrogen selenide, H₂Se, and hydrogen telluride, H₂Te, the hydrogen compounds of three elements of group 16. In water each is a weak acid, and the p$K_a$ of the first proton is about 7.0, about 3.9 and about 2.6. They get stronger going down the group, by a factor of more than ten thousand from top to bottom.

Electronegativity cannot be the reason. On topic 1.7's scale sulfur and selenium are both 2.6 and tellurium is 2.1. If electronegativity decided, hydrogen telluride would be the weakest of the three.

What changes most going down a group is the size of the atom. A larger atom makes a longer bond to hydrogen, and a longer bond is usually a weaker one (topic 2.2). The H–Te bond is the longest and weakest of the three, so its proton comes away most easily. The larger anion helps as well: the same 1− charge is spread over a bigger ion.

That settles something else. Sulfur and selenium have the same electronegativity as carbon, 2.6, so an S–H or an Se–H bond is no more polar than a C–H bond, and an H–Te bond (2.2 and 2.1) is less polar still. Yet H₂S, H₂Se and H₂Te are all acids in water, and the hydrogen atoms of a CH₃ group are not acidic. The polarity of the bond is the first thing to look at, not the only one: sulfur, selenium and tellurium are larger atoms than carbon, so each holds hydrogen with a longer, weaker bond and carries the charge on a larger ion.

Across a period it is the other way around. Phosphine, PH₃, hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, and hydrogen chloride, HCl, are the hydrogen compounds of three neighbors in period 3. The three atoms are close in size. The bond to hydrogen even gets stronger from P–H to H–Cl, and still the acid strength climbs: PH₃ is not measurably acidic in water, H₂S is a weak acid, and HCl is a strong one. Here electronegativity decides (2.2 for phosphorus, 2.6 for sulfur, 3.2 for chlorine): the more electronegative atom carries the negative charge better.

<KeyIdea>

For hydrogen bonded to different atoms: across a period, the more electronegative atom gives the stronger acid. Down a group, the larger atom, with its longer and weaker bond, gives the stronger acid.

</KeyIdea>

## Oxygen atoms on the central atom

In an **oxyacid** the acidic hydrogen is on an oxygen atom, and that oxygen is bonded to a central atom: H–O–Z. Nitric acid, HNO₃, is HO–NO₂: an O–H group and two more oxygen atoms on nitrogen. Nitrous acid, HNO₂, is HO–NO: an O–H group and one more oxygen atom. The acidic hydrogen is in the same place in both, bonded to the same kind of atom, and yet nitric acid is a strong acid and nitrous acid is a weak one (lesson 4.8's table).

Each oxygen atom on the central atom does two things, and they are the two effects you have already met.

- It is electronegative, so it pulls electron density toward itself, through the nitrogen atom, away from the O–H bond. That is the inductive effect, and it makes the bond more polar and the proton easier to remove.
- It gives the anion one more atom to share the negative charge by resonance. In the nitrate ion, NO₃⁻, three oxygen atoms share one negative charge (topic 2.6 drew its three diagrams). In the nitrite ion, NO₂⁻, only two do.

So do not say "it has more oxygen atoms" and stop. Say what the oxygen atoms do.

This is what the strong acids have in common. Every strong acid of topic 4.2's list leaves behind a conjugate base that carries its charge very well, so well that it essentially never takes a proton back from H₃O⁺: an ion with its charge shared by several oxygen atoms, such as nitrate, or a large halide ion.

## What makes a base

A base takes a proton (topic 4.8), and to bond to that proton it needs a pair of electrons that is not already in a bond. Two kinds of weak base turn up again and again.

**Nitrogen bases.** A nitrogen atom with three bonds has one unshared pair of electrons, and that pair can bond to a proton. Trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N, is one (topic 4.2 called it a weak base): its nitrogen atom takes a proton from water and becomes (CH₃)₃NH⁺, leaving OH⁻ behind.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{(CH3)3N(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{(CH3)3NH+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Its nine C–H hydrogens play no part. Ammonia and methylamine work the same way. The molecule has no OH in it: the hydroxide ion comes from the water.

**Carboxylate ions.** The conjugate base of a carboxylic acid is a weak base. It is stable, because of its shared charge, but not so stable that it never takes a proton: a carboxylic acid is a weak acid, so its conjugate base has some pull (topic 4.8). That is why a solution of a salt such as sodium lactate is slightly basic (topic 8.4).

**The strong bases** are different in kind. The hydroxides of group 1, and calcium, strontium and barium hydroxide, are ionic compounds that release OH⁻ itself into the water (topic 8.2). The conjugate acid of OH⁻ is H₂O, a very weak acid: the same rule as before, read from the other end. A strong base has a very weak conjugate acid, as a strong acid has a very weak conjugate base.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Counting every hydrogen in the formula as acidic.** Look at what each hydrogen is bonded to. The six hydrogen atoms on the carbon chain of gluconic acid, and the two on phosphorus in H₃PO₂, stay on.
- **Reaching for electronegativity down a group.** Down a group the atoms change size, and the longer, weaker bond to hydrogen decides. Electronegativity decides across a period.
- **Saying "it has more oxygen atoms" and stopping.** Say what they do: they pull electron density from the O–H bond, and they share the anion's charge.
- **Taking a stable conjugate base for a strong base.** It is the reverse: an anion that carries its charge well has little pull on a proton, so its acid is strong.
- **Looking for OH in the formula of a base.** A nitrogen base has none. It takes a proton with its unshared pair, and the OH⁻ comes from water.
- **Calling any molecule with an O–H group an acid.** Ethanol's O–H is not acidic in water. A –COOH group is, because of the second oxygen atom.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The strength of an acid follows the stability of its conjugate base: the better the anion carries its negative charge, the stronger the acid, and the weaker the anion is as a base.
- The first place to look for an acidic hydrogen is on an electronegative atom such as oxygen. The hydrogen atoms of CH₃ and CH₂ groups are not acidic.
- A carboxylic acid, with the group –COOH, is a weak acid because its conjugate base, a carboxylate ion, shares its negative charge between two oxygen atoms by resonance.
- An electronegative atom near the acidic group pulls electron density toward itself (the inductive effect) and makes the acid stronger, the more so the more electronegative it is.
- For hydrogen bonded to different atoms, electronegativity decides across a period, and the length and strength of the bond decide down a group, so H₂Te is a stronger acid than H₂S.
- Common weak bases are nitrogen bases, which take a proton with the unshared pair on nitrogen, and carboxylate ions. A strong base, a hydroxide of group 1 or of calcium, strontium or barium, releases OH⁻, whose conjugate acid is water.

</KeyPoints>
