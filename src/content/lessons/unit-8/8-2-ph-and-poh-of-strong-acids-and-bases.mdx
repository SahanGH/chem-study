Stir slaked lime, which is calcium hydroxide, into water, let the cloudiness settle and pour off the clear liquid. That liquid is limewater, and cooks have soaked corn in it for thousands of years to make the dough for tortillas. Very little of the solid dissolves, less than 2 grams in a liter, so limewater is a dilute solution. Yet its pH is above 12.

Calcium hydroxide is a strong base. This lesson is about what "strong" does to a pH, and what it has nothing to do with.

## Strong means all of it

Topics 4.2 and 4.8 met the strong acids. In a **strong acid**, essentially every molecule gives its proton to water. Topic 4.8 gave the working definition: a strong acid hands essentially every proton to water, so its equation takes a single arrow.

The strong acids are the six that topic 4.2 listed: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (its first proton) and HClO₄. This list is not on the equation sheet, so you have to know it. Any other acid in this course is weak unless a question says otherwise.

Now see what that does to a calculation: it leaves nothing to solve. If every molecule of a strong acid with one proton ionizes, then $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is simply the concentration of the acid that was dissolved. There is no equilibrium table, because there is no acid left at equilibrium to put in it.

Two things are set aside here. Water's own ions, 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M of each at 25 °C (topic 8.1), are far too few to matter beside the acid's. And sulfuric acid is a special case: its first proton is strong and its second is not, so its pH is not found this way, and this course does not ask for it.

<Worked title="Perchloric acid">

A solution is 0.0062 M in perchloric acid, HClO₄, a strong acid. Find its pH.

1. **Strong, so all of it ionizes.** Each HClO₄ gives one H₃O⁺, so the hydronium ion concentration is the acid's concentration.

   $$
   [\ce{H3O+}] = 0.0062\ \text{M}
   $$

2. **Take the pH.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= -\log(0.0062) \\
   &= 2.21
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Check it.** 0.0062 is 6.2 × 10⁻³, so the pH must lie between 2 and 3 (topic 8.1), and it does. The concentration has two significant figures, so the pH has two decimal places.

</Worked>

Two different questions get mixed up here. One is how much acid you put in: that is concentrated or dilute. The other is what the acid does once it is in the water: that is strong or weak. A dilute solution of perchloric acid is still a strong acid. Pour in a lot of formic acid and you have a concentrated weak one. Neither word tells you the other. To compare pH, you need both: how much acid, and how much of it gave its proton away.

<KeyIdea>

For a strong acid with one proton, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is the acid's concentration, and the pH is its negative logarithm. How strong the acid is and how much of it is in the flask are separate questions.

</KeyIdea>

## Strong bases: read the formula for OH

The strong bases were met in topic 4.2: they come apart into ions completely when they dissolve, and one of the ions is OH⁻. These are the ones to know: the hydroxides of the group 1 metals (LiOH, NaOH, KOH and the rest) and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂. This list is not on the equation sheet either.

Here are two of them dissolving. Both arrows are single:

$$
\ce{LiOH(s) -> Li+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Sr(OH)2(s) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{Sr^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second is the one to look at. One formula unit of strontium hydroxide puts two OH⁻ into the water. So $[\ce{OH-}]$ is twice the concentration of the strontium hydroxide that dissolved.

<Figure
  src="/figures/8-2-two-hydroxides.svg"
  alt="A box of particles for strontium hydroxide dissolved in water. It holds 4 strontium ions, drawn as dark circles marked 2 plus, and 8 hydroxide ions, drawn as pale pairs of circles marked minus, all separate from one another. There are two hydroxide ions for every strontium ion."
  caption="Four formula units of strontium hydroxide in solution: 4 Sr²⁺ ions and 8 OH⁻ ions. The hydroxide concentration is twice the concentration of the base. Water molecules are left out."
/>

A base takes a longer route to its pH than an acid does, because what you know first is its hydroxide:

1. From the formula, find $[\ce{OH-}]$: the concentration of the base times the number of OH in its formula.
2. Take the pOH, $-\log[\ce{OH-}]$.
3. At 25 °C, pH = 14.00 − pOH.

There is a check that costs nothing. At 25 °C a solution of a base must have a pH above 7. If yours came out below 7, you have reported the pOH.

<Worked title="Strontium hydroxide">

A solution at 25 °C is 0.0037 M in strontium hydroxide, Sr(OH)₂, a strong base. Find its pH.

1. **Read the OH off the formula.** Sr(OH)₂ has two, and they both come off when it dissolves.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{OH-}] &= 2 \times 0.0037 \\
   &= 0.0074\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **pOH first.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pOH} &= -\log(0.0074) \\
   &= 2.13
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Then pH.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.13 \\
   &= 11.87
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Check it.** This is a base, and the pH is above 7. Leaving out the factor of two would have given 11.57, too low by 0.30, which is the logarithm of 2.

</Worked>

## From a pH back to a concentration

Every step so far can be run backward. From a pH, you get the hydronium ion concentration by undoing the logarithm (topic 8.1):

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}
$$

For a strong acid with one proton, that is the concentration of the acid.

For a strong base, go from the pH to the pOH, and from the pOH to $[\ce{OH-}]$. Then divide by the number of OH in the formula to get the concentration of the base.

The bottom side is labeled pH plus pOH equals 14.00 at 25 °C. Any one of the four quantities gives the other three, and no side of the square needs more than one step.

## Comparing and diluting

To compare solutions of strong acids and bases, turn each into the one quantity they share: $[\ce{H3O+}]$ for acids, $[\ce{OH-}]$ for bases. The number on the label is not always that quantity.

  }}
>
Four solutions are at 25 °C. The three hydroxides are strong bases, and hydrobromic acid is a strong acid.

| Solution | What it holds |
|---|---|
| A | 0.011 M rubidium hydroxide, RbOH |
| B | 0.0060 M strontium hydroxide, Sr(OH)₂ |
| C | 0.0090 M sodium hydroxide, NaOH |
| D | 0.018 M hydrobromic acid, HBr |

Which solution has the highest pH?

- Solution A
- Solution B
- Solution C
- Solution D

<div slot="why">

The highest pH goes with the highest $[\ce{OH-}]$. Rubidium hydroxide and sodium hydroxide each give one OH⁻ per formula unit: 0.011 M and 0.0090 M. Strontium hydroxide gives two:

$$
2 \times 0.0060 = 0.012\ \text{M}
$$

That is the highest of the three, though its own concentration is the lowest, so solution B has the highest pH. The pH values are 12.08 for the strontium hydroxide (B), 12.04 for the rubidium hydroxide (A) and 11.95 for the sodium hydroxide (C).

The hydrobromic acid has the largest number on its label, but it is an acid, with a pH of 1.74.

</div>
</Check>

**Diluting.** Add water to a solution of a strong acid until its volume is ten times what it was, and the same moles of H₃O⁺ are spread through ten times the volume. $[\ce{H3O+}]$ falls to one-tenth, and the pH rises by 1. For a strong base the same dilution cuts $[\ce{OH-}]$ to one-tenth, so the pOH rises by 1 and the pH falls by 1. Either way, at 25 °C dilution moves the pH toward 7 and never across it.

**Beyond the exam:** in a strong acid solution as dilute as 10⁻⁸ M, the ions from water itself outnumber the acid's, and the simple rule no longer holds: the pH is just under 7, not 8.

A technician pipets 10.0 mL of a hydrobromic acid solution that has a pH of 2.40 into a 1.00 L flask and fills the flask to the mark with water. Hydrobromic acid is a strong acid. What is the pH of the solution in the flask?

- 4.40, because [H₃O⁺] falls to one-hundredth
- 0.40, because a hundredfold dilution takes 2 off the pH
- 3.40, because one dilution adds 1 to the pH
- 0.024, because the pH falls to one-hundredth

<div slot="why">

10.0 mL becomes 1.00 L, which is 1,000 mL: the volume is 100 times larger, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is one-hundredth of what it was. That is two tenfold steps, and each raises the pH by 1:

$$
2.40 + 2 = 4.40
$$

In numbers, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ goes from 4.0 × 10⁻³ M to 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. The pH goes up, not down, because there is less hydronium in each liter. It is the concentration that is divided by 100, not the pH.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Six mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Forgetting the second hydroxide ion.** Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂ give two OH⁻ each. Double the concentration going forward, and halve it going back.
- **Reporting the pOH of a base as its pH.** At 25 °C a base's pH is above 7.
- **Letting "strong" stand in for "a lot".** A strong acid can be very dilute, and a weak acid can be concentrated.
- **Building an equilibrium table for a strong acid.** It ionizes completely, so the concentration is the answer.
- **Doubling for a group 1 hydroxide.** LiOH, NaOH and KOH give one OH⁻ each.
- **Dividing the pH by the dilution factor.** Dilution divides the concentration. Each tenfold step moves the pH by 1, toward 7.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A strong acid ionizes completely, so for one with a single proton $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals the acid's concentration and the pH is its negative logarithm.
- The strong acids are HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (first proton) and HClO₄. The strong bases are the group 1 hydroxides and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂. Neither list is on the equation sheet.
- For a strong base, $[\ce{OH-}]$ is the base's concentration times the number of OH in its formula. Then find the pOH, and then pH = 14.00 − pOH at 25 °C.
- Working backward, $[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$ and $[\ce{OH-}] = 10^{-\text{pOH}}$, and a group 2 hydroxide's concentration is half its $[\ce{OH-}]$.
- Strong or weak tells you how much of the acid ionizes. Concentrated or dilute tells you how much acid is dissolved. A pH needs both.
- A tenfold dilution of a strong acid raises its pH by 1, and a tenfold dilution of a strong base lowers its pH by 1.

</KeyPoints>
