A school laboratory sets its pH meter with a bottle of calibration solution marked pH 7. Put one drop, about 0.05 mL, of 1.0 M hydrochloric acid into a liter of pure water at 25 °C and the pH falls from 7.00 to 4.30. Put the same drop into a liter of the calibration solution and the meter's reading does not change in the second decimal place.

The solution is a buffer. What it holds is two ions you met in topic 4.8: H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻, the pair that helps to hold the acidity inside your cells steady.

## Two members of one pair

A **buffer** is a solution that holds large amounts of both members of a conjugate acid–base pair (topic 4.8), and so resists a change in its pH when a little strong acid or strong base is added. The member that has the proton is the acid form, and the member without it is the base form, as in topic 8.7.

In the calibration solution the acid form is the dihydrogen phosphate ion, H₂PO₄⁻, and the base form is the hydrogen phosphate ion, HPO₄²⁻. They differ by one proton. They are put in as two salts, potassium dihydrogen phosphate, KH₂PO₄, and sodium hydrogen phosphate, Na₂HPO₄. The K⁺ and Na⁺ ions are spectators.

The pair must be a weak one: a weak acid with its conjugate base, or a weak base with its conjugate acid. Here is why. The acid form has to keep its proton until a base arrives, which a weak acid does. The base form has to be able to take a proton, which the conjugate base of a weak acid can.

"Large amounts" means two things. Each member is present in an amount much larger than the acid or base the solution is meant to take. And neither member is tiny beside the other. That is why a weak acid alone in water is not a buffer. In most solutions of a weak acid only a few percent of it, or less, is in the base form (topic 8.3), so it has almost nothing that can take up added acid.

Topic 8.4 met a buffer once already, without dwelling on it: it is what is left in the flask when a weak acid has reacted with less than enough strong base. So there are two ways to make one.

- Dissolve both members: the weak acid and a salt of its conjugate base.
- Start with one member and turn part of it into the other with a strong base or a strong acid.

## What happens to added acid, and to added base

A strong acid arrives in the buffer as H₃O⁺ ions, and a strong base arrives as OH⁻ ions (topic 8.2). The buffer has something ready for each.

<Worked title="The two reactions of the phosphate buffer">

A buffer holds H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ in large amounts. Write the reaction that uses up added strong acid and the reaction that uses up added strong base, and say what each does to the amounts of the two members.

1. **Name the two forms.** H₂PO₄⁻ has the extra proton: it is the acid form. HPO₄²⁻ is the base form.

2. **Added acid meets the base form.** H₃O⁺ is an acid, so it reacts with the member that can take a proton, HPO₄²⁻.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{H3O+(aq) + HPO4^2-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{H2PO4-(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The arrow is single: H₃O⁺ is the form a strong acid takes in water, and its reaction with a weak base goes essentially to completion (topic 8.4).

3. **Added base meets the acid form.** OH⁻ is a base, so it reacts with the member that has a proton to give, H₂PO₄⁻.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{OH-(aq) + H2PO4-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{HPO4^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   A single arrow again, for a strong base.

4. **Say what each reaction does to the amounts.** Each H₃O⁺ ion that reacts turns one HPO₄²⁻ ion into one H₂PO₄⁻ ion. The base form goes down by the amount of acid added, and the acid form goes up by the same amount. Each OH⁻ ion does the reverse. Either way the total of the two members stays the same.

5. **Say what became of the strong acid or base.** The H₃O⁺ is gone, and in its place there is a little more H₂PO₄⁻, a weak acid. The OH⁻ is gone, and in its place there is a little more HPO₄²⁻, a weak base. Each reaction trades a strong acid or base for a weak one.

</Worked>

<KeyIdea>

A buffer holds both members of a weak conjugate pair. Its base form uses up added H₃O⁺ and its acid form uses up added OH⁻. An explanation of a buffer needs both reactions.

</KeyIdea>

## What is not a buffer

Three things can go wrong. The pair may not be a weak one. The two substances may not be the two members of one pair. Or one member may be missing once the mixture has finished reacting.

So judge a mixture in two steps. First let any reaction with a strong acid or strong base go to completion, in moles (topic 8.4). Then look at what is left, and ask whether both members of one weak pair are there in large amounts.

<Worked title="Buffer or not?">

Four solutions are made up, each in a liter of water. Which are buffers?

- (a) 100 mmol of formic acid, HCHO₂, a weak acid, with 40 mmol of sodium hydroxide.
- (b) Formic acid with pyridinium bromide, the salt that supplies the pyridinium ion, C₅H₅NH⁺, the conjugate acid of the weak base pyridine.
- (c) 50 mmol of sodium formate with 50 mmol of hydrobromic acid, a strong acid.
- (d) Tris, a weak base used in biology laboratories, with its salt Tris hydrochloride, which supplies the conjugate acid of Tris.

1. **(a) React first.** Formic acid reacts with the hydroxide ion, to completion:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{HCHO2(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{CHO2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The hydroxide runs out first. Here is the table of moles, with every value in mmol.

   | | HCHO₂ | OH⁻ | CHO₂⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Before | 100 | 40 | 0 |
   | Change | −40 | −40 | +40 |
   | After | 60 | 0 | 40 |

   What is left is 60 mmol of formic acid and 40 mmol of formate ion: both members of a weak pair, in large amounts. **A buffer**, though no formate was put in.

2. **(b) Look for the pair.** Formic acid is the acid form of one pair, and its base form, the formate ion, was not put in. The pyridinium ion is an acid form too, of another pair, and its base form, pyridine, was not put in either. Both are acids, so they do not react with each other, and nothing here makes more than a trace of either base form. Added OH⁻ would be used up, by either acid. Added H₃O⁺ would find almost nothing to take its proton. **Not a buffer.** Two weak members are not enough: they must be an acid form and its own base form.

3. **(c) React first.** The H₃O⁺ of the hydrobromic acid reacts with the formate ion, the base form, to completion:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{H3O+(aq) + CHO2-(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{HCHO2(aq) + H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Here is the table of moles, with every value in mmol.

   | | H₃O⁺ | CHO₂⁻ | HCHO₂ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Before | 50 | 50 | 0 |
   | Change | −50 | −50 | +50 |
   | After | 0 | 0 | 50 |

   What is left is 50 mmol of formic acid, with Na⁺ and Br⁻ as spectators. All of the base form went into making the acid form, and a weak acid alone is not a buffer. **Not a buffer.**

4. **(d) Look for the pair.** Tris is a weak base, so its conjugate acid is a weak acid. Both members of one pair are present, and nothing reacts to completion. **A buffer**: the conjugate acid uses up added OH⁻, and Tris uses up added H₃O⁺.

</Worked>

The first of the three things, a pair that is not weak, is the case of a strong acid, HX, with a salt of its anion, X⁻. In water all of the acid is H₃O⁺ and X⁻, and X⁻ essentially never takes a proton (topic 4.8). In the drawing, the same added H₃O⁺ ion meets a buffer on the left and a strong acid with its salt on the right.

## Why the pH barely moves

Topic 8.4 found the hydronium concentration of a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base from the $K_a$ expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times {} \\
&\quad \frac{\text{moles of acid form}}{\text{moles of base form}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$K_a$ is fixed at a given temperature. So $[\ce{H3O+}]$ moves only as far as the ratio of the two amounts moves.

Put numbers on that ratio. Take a liter of a phosphate buffer that holds 40 mmol of H₂PO₄⁻ and 40 mmol of HPO₄²⁻, and add 1 mmol of H₃O⁺. The reaction of the worked example turns 1 mmol of the base form into the acid form, which leaves 41 mmol of H₂PO₄⁻ and 39 mmol of HPO₄²⁻. The ratio of acid form to base form goes from 1.00 to

$$
\frac{41}{39} = 1.05
$$

So $[\ce{H3O+}]$ rises by 5 percent. A 5 percent change in $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is about 0.02 of a pH unit.

Now put the same 1 mmol of H₃O⁺ into a liter of pure water, where nothing reacts with it. $[\ce{H3O+}]$ goes from 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M to 1.0 × 10⁻³ M. That is ten thousand times as large, and the pH falls from 7.00 to 3.00 (at 25 °C).

The difference is the two things the buffer does. It removes the strong acid by reaction. And it has so much of both members that the reaction hardly changes their ratio.

Notice what a buffer does not do: it does not hold the pH perfectly still. The ratio did change, a little, and so did the pH. A buffer resists a change in pH.

## A buffer has a limit

Each of the two reactions takes from one member of the pair and adds to the other.

>
A liter of a buffer holds 80 mmol of Tris, a weak base, and 40 mmol of its conjugate acid. A technician adds 10 mmol of hydrobromic acid, a strong acid. What does the buffer hold when the reaction is over, and which way has its pH moved?

- 90 mmol of Tris and 30 mmol of its conjugate acid, and the pH is a little higher
- 70 mmol of Tris and 40 mmol of its conjugate acid, and the pH is a little lower
- 80 mmol of Tris and 40 mmol of its conjugate acid, as before, and the pH has not moved
- 70 mmol of Tris and 50 mmol of its conjugate acid, and the pH is a little lower

<div slot="why">

The H₃O⁺ of the hydrobromic acid is an acid, so it reacts with the base form, which in this buffer is Tris itself. Each H₃O⁺ ion turns one Tris molecule into one ion of the conjugate acid. Tris goes down by 10 mmol, to 70 mmol, and the conjugate acid goes up by 10 mmol, to 50 mmol. The total is still 120 mmol.

The ratio of acid form to base form rose from 0.50 to 0.71, and $[\ce{H3O+}]$ rose with it, so the pH is lower, though only a little.

The Tris that reacted did not vanish: it became the other member. And a buffer does not stop the reaction or pin the pH: the reaction is how it holds the pH nearly steady.

</div>
</Check>

In that question the hydrobromic acid used up an eighth of the Tris, and each further addition uses up more. A buffer works only while both members are left.

Add strong base and the acid form goes down. When it is used up, the next hydroxide ions have nothing to react with. They stay in the solution as OH⁻, and the pH jumps. Add strong acid and the same thing happens at the other end, when the base form is used up.

You have seen this before. On the titration curve of a weak acid (topic 8.5), the nearly flat stretch is the buffer at work, and the steep rise at the equivalence point is the acid form running out.

How much strong acid or base a buffer can take before that happens depends on how much of each member it holds, and topic 8.10 takes that up.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Giving one reaction when the question asks how a buffer works.** An explanation needs both: the base form uses up added acid, and the acid form uses up added base.
- **Having the wrong member react.** Added OH⁻ reacts with the acid form, and added H₃O⁺ with the base form. A base does not use up a base.
- **Saying a buffer keeps the pH constant.** The pH does change, by a little. A buffer resists a change.
- **Calling a strong acid with its salt a buffer.** The anion of a strong acid takes up nothing. The pair must be a weak one.
- **Taking any two weak acids or bases for a buffer.** The two members must be an acid form and its own base form, one proton apart.
- **Judging a mixture before it has reacted.** A weak acid with less than enough strong base becomes a buffer. With an equal amount it does not, and a base form with an equal amount of strong acid does not either.
- **Expecting water to change a buffer's pH the way it changes a strong acid's.** Water changes both members' concentrations alike and leaves their ratio alone.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A buffer is a solution that holds large amounts of both members of a weak conjugate acid–base pair: the acid form and the base form.
- The base form uses up added H₃O⁺ and the acid form uses up added OH⁻. Each reaction goes to completion and turns a strong acid or base into a weak one.
- In a buffer, $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals $K_a$ times the ratio of acid form to base form. A small addition changes that ratio, and so the pH, only a little.
- A buffer resists a change in pH. It does not hold the pH constant.
- A strong acid with its salt, two acid forms from different pairs and a weak acid alone are not buffers. Judge a mixture after any reaction with a strong acid or strong base has gone to completion.
- Diluting a buffer with water leaves its pH almost unchanged, because both members are diluted alike.
- A buffer works until one of its two members is used up, and then the pH changes sharply.

</KeyPoints>
