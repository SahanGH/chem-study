Hexanoic acid is one of the acids behind the sharp smell of goat cheese. Its old name, caproic acid, comes from the Latin word for a goat. A chemist who dissolves a little of it in water and runs in sodium hydroxide solution finds that the smell fades, because the base turns the acid's molecules into ions, and the ions stay in the water. The smell is slow to go: it is nearly gone only when nearly all of the acid is in its ion form.

Where the base is stopped decides what is in the flask. Stop when two fifths of the base the acid needs is in, and the mixture sits near pH 4.7. Stop exactly on time and the pH is about 8.7. Let it run a little long and the pH is above 11.5. The same two chemicals and one reaction give three very different solutions. The skill is telling which one you have.

## React first, then see what is left

You already have two tools. Unit 4 treats a reaction that goes to completion with moles and a limiting reactant. Unit 7 treats a reaction that stops short with an ICE table. A flask in which an acid has met a base may need both, and the order matters.

Start with why a reaction with a strong acid or a strong base can be treated as complete. When H₃O⁺ meets OH⁻, the reaction is the reverse of water's reaction with itself (topic 8.1):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Reversing an equation turns $K$ into $1/K$ (topic 7.6), so its constant is $1/K_w$, which is 1.0 × 10¹⁴ at 25 °C. Topic 4.2 wrote the reaction as

$$
\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}
$$

and that is the same reaction with the water molecule that carries the proton left out: one reaction written two ways (topic 4.8).

A hydroxide ion also takes the proton straight off a weak acid's molecules (topic 4.8). For hexanoic acid, HC₆H₁₁O₂, the acid of the opening:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HC6H11O2(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C6H11O2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Topic 4.2 wrote the same kind of equation for benzoic acid on its way to sodium benzoate. This equation is the acid's ionization, for which $K_a$ = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵, added to the equation above. So its constant is the product of the two (topic 7.6):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{K_a}{K_w} &= \frac{1.3 \times 10^{-5}}{1.0 \times 10^{-14}} \\
&= 1.3 \times 10^{9}
\end{aligned}
$$

A constant that large means that essentially nothing of the limiting reactant is left (topic 7.5). That is why these equations carry a single arrow, and why you can treat them the way Unit 4 treats any reaction: the reactant with fewer moles is used up.

**The table of moles.** Keep the books in millimoles, which are milliliters times molarity (topic 4.6), in a table with three rows: Before, Change and After. It looks like an ICE table and it is not one. It has no $x$ in it, and nothing in it is at equilibrium yet. It records a reaction that ran until something ran out. If an equilibrium is still to come, it comes second and gets its own table.

The routine has three steps:

1. If a strong acid or a strong base is among the things mixed, let it react to completion, in moles.
2. Read the After row and say what the flask now holds in large amounts.
3. Only then choose how to find the pH.

The equation sheet has the $K_a$ and $K_b$ expressions, $K_w$, pH and pOH on it. Nothing on it says to react first, or what is left over. That part is yours.

<KeyIdea>

Stoichiometry first, then equilibrium. A strong acid or a strong base reacts until one reactant is gone. What is left in the flask afterward decides how the pH is found.

</KeyIdea>

## A strong acid with a strong base

Both are fully ionized before they meet (topic 8.2), so the only reaction is H₃O⁺ with OH⁻. The other ions, such as ClO₄⁻ and K⁺, are spectators (topic 4.2), and they stay spectators: they neither give a proton to water nor take one from it.

Whichever of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ is left over sets the pH, exactly as in topic 8.2. There is one thing to watch. The leftover ions are now spread through both solutions, so the concentration is the leftover moles divided by the **total volume**.

If the moles are equal, neither is left over. The flask holds water and spectator ions, and the solution is neutral: pH 7.00 at 25 °C.

<Worked title="Perchloric acid meets potassium hydroxide">

25.0 mL of 0.120 M perchloric acid, HClO₄, a strong acid, is mixed with 15.0 mL of 0.130 M potassium hydroxide, KOH, a strong base. What is the pH of the mixture at 25 °C?

1. **Count the moles.** Milliliters times molarity gives millimoles.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \ce{H3O+}&: (25.0)(0.120) = 3.00\ \text{mmol} \\
   \ce{OH-}&: (15.0)(0.130) = 1.95\ \text{mmol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **React to completion.** The equation is 1 to 1, so the OH⁻ runs out first. Every value in the table is in mmol.

   | | H₃O⁺ | OH⁻ |
   |---|---|---|
   | Before | 3.00 | 1.95 |
   | Change | −1.95 | −1.95 |
   | After | 1.05 | 0 |

3. **Say what is left.** 1.05 mmol of H₃O⁺, with the spectators ClO₄⁻ and K⁺. A strong acid is left over.

4. **Find its concentration in the total volume.** The mixture is 25.0 + 15.0 = 40.0 mL.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{H3O+}] &= \frac{1.05}{40.0} \\
   &= 0.02625\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Take the pH.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= -\log(0.02625) \\
   &= 1.58
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Does it make sense?** The acid started at pH 0.92, which is −log(0.120). Part of it was neutralized and the rest was diluted, so the pH should be higher than 0.92 and still well below 7. It is.

</Worked>

## A weak acid with a strong base: three outcomes

In water alone, a weak acid keeps most of its protons (topic 8.3). A hydroxide ion does not wait for them: it takes the proton off each acid molecule it meets. Write HA for the acid and A⁻ for its conjugate base:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{A-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here eight acid molecules meet three hydroxide ions.

<Figure
  src="/figures/8-4-partial-neutralization-particles.svg"
  alt="Two boxes of particles. The box labeled Before holds eight whole acid molecules, HA, three hydroxide ions and three sodium ions. The box labeled After holds five whole acid molecules, three A⁻ ions and three sodium ions, and no hydroxide ions. Water molecules are not drawn."
  caption="Three OH⁻ ions take the protons of three HA molecules. What is left is both members of the pair, HA and A⁻. Water molecules, including the three that form, are left out."
/>

What the flask holds afterward depends on one comparison only: the moles of OH⁻ added against the moles of HA that were there.

<Figure
  src="/figures/8-4-what-is-left.svg"
  alt="A line showing the moles of hydroxide ion added to a weak acid, HA, with a mark where they equal the moles of HA at the start. To the left of the mark, a shaded stretch is labeled HA and A⁻, a buffer. At the mark, a point is labeled A⁻ alone, a weak base. To the right, a hatched stretch is labeled OH⁻ left over, a strong base."
  caption="Add a strong base to a weak acid and the flask ends up as one of three things. Which one depends only on the moles of OH⁻ added against the moles of HA that were there."
/>

- **Fewer moles of OH⁻ than of HA.** Some HA is left, and A⁻ has been formed. The flask holds large amounts of both members of a conjugate acid–base pair. A solution like that is called a **buffer**. (Topic 8.8 explains what a buffer is good for. Here it is one of three things a flask can turn out to hold.)
- **Equal moles.** All of the HA has become A⁻ and no OH⁻ is left. The flask holds the conjugate base alone, and a conjugate base of a weak acid is a weak base (topic 4.8).
- **More moles of OH⁻ than of HA.** All of the HA has become A⁻, and OH⁻ is left over. A strong base is left over, and it sets the pH.

**The pH of the buffer, from the $K_a$ expression.** Both HA and A⁻ are in the flask, so the acid's ionization equilibrium (topic 8.3) holds. There is one difference from a solution of the acid alone: there is already plenty of A⁻. Here is the expression, and then the same line solved for the hydronium ion:

$$
K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times \frac{[\ce{HA}]}{[\ce{A-}]}
$$

HA and A⁻ share one flask, so both concentrations are moles over the same volume, and the volume cancels. The ratio of the concentrations is the ratio of the moles:

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times \frac{\text{moles of HA}}{\text{moles of A}^-}
$$

Why can the After row's amounts go straight in? The A⁻ already present pushes the acid's ionization back toward HA. That is Le Châtelier's principle (topic 7.9), and the common-ion effect of topic 7.12 is the same push. So the $x$ of the ICE table is even smaller than it would be for the acid alone. It is still checked, as always (topic 7.7). Topic 8.9 writes this same line in logarithms, and nothing new will be in it.

<Worked title="Hexanoic acid with less base than it needs">

50.0 mL of 0.0450 M hexanoic acid, HC₆H₁₁O₂, is mixed with 10.0 mL of 0.0900 M NaOH. For hexanoic acid, $K_a$ = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵. What is the pH of the mixture at 25 °C?

Write HA for hexanoic acid and A⁻ for the hexanoate ion, C₆H₁₁O₂⁻.

1. **Count the moles.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \ce{HA}&: (50.0)(0.0450) = 2.25\ \text{mmol} \\
   \ce{OH-}&: (10.0)(0.0900) = 0.90\ \text{mmol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **React to completion.** Every value is in mmol.

   | | HA | OH⁻ | A⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Before | 2.25 | 0.90 | 0 |
   | Change | −0.90 | −0.90 | +0.90 |
   | After | 1.35 | 0 | 0.90 |

3. **Say what is left.** 1.35 mmol of HA and 0.90 mmol of A⁻: both members of the pair. The flask holds a buffer.

4. **Now the equilibrium, in its own table.** The total volume is 50.0 + 10.0 = 60.0 mL.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{HA}] &= \frac{1.35}{60.0} = 0.0225\ \text{M} \\
   [\ce{A-}] &= \frac{0.90}{60.0} = 0.0150\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   This one is an ICE table, because now something is at equilibrium: the ionization of HA. Every value is in M.

   | | HA | H₃O⁺ | A⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | I | 0.0225 | 0 | 0.0150 |
   | C | −x | +x | +x |
   | E | 0.0225 − x | x | 0.0150 + x |

5. **Leave $x$ out where it is added to or subtracted from a larger number, and solve.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{H3O+}] &= x \\
   &= (1.3 \times 10^{-5}) \times \frac{0.0225}{0.0150} \\
   &= 1.95 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The ratio 0.0225 ÷ 0.0150 is the ratio of the moles, 1.35 ÷ 0.90: the 60.0 mL cancels.

6. **Check the approximation.** $x$ was left out beside two numbers. Test it against the smaller.

   $$
   \frac{1.95 \times 10^{-5}}{0.0150} \times 100\% = 0.13\%
   $$

   That is under 5 percent. (Against the larger number it is 0.09%.)

7. **Take the pH.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= -\log(1.95 \times 10^{-5}) \\
   &= 4.71
   \end{aligned}
   $$

8. **Does it make sense?** There is more HA than A⁻, so the ratio is above 1 and $[\ce{H3O+}]$ comes out a little above $K_a$.

</Worked>

1. **(a) Count and react.** 25.0 mL brings (25.0)(0.0900) = 2.25 mmol of OH⁻, exactly the moles of HA. Every value is in mmol.

   \end{aligned}

   | | A⁻ | HA | OH⁻ |

   | I | 0.0300 | 0 | 0 |

   | E | 0.0300 − x | x | x |

   $$
   \begin{aligned}

   \end{aligned}
   $$

   \end{aligned}

   \begin{aligned}

   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The acid has been exactly neutralized, and the solution is not neutral.

   | | HA | OH⁻ | A⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|

   | Change | −2.25 | −2.25 | +2.25 |
   | After | 0 | 0.36 | 2.25 |

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{OH-}] &= \frac{0.36}{79.0} \\
   &= 0.00456\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pOH} &= -\log(0.00456) \\
   &= 2.34
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= 14.00 - 2.34 \\
   &= 11.66
   \end{aligned}
   $$

9. **What about the hexanoate ion?** It is still a base. But in part (a), with the flask to itself, it made only 4.80 × 10⁻⁶ M of OH⁻. Beside 0.00456 M that is nothing, so it is left out.

## A weak base with a strong acid: the mirror image

Turn everything around. In water alone, only a small share of a weak base's molecules have taken a proton (topic 8.3). But H₃O⁺ is a far stronger acid than water, and a molecule of the base, B, takes its proton essentially every time:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{B(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HB+(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Topic 4.2 wrote an equation of this kind for pyridine, as

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{C5H5N(aq) + H+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{C5H5NH+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

and it is the same reaction with another base.

The example here is hydroxylamine, NH₂OH, a weak base whose conjugate acid is the hydroxylammonium ion, NH₃OH⁺. For hydroxylamine, $K_b$ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁸, so the constant of its reaction with H₃O⁺ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{K_b}{K_w} &= \frac{1.1 \times 10^{-8}}{1.0 \times 10^{-14}} \\
&= 1.1 \times 10^{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is large enough to treat the reaction as complete.

The three outcomes are the mirror image of the weak acid's:

| H₃O⁺ added, against B | Left in the flask | Which is |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer moles | B and HB⁺ | a buffer |
| Equal moles | HB⁺ alone | a weak acid |
| More moles | H₃O⁺ left over | a strong acid |

For the buffer, the acid of the pair is HB⁺, so the constant to use is the $K_a$ of HB⁺, which is $K_w / K_b$:

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times \frac{\text{moles of HB}^+}{\text{moles of B}}
$$

For equal moles, HB⁺ gives protons to water:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HB+(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{B(aq) + H3O+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

and the solution is acidic. Here $x$ is $[\ce{H3O+}]$, so its negative logarithm is the pH itself.

K_a &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.1 \times 10^{-8}} \\
&= 9.1 \times 10^{-7}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now the ICE table, with $x$ as $[\ce{H3O+}]$. Every value is in M.

| | HB⁺ | B | H₃O⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.0462 | 0 | 0 |
| C | −x | +x | +x |
| E | 0.0462 − x | x | x |

Leave $x$ out beside 0.0462:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x^2}{0.0462} &= 9.1 \times 10^{-7} \\
x &= 2.05 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check the approximation:

$$
\frac{2.05 \times 10^{-4}}{0.0462} \times 100\% = 0.44\%
$$

That is under 5 percent. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pH} &= -\log(2.05 \times 10^{-4}) \\
&= 3.69
\end{aligned}
$$

</div>
</Check>

## Why a salt solution can be acidic or basic

The two equal-moles flasks above are nothing more than salt solutions: sodium hexanoate in water, and hydroxylammonium chloride in water. Dissolve either solid salt in water to the same concentration and you get the same solution, with the same pH, as by neutralizing. So a salt solution does not have to be neutral.

To judge a salt, split it into its ions (topic 4.2) and ask of each one: is it the conjugate of something weak?

- The conjugate base of a weak acid, such as the hexanoate ion, takes protons from water and makes the solution basic.
- The conjugate acid of a weak base, such as NH₃OH⁺, gives protons to water and makes the solution acidic.
- The anion of a strong acid (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NO₃⁻, ClO₄⁻) and the cation of a strong base (the group 1 ions, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺) do neither. Topic 4.8 gave the reason for the anions: a strong acid's conjugate base is so weak that it essentially never takes a proton from water.

A salt whose ions are all of the third kind gives a neutral solution. If both of its ions react with water, compare the cation's $K_a$ with the anion's $K_b$: the larger one decides.

The calculation, when a question asks for it, is one you have met twice already, for the hexanoate ion and for NH₃OH⁺: an ICE table for the ion that reacts, with the constant found from $K_w$.

- Sodium perchlorate, NaClO₄
- Methylammonium nitrate, CH₃NH₃NO₃
- Strontium iodide, SrI₂

## A weak acid with a weak base

When neither is strong, the reaction is not sure to go to completion. A weak acid and a weak base pass a proton back and forth until the mixture settles at an equilibrium, with all four species present. How far it goes depends on the two acids.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA(aq) + B(aq) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{A-(aq) + HB+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Which side is favored is topic 4.8's question, and it has topic 4.8's answer: there is an acid on each side, HA and HB⁺, and the side with the weaker acid is favored. Now you can compare the two acids by their $K_a$ values.

Take hexanoic acid and hydroxylamine:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HC6H11O2(aq) + NH2OH(aq) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{C6H11O2-(aq) + NH3OH+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The acids are hexanoic acid ($K_a$ = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵) on the left and NH₃OH⁺ ($K_a$ = 9.1 × 10⁻⁷) on the right. The weaker acid is on the right, so the products are favored. The equation is hexanoic acid's ionization added to the reverse of the hydroxylammonium ion's (topic 7.6), so its constant is the first $K_a$ divided by the second:

$$
\frac{1.3 \times 10^{-5}}{9.1 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 14
$$

That is nothing like 10⁹. With equal amounts mixed, about a fifth of each reactant is still there when the mixture has settled.

This unit stops at the equilibrium and its direction, and does not calculate the pH of such a mixture.

- A minor share of the NH₃OH⁺ ions pass a proton to hexanoate ions; most do not
- Most of the NH₃OH⁺ ions pass a proton to hexanoate ions; a minor share do not
- Essentially every NH₃OH⁺ ion passes a proton to a hexanoate ion, as each would to an OH⁻ ion
- No proton passes at all, because the ions of two dissolved salts are spectators

<div slot="why">

The mixture holds a weak acid, NH₃OH⁺, and a weak base, the hexanoate ion, so they react to an equilibrium:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{NH3OH+(aq) + C6H11O2-(aq) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{NH2OH(aq) + HC6H11O2(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The acid on the left is NH₃OH⁺ and the acid on the right is hexanoic acid. Hexanoic acid has the larger $K_a$, so it is the stronger acid, and the weaker acid is on the left: the reactants are favored. The constant is

$$
\frac{9.1 \times 10^{-7}}{1.3 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.070
$$

and about a fifth of the ions react.

Neither ion is strong, so the reaction is not sure to go to completion, and with a constant of 0.070 it stops far short. And these two ions are not spectators: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are.

## One question sorts every flask

Every flask that a strong acid or a strong base went into was sorted by one question, asked after the complete reaction was over: what is left? A salt dissolved in water is sorted the same way.

- **A strong acid or a strong base left over.** Its concentration in the total volume gives the pH.
- **A weak acid alone or a weak base alone, an ion included.** An ICE table with $K_a$ or $K_b$ gives the pH.
- **Both members of a conjugate pair.** $K_a$ times the ratio of moles gives $[\ce{H3O+}]$.
- **Only ions from strong partners.** The solution is neutral.

<KeyIdea>

Before any pH calculation, name what the flask holds once every complete reaction is over. The numbers come after the name.

</KeyIdea>

<Trap>

Seven mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Putting a strong base or a strong acid into an equilibrium table.** It reacts to completion first, in a table of moles. Only what is left goes into an ICE table.
- **Comparing volumes when the moles decide.** Milliliters times molarity, every time.
- **Dividing by the volume of one solution.** After mixing, every concentration uses the total volume.
- **Calling equal moles "neutral".** A weak acid exactly neutralized by a strong base leaves a weak base, and the pH is above 7 at 25 °C. A weak base exactly neutralized leaves a weak acid.
- **Using the acid's $K_a$ for its conjugate base.** The hexanoate ion needs $K_b = K_w / K_a$. The hydroxylammonium ion needs $K_a = K_w / K_b$.
- **Stopping at the pOH.** When $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$, the pH is 14.00 minus the pOH at 25 °C.
- **Treating Cl⁻, ClO₄⁻ or Na⁺ as an acid or a base.** Ions from strong acids and strong bases do not react with water.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A strong acid or a strong base reacts to completion. Do the stoichiometry first, in a table of moles, and the equilibrium second.
- A strong acid with a strong base leaves whichever was in excess, and its concentration in the total volume gives the pH. Equal moles give pH 7.00 at 25 °C.
- A weak acid with a strong base, in which HA and $\ce{OH-}$ give $\ce{A-}$ and water, leaves one of three things: HA and $\ce{A-}$ together (a buffer), $\ce{A-}$ alone (a weak base, pH above 7 at 25 °C) or leftover $\ce{OH-}$.
- A buffer holds large amounts of both members of a conjugate pair. Its $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is $K_a$ times the moles of the acid form over the moles of the base form.
- A weak base with a strong acid is the mirror image. Equal moles leave the conjugate acid alone, so the pH is below 7 at 25 °C.
- A salt solution is acidic or basic when one of its ions is the conjugate of a weak acid or a weak base, and neutral when every ion comes from a strong one.
- A weak acid and a weak base react to an equilibrium that favors the side with the weaker acid.

</KeyPoints>
