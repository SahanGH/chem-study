In topic 4.6 a flask of lactic acid stayed yellow while 15 mL of sodium hydroxide ran in, and then turned blue within a couple of drops. The color reports one moment. Put a pH probe in the same flask, write down the reading after every addition, and the whole run shows: a quick climb at the start, a long stretch where the pH hardly moves, and then a leap of more than six pH units between 14.5 mL and 15.5 mL.

## The titration curve

A **titration curve** is a graph of the pH of the flask, up the side, against the volume of titrant added, along the bottom. It is the titration of topic 4.6 with every moment recorded, not only the last.

You already know how to find any point on it. Each portion of titrant reacts to completion with what is in the flask (topic 8.4). So every point on the curve is one of topic 8.4's flasks: count the moles, react, see what is left, find the pH.

Where the equivalence point lies along the bottom is a matter of moles and the balanced equation, as in topic 4.6, and of nothing else. Take the flask of topic 4.6's diagram. It holds 20.0 mL of 0.150 M lactic acid:

$$
(20.0)(0.150) = 3.00\ \text{mmol}
$$

The reaction is 1 to 1, so the equivalence point needs 3.00 mmol of OH⁻. From 0.200 M NaOH that is

$$
\frac{3.00}{0.200} = 15.0\ \text{mL}
$$

On the curve, the equivalence point is in the middle of the steepest part of the rise.

## Four stretches of a weak acid's curve

Write HA for the weak acid and A⁻ for its conjugate base; the titrant is a strong base. What the flask holds changes as the base goes in, and so does the method that gives the pH.

| Stretch | The flask holds | What sets the pH |
|---|---|---|
| Before any base | HA alone | the weak acid's equilibrium (topic 8.3) |
| Before the equivalence point | HA and A⁻ | $K_a$ × moles of HA ÷ moles of A⁻ (topic 8.4) |
| At the equivalence point | A⁻ alone | the weak base's equilibrium, with $K_b$ (topic 8.4) |
| After it | A⁻ and leftover OH⁻ | the leftover OH⁻ in the total volume |

The curve is four methods joined end to end. Nothing in it is new. What is new is seeing where one hands over to the next.

## Halfway there: pH = pKa

The **half-equivalence point** is the point at half of the equivalence volume. Half of the acid's moles have been turned into A⁻ and half are still HA, so the moles of HA and of A⁻ are equal. Topic 8.4's line, $K_a$ times the moles of HA divided by the moles of A⁻, then reads $[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times 1$:

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \quad\text{so}\quad \text{pH} = \text{p}K_a
$$

This is not on the equation sheet, and it is worth knowing by heart, because it works backward. Read the pH at half of the equivalence volume and you have the acid's p$K_a$, and from it

$$
K_a = 10^{-\text{p}K_a}
$$

This is how a $K_a$ is measured.

Around the half-equivalence point the curve is nearly level. The flask holds comparable amounts of HA and A⁻, which makes it a buffer (topic 8.4), and this stretch of the curve is called the **buffer region**. Topic 8.8 explains why the pH moves so little there.

"Half" means half of the equivalence volume. It is not half of the volume of acid in the flask, and not half of the total volume.

One caution. The result leans on topic 8.4's approximation, that the amounts in the flask are far larger than the $[\ce{H3O+}]$, which holds for lactic acid and propanoic acid here. For the made-up acid of the last section, whose first proton is a stronger acid than either, the reading halfway to its first rise comes out two hundredths of a pH unit above the p$K_a$.

<KeyIdea>

At the half-equivalence point of a weak acid, half of the acid has become its conjugate base, the two are equal in amount, and pH = p$K_a$. The volume there is half of the equivalence volume.

</KeyIdea>

## At the equivalence point: not pH 7

The equivalence point is a statement about moles (topic 4.6). It says nothing about the pH. The pH there depends on what the flask holds, and for a weak acid titrated with a strong base it holds the conjugate base alone, a weak base. So the pH is above 7 at 25 °C.

For a strong acid titrated with a strong base, the flask at the equivalence point holds water and spectator ions, and of the titrations in this unit, only then is the pH 7.00 at 25 °C.

<Worked title="The equivalence point of lactic acid, by hand">

20.0 mL of 0.150 M lactic acid, HC₃H₅O₃, is titrated with 0.200 M NaOH. The p$K_a$ of lactic acid is 3.86. What is the pH at the equivalence point, at 25 °C? Write HA for lactic acid and A⁻ for the lactate ion, C₃H₅O₃⁻.

1. **Find the equivalence volume.** The flask holds 3.00 mmol of HA, which needs 3.00 mmol of OH⁻. The volumes are in mL.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   (20.0)(0.150) &= 3.00 \\[4pt]
   \frac{3.00}{0.200} &= 15.0
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The equivalence point is at 15.0 mL.

2. **React to completion.** Every value is in mmol.

   | | HA | OH⁻ | A⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Before | 3.00 | 3.00 | 0 |
   | Change | −3.00 | −3.00 | +3.00 |
   | After | 0 | 0 | 3.00 |

3. **Say what is left.** The lactate ion alone, a weak base. It takes protons from water.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{C3H5O3-(aq) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
   &\quad \ce{HC3H5O3(aq) + OH-(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Find its concentration in the total volume.** The flask now holds 20.0 + 15.0 = 35.0 mL.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{A-}] &= \frac{3.00}{35.0} \\
   &= 0.0857\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Find $K_b$.** The question gives the p$K_a$ of lactic acid, so undo the logarithm.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   K_a &= 10^{-3.86} \\
   &= 1.38 \times 10^{-4}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   For lactic acid and its own conjugate base, the lactate ion, $K_a \times K_b = K_w$, and $K_w$ is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   K_b &= \frac{K_w}{K_a} \\
   &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{10^{-3.86}} \\
   &= 7.24 \times 10^{-11}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Set up the table, with $x$ as $[\ce{OH-}]$.** Every value is in M.

   | | A⁻ | HA | OH⁻ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | I | 0.0857 | 0 | 0 |
   | C | −x | +x | +x |
   | E | 0.0857 − x | x | x |

   Leave $x$ out beside 0.0857:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{x^2}{0.0857} &= 7.24 \times 10^{-11} \\
   x &= 2.49 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

7. **Check the approximation.**

   $$
   \frac{2.49 \times 10^{-6}}{0.0857} \times 100\% = 0.0029\%
   $$

   That is far under 5 percent.

8. **pOH first, then pH.** $x$ is $[\ce{OH-}]$.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pOH} &= -\log(2.49 \times 10^{-6}) \\
   &= 5.60
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= 14.00 - 5.60 \\
   &= 8.40
   \end{aligned}
   $$

</Worked>

## A weak base titrated with a strong acid

Turn the titration around: a weak base, B, in the flask and a strong acid in the buret. The flask starts basic, and each portion of acid turns some B into HB⁺ (topic 8.4):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{B(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HB+(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the curve falls.

It has the same four stretches, mirrored: B alone; B and HB⁺ together, a buffer region; HB⁺ alone at the equivalence point; leftover H₃O⁺ after it.

At the half-equivalence point the moles of B and HB⁺ are equal, so $[\ce{H3O+}]$ equals the $K_a$ of HB⁺, and the pH equals the p$K_a$ of the conjugate acid, HB⁺. It is the conjugate acid's p$K_a$ that is read from the curve, never a p$K_b$.

At the equivalence point the flask holds HB⁺ alone, a weak acid, so the pH is below 7 at 25 °C.

   \end{aligned}

## A curve with two rises

Some acids have two acidic protons, and a strong base takes them one after the other. To see the pattern at its plainest, this section uses an acid made up for the purpose. Call it H₂A, and give it p$K_{a1}$ = 3.00 and p$K_{a2}$ = 8.00. H₂A is not a real substance: real acids with two acidic protons behave in the same way, with less tidy numbers.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H2A(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HA-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{HA-(aq) + OH-(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{A^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first proton is far more acidic than the second: the two p$K_a$ values are five units apart. So essentially every molecule has lost its first proton before the second ones start to go. The curve shows it: two steep rises, one for each proton.

Here is the curve for 20.0 mL of 0.120 M H₂A, titrated with 0.200 M NaOH.

Read such a curve in four moves:

- **Count the rises.** Each rise marks one proton removed from every molecule. Two rises: two acidic protons.
- **Read the volumes.** Each proton takes the same moles of OH⁻. The flask holds (20.0)(0.120) = 2.40 mmol of acid, so the first rise is at 2.40 ÷ 0.200 = 12.0 mL and the second at twice that, 24.0 mL.
- **Name what the flask holds.** Before the first rise: H₂A and HA⁻. At the first rise: nearly all HA⁻. Between the rises: HA⁻ and A²⁻. At the second rise: nearly all A²⁻. After it: A²⁻ and leftover OH⁻. Within a stretch the main form changes at the halfway point: before the first rise it is H₂A until 6.0 mL and HA⁻ after that, and between the rises it is HA⁻ until 18.0 mL and A²⁻ after that.
- **Find a p$K_a$ at each halfway point.** Halfway to the first rise, at 6.0 mL, H₂A and HA⁻ are very nearly equal in amount, and the pH there is very nearly p$K_{a1}$: the curve reads 3.02, two hundredths above 3.00 for the reason given in "Halfway there". Halfway between the two rises, at 18.0 mL, HA⁻ and A²⁻ are equal in amount, and the pH there is p$K_{a2}$: the curve reads 8.00. So each halfway reading gives one p$K_a$, the first reading the first proton's and the second reading the second proton's.

**Beyond the exam:** the concentration of each form of the acid at a point on a curve like this can be calculated, but the exam asks you only to read the curve: how many protons, which forms, which p$K_a$.

<Trap>

Eight mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Taking pH 7 for every equivalence point.** Of the titrations in this unit, it is 7.00 at 25 °C only for a strong acid with a strong base. A weak acid ends above 7 and a weak base below.
- **Halving the wrong volume.** The half-equivalence point is at half of the equivalence volume, not half of the flask's volume or of the total.
- **Letting the acid's strength move the equivalence point.** Moles and the balanced equation set the volume; strength sets the pH.
- **Dividing by the volume of the flask alone.** Every concentration after mixing uses the total volume.
- **Reading a p$K_a$ off a strong acid's curve.** There is no conjugate pair in the flask to be equal.
- **Using a p$K_b$ at a weak base's half-equivalence point.** The reading there is the p$K_a$ of the conjugate acid.
- **Reading a p$K_a$ at a rise.** On a curve with two rises, each p$K_a$ is read halfway to its rise, where two forms are equal in amount. At a rise the flask holds nearly all one form.
- **Stopping at the pOH** past the equivalence point of an acid. The leftover ion is OH⁻, and the pH takes one more step.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A titration curve is pH against volume of titrant added, and every point on it is a flask of topic 8.4: react in moles, see what is left, find the pH.
- The equivalence volume comes from moles and the balanced equation, the same for a strong acid and a weak one.
- A weak acid's curve has four stretches, set in turn by the weak acid alone, by both members of the pair, by the conjugate base alone and by leftover OH⁻.
- At the half-equivalence point, at half of the equivalence volume, the two members of the pair are equal in amount and pH = p$K_a$, which is how a p$K_a$ is read from a curve.
- At the equivalence point the pH is above 7 for a weak acid titrated with a strong base, below 7 for a weak base titrated with a strong acid, and, of the titrations in this unit, 7.00 at 25 °C only when both are strong.
- A curve with two rises belongs to an acid with two acidic protons. The second rise comes at twice the volume of the first, the pH halfway to the first rise is the first p$K_a$, and the pH halfway between the rises is the second.

</KeyPoints>
