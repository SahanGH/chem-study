Pure water conducts electricity. Only barely, so that it takes a sensitive meter to see it, but the current is there, and a current through water is carried by ions. The only thing in pure water is water. So the ions must come from the water molecules themselves, and they are the reason every solution in this unit has a pH.

## Water reacts with itself

Topic 4.8 said that an acid gives a proton and a base takes one. A water molecule can do either. When it takes a proton it becomes the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺, and when it gives one up, what is left is the hydroxide ion, OH⁻.

So one water molecule can give a proton to another:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{H2O(l) + H2O(l) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **autoionization of water**: water forming ions from itself by passing a proton from one molecule to another.

It is an equilibrium, so it has a constant. Water is a pure liquid and is left out of the expression (topic 7.3), which leaves only the two ions:

$$
K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}]
$$

At 25 °C, $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$. That is a very small $K$, so the equilibrium lies far toward the reactants (topic 7.5): nearly all of the water stays as molecules.

**Pure water.** Each proton transfer makes one H₃O⁺ and one OH⁻, so in pure water the two concentrations are equal. Call each $x$. Then, at 25 °C:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2 &= 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \\
x &= 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

For scale, a liter of water holds about 55 mol of water molecules, so there is roughly one hydronium ion for every 550 million water molecules.

**Two names, one ion.** The exam's equation sheet writes this same constant with H⁺(aq) where this lesson writes H₃O⁺(aq). They are the same ion, and "hydrogen ion" and "hydronium ion" are two names for it: a proton in water is always attached to a water molecule (topic 4.8). The exam accepts either. This site writes H₃O⁺.

**Not only pure water.** The equilibrium is there in every solution made with water. So in any dilute water solution at 25 °C, the product of the two concentrations is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. Add something that raises $[\ce{H3O+}]$, and the equilibrium shifts back toward water (Le Châtelier's principle, topic 7.9), using up OH⁻ until the product is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ again. Raise $[\ce{H3O+}]$ to 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ M, fifty times its value in pure water, and $[\ce{OH-}]$ falls to 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ M, one-fiftieth of its value in pure water.

Neither concentration is ever zero. An acidic solution still holds some OH⁻, and a basic one still holds some H₃O⁺. The three words are about which ion there is more of:

- A solution is **neutral** when $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{OH-}]$.
- It is **acidic** when $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is the larger of the two.
- It is **basic** when $[\ce{OH-}]$ is the larger of the two.

<KeyIdea>

In water at 25 °C, whatever is dissolved:

$$
[\ce{H3O+}] \times [\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
$$

Raise one and the other falls.

</KeyIdea>

## pH: counting in powers of ten

The values of $[\ce{H3O+}]$ met in the lab run from more than 1 M down to less than 10⁻¹⁴ M. Numbers spread over a range like that are awkward to compare, so chemists report the exponent:

$$
\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]
$$

And the same for hydroxide:

$$
\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]
$$

Both are on the equation sheet. A "p" in front of any quantity means "the negative logarithm of": this unit also uses $\text{p}K_w$, and later $\text{p}K_a$.

The minus sign is there so that the usual values come out positive. Its cost is that the scale runs backward: **the higher $[\ce{H3O+}]$, the lower the pH**. Topic 7.9 said this in passing.

Three facts about logarithms are all this unit needs.

1. **The logarithm of a product is a sum.** Take $[\ce{H3O+}] = 4.7 \times 10^{-6}$ M:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &-\log(4.7 \times 10^{-6}) \\
   &= -\log 4.7 - \log 10^{-6} \\
   &= -0.67 + 6 \\
   &= 5.33
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   So the pH of a concentration $a \times 10^{-b}$ M, with $a$ between 1 and 10, lies between $b - 1$ and $b$. That is a check you can do with no calculator: 4.7 × 10⁻⁶ M must have a pH between 5 and 6.

2. **Going back undoes the logarithm.**

   $$
   [\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}
   $$

   $$
   [\ce{OH-}] = 10^{-\text{pOH}}
   $$

3. **One pH unit is a factor of ten.** A solution at pH 4 has ten times the hydronium concentration of a solution at pH 5, and 1,000 times that of a solution at pH 7.

In the figure, read across a row to see one solution three ways, and read down to see each step change both concentrations by a factor of ten, in opposite directions.

**How many digits.** In pH 5.33, the 5 only says which power of ten, as the exponent does in 4.7 × 10⁻⁶. The digits after the decimal point carry the 4.7. So the digits after the decimal point are a pH's significant figures: a concentration with two significant figures gives a pH with two decimal places. Going back, a pH with two decimal places gives a concentration with two significant figures. Nearly every constant in this unit has two significant figures, so nearly every pH in it has two decimal places.

## pH and pOH add to 14.00

Take the negative logarithm of both sides of $K_w$'s expression at 25 °C:

$$
[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
$$

The logarithm of a product is a sum (fact 1), so the left side becomes the pH plus the pOH. At 25 °C:

$$
\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00
$$

The 14.00 is $\text{p}K_w$, the negative logarithm of $K_w$. This line is on the equation sheet, as 14 = pH + pOH.

So there are two ways from an $[\ce{OH-}]$ to a pH: through $[\ce{H3O+}]$ with $K_w$, or through the pOH. Both give the same answer.

At 25 °C, pure water has a pH of 7.00 and a pOH of 7.00. At that temperature an acidic solution has a pH below 7.00 and a pOH above it, and a basic solution has a pH above 7.00 and a pOH below it.

<Worked title="A soil extract">

Blueberry bushes grow well only in acidic soil. A grower shakes a soil sample with pure water and filters it. At 25 °C the clear extract has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 4.7 \times 10^{-6}$ M. Find the pH, $[\ce{OH-}]$ and the pOH of the extract.

1. **pH from the hydronium concentration.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= -\log(4.7 \times 10^{-6}) \\
   &= 5.33
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   This is the value worked out in fact 1.

2. **$[\ce{OH-}]$ from $K_w$.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{OH-}] &= \frac{K_w}{[\ce{H3O+}]} \\
   &= \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{4.7 \times 10^{-6}} \\
   &= 2.1 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **pOH, two ways.** From the concentration, keeping the unrounded 2.13 × 10⁻⁹:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pOH} &= -\log(2.13 \times 10^{-9}) \\
   &= 8.67
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   From the pH:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pOH} &= 14.00 - 5.33 \\
   &= 8.67
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   They agree.

4. **Say what it means.** $[\ce{H3O+}]$ is larger than $[\ce{OH-}]$, by a factor of about 2,200, so the extract is acidic: right for blueberries.

</Worked>

## Pure water at other temperatures

$K_w$ is an equilibrium constant, and like any $K$ it changes with temperature (topic 7.9). Pulling a proton off one water molecule and putting it on another takes energy: the autoionization of water is endothermic. So $K_w$ rises when water is warmed and falls when it is cooled. These are measured values:

| Temperature | $K_w$ |
|---|---|
| 10 °C | 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ |
| 25 °C | 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ |
| 40 °C | 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁴ |

The matching digits at 10 °C and 40 °C are a coincidence. The two values differ by a factor of ten.

Neutral is a comparison, and the comparison does not change with temperature: is there as much H₃O⁺ as OH⁻? What moves is the pH where the answer is yes.

<Worked title="Cold water">

A bottle of pure water is cooled to 10 °C, where $K_w = 2.9 \times 10^{-15}$. Find $[\ce{H3O+}]$, $[\ce{OH-}]$ and the pH. Is the water still neutral?

1. **It is still pure water.** Every H₃O⁺ was made together with one OH⁻, so the two concentrations are equal. Call each $x$.

2. **Use $K_w$ for this temperature.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   x^2 &= 2.9 \times 10^{-15} \\
   x &= 5.4 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{M}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   That is both $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and $[\ce{OH-}]$.

3. **Take the pH,** from the unrounded 5.39 × 10⁻⁸.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{pH} &= -\log(5.39 \times 10^{-8}) \\
   &= 7.27
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Answer the question.** The water is neutral, because the two concentrations are equal. Its pH is 7.27, not 7.00, because cold water has ionized less. Its pOH is 7.27 too, and the two add to 14.54, which is $\text{p}K_w$ at 10 °C. The sum is 14.00 only at 25 °C.

</Worked>

<KeyIdea>

Neutral is about the two concentrations being equal, and cold or hot water passes that test. The pH it shows then depends on $K_w$ at that temperature. Only at 25 °C is it 7.00.

</KeyIdea>

<Trap>

Five mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Reading pH 7 as "neutral" when the water is not at 25 °C.** Compare the two concentrations instead. If the question gives a $K_w$ other than 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, that is the one to use, and 14.00 is no longer the sum of pH and pOH.
- **Reporting the pOH as the pH.** The negative logarithm of a hydroxide concentration is the pOH. A basic solution at 25 °C must come out above 7.
- **Reading a pH difference as a plain ratio.** A solution at pH 4 has 1,000 times the hydronium concentration of one at pH 7, not about twice.
- **Thinking an acidic solution has no OH⁻.** Both ions are in every water solution. What differs is which concentration is larger.
- **Giving a pH the wrong number of digits.** Two significant figures in the concentration, two decimal places in the pH.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Water passes protons between its own molecules:

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  &\ce{2H2O(l) <=>} \\
  &\quad \ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
  \end{aligned}
  $$

  At 25 °C, in every dilute water solution:

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  K_w &= [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] \\
  &= 1.0 \times 10^{-14}
  \end{aligned}
  $$

- H⁺(aq) and H₃O⁺(aq) are two ways to write one ion, and the exam accepts both.
- $\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H3O+}]$ and $\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]$, so $[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$. A lower pH means more hydronium, and each pH unit is a factor of ten.
- At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14.00.
- The digits after the decimal point are a pH's significant figures, so a two-figure concentration gives a pH with two decimal places.
- Neutral means equal concentrations of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻. In pure water that happens at pH 7.00 only at 25 °C, because $K_w$ grows with temperature.

</KeyPoints>
