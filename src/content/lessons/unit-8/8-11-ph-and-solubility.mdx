The steel tanks of a brewery slowly grow a hard, chalky crust that brewers call beerstone. Hot water and scrubbing barely touch it. An acid cleaner takes it off. Beerstone is mostly calcium oxalate, a salt that hardly dissolves in water.

Why should acid help? And why does acid do almost nothing for some other salts that hardly dissolve?

## Taking an ion away

Start with what you know. A saturated solution with solid lying in it is an equilibrium (topic 7.11). For strontium fluoride:

$$
\ce{SrF2(s) <=> Sr^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{sp} &= [\ce{Sr^2+}][\ce{F-}]^2 \\
&= 4.33 \times 10^{-9}
\end{aligned}
$$

That value is for 25 °C, and it is the one in topic 7.11's table.

Topic 7.12 put more of one ion into a solution like this, and less solid dissolved. Now do the opposite: take an ion away.

Acid can take an ion away if that ion is a base. The fluoride ion is the conjugate base of hydrofluoric acid, HF, which is a weak acid. The conjugate base of a weak acid is itself a weak base (topics 4.8 and 8.4), so F⁻ takes a proton from H₃O⁺:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{F-(aq) + H3O+(aq) ->} \\
&\quad \ce{HF(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Follow what happens next with $Q$ (topics 7.10 and 7.11).

$$
Q = [\ce{Sr^2+}][\ce{F-}]^2
$$

Some F⁻ has become HF, so [F⁻] is lower, and $Q$ is now less than $K_{sp}$. The solution is no longer saturated. More solid dissolves, and it goes on dissolving until $Q$ is back up to $K_{sp}$.

Le Châtelier's principle (topic 7.9) tells the same story. F⁻ is a product of the dissolving equation. Take away a product and the equilibrium shifts toward products: more solid becomes ions.

Look at where it ends. More strontium is in solution than before, so strontium fluoride is **more soluble in the acidic solution** than in pure water. With enough acid, most of the fluorine that has dissolved is in HF molecules.

<KeyIdea>

When the anion of a slightly soluble salt is a weak base, acid makes the salt more soluble. H₃O⁺ turns the anion into its conjugate acid, the concentration of the anion falls, and more solid dissolves to replace it.

</KeyIdea>

## The question that decides

H₃O⁺ can take away only an ion that it reacts with. So one question decides whether acid changes a salt's solubility: **is the anion a base?**

An anion that is the conjugate base of a weak acid is a base, and acid raises the solubility of its salts. Fluoride and oxalate are two such anions, and so are carbonate, phosphate and sulfide.

An anion that is the conjugate base of a strong acid is not a base to any degree that matters. Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NO₃⁻ and ClO₄⁻ essentially never take a proton in water (topic 4.8). H₃O⁺ has nothing to react with, no ion is taken away, $Q$ stays at $K_{sp}$, and the solubility is essentially the same in acid as in water.

Notice what decides: the anion in the solid, not how strong the acid in the bottle is. Any strong acid supplies the H₃O⁺. This lesson uses nitric acid, because its nitrate ion takes no part. An acid that brought one of the salt's own ions would also act as a common ion (topic 7.12).

When a question asks you to explain, a full answer says three things:

1. Name the anion and say that it is a base, the conjugate base of a weak acid.
2. Say what H₃O⁺ does to it.
3. Say how the dissolving equilibrium responds: $Q$ falls below $K_{sp}$, or the equilibrium shifts toward the ions, so more solid dissolves.

Naming the anion alone is a third of an answer.

<Worked title="Two lead salts and a little nitric acid">

Two beakers hold saturated solutions at 25 °C, each with solid on the bottom: lead(II) fluoride, PbF₂, in one and lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂, in the other. A few drops of nitric acid, a strong acid, go into each, so the volume hardly changes. HF is a weak acid and HBr is a strong acid. In which beaker does the acid make more of the solid dissolve?

1. **Write each dissolving equation and find the anion.**

   $$
   \ce{PbF2(s) <=> Pb^2+(aq) + 2F-(aq)}
   $$

   $$
   \ce{PbBr2(s) <=> Pb^2+(aq) + 2Br-(aq)}
   $$

   The cation is the same in both, so whatever differs between the beakers is the anion's doing. The anions are F⁻ and Br⁻.

2. **Ask whether each anion is a base.** F⁻ is the conjugate base of a weak acid, HF, so it is a weak base. Br⁻ is the conjugate base of a strong acid, HBr, so it is not.

3. **Lead(II) fluoride.** H₃O⁺ turns F⁻ into HF, by the equation of the first section. [F⁻] falls, so $Q$ drops below $K_{sp}$:

   $$
   Q = [\ce{Pb^2+}][\ce{F-}]^2
   $$

   More PbF₂ dissolves until $Q$ equals $K_{sp}$ again.

4. **Lead(II) bromide.** H₃O⁺ does not react with Br⁻, and it does not react with Pb²⁺. Nothing is taken away, $Q$ still equals $K_{sp}$, and essentially no more PbBr₂ dissolves.

5. **The nitrate ions.** The nitrate ions that came with the acid take no part in either beaker: lead(II) nitrate is soluble (topic 4.7's rule).

6. **$K_{sp}$.** Each solid has its own, and each is the same after the acid as before. The temperature has not changed.

The acid makes more solid dissolve in the beaker of lead(II) fluoride. In the beaker of lead(II) bromide it makes essentially no difference.

</Worked>

## Hydroxides: the pH works both ways

In a slightly soluble hydroxide the anion is the hydroxide ion itself, a strong base. H₃O⁺ reacts with it essentially completely:

$$
\ce{H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> 2H2O(l)}
$$

And for a hydroxide the pH is more than a way of adding acid. The pH is itself a measure of [OH⁻] (topic 8.1): a higher pH means more OH⁻ in the solution, and OH⁻ is one of the ions the solid gives. So raising the pH works on a hydroxide as a common ion does (topic 7.12), and lowers its solubility.

For a hydroxide such as nickel(II) hydroxide:

- Lower the pH, and it is more soluble.
- Raise the pH, and it is less soluble.

<Worked title="Nickel out of a spent battery, and back">

The positive plate of a nickel–metal hydride cell, the common rechargeable AA battery, holds nickel(II) hydroxide, Ni(OH)₂, once the cell has run down. It is a pale green solid that is very slightly soluble in water. A recycler wants the nickel in solution, away from the rest of the cell, and later wants it back as a solid. Explain how a change in pH does each.

1. **Write the equilibrium.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Ni(OH)2(s) <=>} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ni^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   K_{sp} = [\ce{Ni^2+}][\ce{OH-}]^2
   $$

2. **Lower the pH to dissolve it.** Added acid supplies H₃O⁺, which reacts with OH⁻ to make water. [OH⁻] falls, $Q$ drops below $K_{sp}$, and more solid dissolves. With enough acid all of it dissolves:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\ce{Ni(OH)2(s) + 2H3O+(aq) ->} \\
   &\quad \ce{Ni^2+(aq) + 4H2O(l)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Raise the pH to bring it back.** One way to get the nickel back is to raise the pH. Adding a strong base to the nickel solution raises [OH⁻]. Once enough has gone in, $Q$ is above $K_{sp}$, and Ni(OH)₂ precipitates until $Q$ has fallen to $K_{sp}$ (topic 7.11). The more OH⁻ in the solution, the less Ni²⁺ can stay in it.

4. **One equilibrium did both.** Acid takes OH⁻ away and the solid dissolves. Base adds OH⁻ and the solid forms. $K_{sp}$ is the same throughout.

</Worked>

## What does not change, and what is not true

$K_{sp}$ does not change when the pH does. It is a constant for the solid at a given temperature, whatever else is in the water. Topic 7.12 said the same of a common ion. A change in pH changes a concentration, so it changes $Q$, and the solid dissolves or forms until $Q$ equals the same $K_{sp}$ again. What changes is the solubility: how much solid a liter of that solution can dissolve.

"Acid dissolves things" is not a rule. Acid raises the solubility of a solid whose anion H₃O⁺ reacts with, and leaves the others essentially alone.

**Beyond the exam:** how much more soluble a salt becomes at a given pH can be calculated, by combining $K_{sp}$ with the $K_a$ of the anion's conjugate acid. The exam does not ask for that. It asks which way the solubility moves, and why.

<Trap>

Six mistakes cost points on this topic:

- **Judging by the acid and not by the anion.** The question is whether the solid's anion is a base. If it is the conjugate base of a strong acid, acid changes essentially nothing.
- **Saying that $K_{sp}$ changes.** The pH changes a concentration and so changes $Q$. $K_{sp}$ is fixed at a given temperature.
- **Stopping at "the anion is a base".** A full answer also says that H₃O⁺ takes the anion away and that more solid dissolves to restore the equilibrium.
- **Having H₃O⁺ react with the metal ion.** Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺ and Ni²⁺ are not bases. The anion is what reacts.
- **Getting a hydroxide backward.** A higher pH means more OH⁻, a common ion, so less of a hydroxide dissolves.
- **Calculating a solubility from a pH.** The question asks for a direction and a reason.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A saturated solution with solid in it is an equilibrium, and anything that takes one of its ions away makes more solid dissolve.
- A slightly soluble salt whose anion is a weak base, the conjugate base of a weak acid, is more soluble in acid than in water, because H₃O⁺ turns the anion into its conjugate acid.
- A salt whose anion is the conjugate base of a strong acid, such as Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ or NO₃⁻, is essentially no more soluble in acid.
- A slightly soluble hydroxide such as Ni(OH)₂ or Co(OH)₂ is more soluble at a lower pH and less soluble at a higher pH, where OH⁻ acts as a common ion.
- A change in pH can change $Q$. It never changes $K_{sp}$, which is fixed at a given temperature.
- A full explanation names the ion that is a base, says what H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ does to its concentration, and says which way the dissolving equilibrium shifts.

</KeyPoints>
