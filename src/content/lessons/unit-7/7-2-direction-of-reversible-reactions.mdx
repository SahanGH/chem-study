A school library lends books all day and takes books back all day. Whether its shelves are filling or emptying comes down to one comparison: which is faster, the lending or the returning? A reversible reaction is run by the same comparison.

## Two rates, one net change

At every moment a reversible reaction has two rates: the rate of the forward reaction and the rate of the reverse reaction. Both are rates of reaction in M/s, as topic 5.1 defined them.

Watching a concentration change does not show either rate by itself. What it shows is the difference between them, the **net** change.

That leaves three cases:

| Comparison | What you observe |
|---|---|
| Forward rate greater than reverse rate | A net conversion of reactants to products: reactant concentrations fall, product concentrations rise |
| Reverse rate greater than forward rate | A net conversion of products to reactants: product concentrations fall, reactant concentrations rise |
| The two rates equal | No net change: equilibrium |

Put numbers on it. Say the forward reaction is running at 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ M/s and the reverse reaction at 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ M/s. The mixture moves toward products at the difference, 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ M/s. The slower reaction has not switched off. It is undoing part of what the faster one does.

<KeyIdea>

Compare the two rates. The larger one sets the direction of the net change. When they are equal there is no net change, and that is equilibrium.

</KeyIdea>

A sealed jar holding liquid water and water vapor has been sitting at equilibrium on a warm windowsill. It is moved into a refrigerator. A minute later, for every 100 water molecules that leave the liquid, about 140 return to it. What is happening in the jar?

- The amount of liquid is shrinking, because water molecules are still leaving its surface.
- Nothing is changing, because both processes are still going on.
- The amount of liquid is growing, because condensation is outpacing evaporation.
- Evaporation has stopped, because the jar is now cold.

<div slot="why">

Here the reverse process, condensation, is the faster one, so there is a net conversion of vapor to liquid, and droplets form on the inside of the glass. Evaporation has not stopped: 100 molecules still leave for every 140 that return.

The liquid will keep gaining until the vapor has thinned enough for the return rate to fall to the leaving rate. Then the jar is at equilibrium again.

</div>
</Check>

## Why the two rates close in on each other

Unit 5 showed that a reaction goes faster when the particles that have to meet are more concentrated (topics 5.2 and 5.5). So the forward rate depends on the concentrations of the reactants, and the reverse rate depends on the concentrations of the products.

Now follow a mixture that starts with reactants only. The forward rate starts at its highest. The reverse rate starts at zero, because there are no products yet to react.

A net conversion to products follows, and it changes both rates. The forward reaction is using up its own reactants, so it slows. It is also making the reverse reaction's reactants, so the reverse reaction speeds up.

The faster reaction always wears down its own supply and builds up its rival's. That is why the gap between the rates can only shrink, and why a closed mixture left alone at a constant temperature always moves toward equal rates, never away from them. (The constant temperature matters. Cooling is what pushed the jar's two rates apart in the Check above.)

The library works the same way. The more books sit on its shelves, the more are borrowed each day, and the more books are out, the more come back.

<Worked title="Which way is the mixture moving?">

Chlorine monofluoride and fluorine combine reversibly to make chlorine trifluoride:

$$
\ce{ClF(g) + F2(g) <=> ClF3(g)}
$$

A chemist puts all three gases into a sealed nickel vessel (nickel stands up to fluorine), held at a certain temperature. At that moment the forward reaction is running at 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ M/s and the reverse reaction at 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ M/s. (The rates are made up for this example.) Which way does the mixture move, how fast, and where does it end?

1. **Compare the rates.** The reverse rate, 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ M/s, is the larger.

2. **Name the net change.** There is a net conversion of product to reactants: [ClF₃] is falling, and [ClF] and [F₂] are rising.

3. **Find how fast.** Subtract the smaller rate from the larger.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &4.5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s} \\
   &\quad - 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s} \\
   &= 3.3 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M/s}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Every coefficient in the equation is 1, so at this moment [ClF₃] is falling by 3.3 × 10⁻⁴ M each second, and [ClF] and [F₂] are each rising by the same amount.

4. **Say what happens to the two rates next.** The reverse reaction is using up ClF₃, so it slows. The forward reaction is being fed ClF and F₂, so it speeds up.

5. **Say where it ends.** The two rates meet somewhere between 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ M/s and 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ M/s. From then on the concentrations are constant, with both reactions still running.

</Worked>

Nitrogen monoxide and bromine react reversibly in a sealed flask at constant temperature:

$$
\ce{2NO(g) + Br2(g) <=> 2NOBr(g)}
$$

At one moment the forward reaction is running at 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s and the reverse reaction at 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s. Nothing is added or removed. Which pair of rates could the flask have once it reaches equilibrium?

- Forward 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s, reverse 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s
- Forward 3.5 × 10⁻⁵ M/s, reverse 3.5 × 10⁻⁵ M/s
- Forward 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s, reverse 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s
- Forward 0 M/s, reverse 0 M/s

<div slot="why">

The forward rate is the larger, so NO and Br₂ are being used up and NOBr is building up. That makes the forward rate fall from 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s and the reverse rate rise from 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s, so they must meet somewhere between those two values.

A forward rate of 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s with a reverse rate of 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s lies in between, but the two are not equal. That mixture is still moving toward products, so it is not at equilibrium yet.

Both rates move, so they cannot meet at 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ M/s, where the forward rate started. And the rates at equilibrium are equal but not zero.

</div>
</Check>

## No molecule knows the direction

Look at the particles and there is no direction to see. Once reactants and products are both present, some reactant particles are colliding and turning into products at any moment, and some product particles are turning back. They do this before equilibrium, at equilibrium and after anything disturbs the mixture.

So "the reaction is going forward" is a statement about a count: more forward events than reverse events each second. The direction belongs to the whole mixture, not to any particle in it.

That one picture of the particles explains everything the whole sample does. Concentrations drift when the two counts differ. They hold steady when the counts match. And a reaction at equilibrium has not stopped, because the events never stop.

For a mixture that starts with some of everything, you need to know which rate is larger. Topic 7.7 gives a quick way to tell from the concentrations, without measuring either rate.

Iodine and bromine react reversibly:

$$
\ce{I2(g) + Br2(g) <=> 2IBr(g)}
$$

A hot sealed bulb is filled with pure IBr gas and nothing else. Which describes what follows?

- Nothing happens, because the bulb holds neither of the reactants on the left of the equation.
- The forward rate starts high and falls until it reaches zero.
- Both rates start out equal, because only one substance is present.
- The reverse rate falls and the forward rate rises from zero until they are equal.

<div slot="why">

With only IBr present, the reverse reaction (IBr breaking down into I₂ and Br₂) starts at its fastest. The forward reaction cannot run at all, because there is no I₂ or Br₂ yet. So there is a net conversion of product to reactants.

As IBr is used up the reverse rate falls. As I₂ and Br₂ build up the forward rate rises from zero. They meet, and the bulb is at equilibrium.

"Reactants" and "products" only name the two sides of the equation as written. A reaction can start from either side.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Thinking "going forward" means the reverse reaction is switched off.** Once both sides are present, both reactions are running. The direction is the net of the two.
- **Thinking both rates fall to zero at equilibrium.** They become equal, and neither is zero.
- **Thinking the faster reaction stays ahead.** It uses up what feeds it and builds up what feeds the other, so the rates close in on each other.
- **Judging the direction from the amounts.** A mixture that is mostly products can still be moving toward products. What decides is which rate is larger.
- **Thinking a reaction can only start from the left-hand side.** Start with products alone and the reverse reaction runs first.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A reversible reaction always has a forward rate and a reverse rate, and what you observe is the net of the two.
- When the forward rate is greater, there is a net conversion of reactants to products. When the reverse rate is greater, there is a net conversion of products to reactants.
- When the two rates are equal there is no net change, and the system is at equilibrium with both reactions still running.
- The faster reaction uses up its own reactants and supplies the other's, so in a closed mixture at a constant temperature the two rates always move toward each other.

</KeyPoints>
