A sealed glass tube holds a pale brown gas. Stand it in a beaker of ice water and the brown fades almost away. Move it to hot water and it turns dark brown. Nothing went in and nothing came out. The tube holds an equilibrium, and each time you changed the temperature you pushed it somewhere new.

## A stress, and a shift that partly undoes it

The tube holds two gases at equilibrium:

$$
\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}
$$

N₂O₄ is colorless and NO₂ is brown. So the depth of the brown shows how much NO₂ the tube holds.

Left alone, the mixture stays as it is. A **stress** is a change made from outside that knocks a system out of equilibrium: a substance added or removed, the volume changed, a solution diluted, the temperature changed.

**Le Châtelier's principle** says what happens next: the system responds with a net reaction in the direction that partly undoes the change, and it settles at a new equilibrium.

There are two phrases to learn for the direction. The equilibrium **shifts toward products**, or "to the right", when the forward reaction outruns the reverse for a while. It **shifts toward reactants**, or "to the left", when the reverse outruns the forward.

Here is a first example. Keep the mixture in a rigid container, so that its volume cannot change. Hold the temperature steady, and inject extra NO₂.

The stress is too much NO₂. The response uses some of it up: for a while, NO₂ turns into N₂O₄ faster than N₂O₄ breaks apart.

The reason lies in the two rates. More NO₂ molecules means more collisions between them (topic 5.5), so the reverse rate jumps above the forward rate. Then there is a net conversion toward reactants (topic 7.2), until the two rates are equal again.

Now look at the word "partly". In the figure, the NO₂ concentration ends at 0.210 M. That is below the 0.265 M it was pushed to, but still above the 0.175 M it started from. The shift used up some of the added NO₂, not all of it. And the N₂O₄ ended higher than it started. The new equilibrium is a different mixture from the old one.

<KeyIdea>

A shift opposes the change, but it never cancels it. The system does not go back to where it was. It settles at a new equilibrium.

</KeyIdea>

## Adding or removing a substance

The injected NO₂ is one case of a rule. Add a substance that is in the equation, and the equilibrium shifts away from it, using some of it up. Remove one, and the equilibrium shifts toward it, replacing some of it.

- Adding a reactant or removing a product shifts the equilibrium toward products.
- Adding a product or removing a reactant shifts it toward reactants.

Here is a second system, this time in solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + SCN^-(aq) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{FeSCN^2+(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Fe³⁺ is pale yellow, SCN⁻ (the thiocyanate ion) is colorless, and FeSCN²⁺ is deep red. So the mixture is orange-red, and its redness shows how much product there is.

Stir in a few crystals of potassium thiocyanate, KSCN. That adds SCN⁻, a reactant. (The K⁺ ions take no part.) The equilibrium shifts toward products, and the solution turns a deeper red.

Add a few drops of silver nitrate solution instead. Ag⁺ ions join with SCN⁻ ions to make a white solid, AgSCN, which drops out of the solution. A solid that forms out of a solution is called a **precipitate** (topic 4.7). SCN⁻ has been removed, so the equilibrium shifts toward reactants to replace some of it: FeSCN²⁺ breaks up, and the red fades.

This is how a substance is usually "removed" in the lab. You do not pick it out. You add something that reacts with it.

Now a case that shifts nothing. Take water that has dissolved all the lead(II) iodide it can, with yellow solid lying on the bottom:

$$
\ce{PbI2(s) <=> Pb^2+(aq) + 2I^-(aq)}
$$

Drop in another spoonful of the solid and nothing changes. The concentrations of the ions stay where they were. Topic 7.3 gave the reason: the concentration of a pure solid does not depend on how much of it there is, so the solid is not in the $K$ expression, and adding more of it is not a stress. The same holds for a pure liquid. (Topic 7.11 comes back to this equilibrium.)

## Squeezing or expanding a gas mixture

Put the N₂O₄ and NO₂ mixture in a syringe with its tip sealed. Push the plunger in, keeping the temperature the same. The same molecules are now in a smaller volume, so every gas is more concentrated and the pressure is higher.

The system can ease that by making fewer gas particles. So the equilibrium shifts toward the side of the equation with **fewer moles of gas**. Here two NO₂ become one N₂O₄, so the shift is toward N₂O₄. Pull the plunger out and it goes the other way, toward the side with more moles of gas.

Look through the side of the syringe while you push. At the moment of the push the brown darkens, because the same NO₂ is packed into less space. Then it fades a little, as NO₂ pairs up into N₂O₄. It does not fade back to the first shade: the shift only partly undoes the squeeze. (Topic 7.10 puts numbers on this.)

To count, add up the coefficients of the gases on each side, and only the gases. Solids and liquids take up almost no room, and they are not counted. If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, a change of volume shifts nothing.

Be careful with the word "pressure". It is the change of volume that matters. Pump argon, which reacts with nothing, into a rigid container of the mixture. The total pressure rises. But the volume is the same, so the concentration and the partial pressure (topic 3.4) of each reacting gas are what they were. No shift.

## Diluting a solution

Adding water to a solution does to dissolved particles what pulling the plunger out does to a gas: it spreads them all out. The equilibrium shifts toward the side with **more dissolved particles**.

In the iron and thiocyanate equilibrium, the left side has two dissolved particles and the right side has one. So dilution shifts it toward reactants: some FeSCN²⁺ breaks up. The red gets paler for two reasons at once. The same ions are spread through more water, and there are now fewer FeSCN²⁺ ions.

Count dissolved particles only. The water is the solvent, and like a pure liquid it is left out of $K$ (topic 7.3), so it is not counted. If both sides have the same number of dissolved particles, dilution shifts nothing.

## Heating and cooling

Two words first. A reaction that releases energy to its surroundings as heat is **exothermic**. A reaction that takes energy in is **endothermic**. (Topic 5.6 used these words for a single step, and Unit 6 treats them in full.) Whatever the forward reaction does, the reverse does the opposite.

Turning N₂O₄ into 2 NO₂ breaks the bond between the two nitrogen atoms, and breaking a bond takes energy. So the forward reaction is endothermic (about 57 kJ for each mole of N₂O₄), and the reverse is exothermic.

The trick is to write the energy into the equation as if it were a substance:

$$
\text{energy} + \ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}
$$

Heating the tube adds energy, so the equilibrium shifts in the direction that uses energy up, the endothermic direction: toward NO₂. The gas darkens. Cooling removes energy, so the equilibrium shifts in the exothermic direction: toward N₂O₄. The gas pales. That is the tube in the opening.

For an exothermic forward reaction, the energy is written on the product side, and heating shifts the equilibrium toward reactants.

One thing makes temperature different from every other stress. After heating, the tube holds more NO₂ and less N₂O₄ than the old value of $K$ allows, and it stays that way. **$K$ itself has changed.**

When the forward reaction is endothermic, $K$ rises as the temperature rises. When it is exothermic, $K$ falls as the temperature rises. Adding or removing a substance, changing the volume and diluting all leave $K$ exactly as it was: the mixture changes until it fits the same $K$ again.

<KeyIdea>

Only a change of temperature changes $K$. After every other stress the concentrations are new, but $K$ is the same number as before.

</KeyIdea>

## What shifts an equilibrium, and what does not

Three changes look like stresses and shift nothing:

1. **More of a pure solid or pure liquid.** Its concentration does not change, so nothing in the $K$ expression changes. Adding more solvent to a solution is different: that is dilution, and it lowers the concentration of everything dissolved.

2. **An unreactive gas added at constant volume.** The reacting gases have the same concentrations and partial pressures as before.

3. **A catalyst.** Topic 5.11 showed that a catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward and the reverse reaction by the same amount, so it speeds both up by the same factor. Two rates that were equal are still equal: no shift. A catalyst gets a mixture to equilibrium sooner. It does not change where equilibrium is.

Now the whole lesson fits in two lists.

**These shift an equilibrium:**

- Adding a substance that is in the equation (the shift uses some of it up), or removing one (the shift replaces some of it).
- Squeezing a gas mixture (toward the side with fewer moles of gas) or expanding it (toward the side with more), when the two sides differ.
- Diluting a solution (toward the side with more dissolved particles), when the two sides differ.
- Heating (in the endothermic direction) or cooling (in the exothermic direction).

**These do not:**

- Adding more of a pure solid or pure liquid. (More solvent added to a solution is not this case: it is dilution, in the first list.)
- Adding an unreactive gas at constant volume.
- Adding a catalyst.

Of everything in the two lists, only heating and cooling give a new $K$.

## Reading a shift from what you can measure

Nobody sees a molecule. A shift is seen through something measured: a color, a temperature, or a pH. The pH of a solution is a measure of its concentration of H⁺ ions, and the higher that concentration, the lower the pH (Unit 8).

**Color.** A deeper color means a higher concentration of the colored substance, as with brown NO₂ and red FeSCN²⁺, when the light passes through the same thickness of gas or solution.

**Temperature.** A shift is a net reaction, and a net reaction releases or absorbs energy. In an insulated container, a shift in the exothermic direction warms the mixture a little, and a shift in the endothermic direction cools it.

Take the pink and blue cobalt equilibrium from the Check on heating and cooling. Its forward reaction, toward blue, is endothermic. In an insulated container, any shift toward blue takes in energy and cools the solution, and any shift toward pink releases energy and warms it. With concentrated solutions like that one, the change can be read on a thermometer. With very dilute solutions, such as the iron thiocyanate mixture, so little reacts that the change is far too small to see.

That warming or cooling is the shift's own effect. Anything else that gives out or takes in heat, such as a solid dissolving or two solutions mixing, adds to it.

**pH.** A higher concentration of H⁺ means a lower pH. So, taken by itself, a shift that makes H⁺ lowers the pH, and a shift that uses H⁺ up raises it.

That is the shift's own effect, and it is the whole story only when the stress did nothing to the concentration of H⁺ directly. If the stress itself added or removed H⁺, the shift undoes only part of that. So the pH still ends on the side the stress pushed it: lower than at the start after H⁺ is added, higher after H⁺ is removed.

Dilution is the awkward case. Adding water lowers the concentration of H⁺ directly, and it can also cause a shift. If that shift uses H⁺ up, both effects lower the concentration, and the pH ends higher. If the shift makes H⁺, the two effects pull against each other, and the direction of the shift cannot tell you which one wins: that takes a calculation with $K$. In every case, compare the concentration of H⁺ at the end with the concentration at the start.

<Worked title="A yellow and orange equilibrium">

A solution holds two chromium ions at equilibrium:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2CrO4^2-(aq) + 2H^+(aq) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{Cr2O7^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The chromate ion, CrO₄²⁻, is yellow, and the dichromate ion, Cr₂O₇²⁻, is orange. The solution starts yellow-orange. Predict the color and the pH after each of three changes, each made to a fresh sample.

1. **A few drops of sulfuric acid.** The acid adds H⁺, a reactant. The equilibrium shifts toward products: more Cr₂O₇²⁻, so the solution turns more orange. The shift uses up part of the added H⁺, not all of it, so the concentration of H⁺ is higher than before the acid went in. The pH is lower than at the start.

2. **A few drops of sodium hydroxide solution.** The OH⁻ ions react with H⁺ to make water, so a reactant is removed. The equilibrium shifts toward reactants: Cr₂O₇²⁻ turns back into CrO₄²⁻, and the solution turns more yellow. The shift releases some H⁺, but it replaces only part of what the base took, so the concentration of H⁺ is lower than before. The pH is higher than at the start.

3. **A few drops of barium nitrate solution.** Ba²⁺ ions join with CrO₄²⁻ ions to make a pale yellow precipitate, BaCrO₄. (Barium dichromate stays dissolved.) A reactant is removed, so the equilibrium shifts toward reactants: the orange of the solution fades while the yellow solid settles. Now look at the equation run backward. Each Cr₂O₇²⁻ that reacts releases 2 H⁺, and this time nothing has taken H⁺ away. A few drops hardly change the volume, so the concentration of H⁺ rises, and the pH falls.

4. **Put it together.** Each row of the table is one of the three changes: the acid, the base and the barium nitrate. Its columns give the side the equilibrium shifts toward, what happens to the color of the solution, and how the pH compares with the start.

| Added | Shift toward | Color | pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acid | Products | More orange | Lower |
| Base | Reactants | More yellow | Higher |
| Barium nitrate | Reactants | Orange fades | Lower |

The last two rows shift the same way and move the pH in opposite directions, because one removes H⁺ directly and the other removes chromate. Find what the added substance does to a species in the equation first. Then apply the principle.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Expecting the shift to cancel the change.** It only partly undoes it. After NO₂ is added, there is more NO₂ at the new equilibrium than at the old one.
- **Shifting an equilibrium by adding more solid.** A pure solid or liquid is not in the $K$ expression, so more of it changes nothing.
- **Counting solids and liquids when the volume changes.** Count moles of gas only.
- **Saying "higher pressure" without asking how.** Compression shifts an equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas. An unreactive gas pumped in at constant volume shifts nothing.
- **Giving a catalyst credit for more product.** It speeds both directions equally: the same equilibrium, reached sooner.
- **Working out a new $K$ after a substance is added or the volume is changed.** At a fixed temperature $K$ keeps its value. It is the mixture that changes to fit it.
- **Reading "shifts toward products" as "goes to completion".** Reactants and products are both still there at the new equilibrium.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Le Châtelier's principle: a system at equilibrium that is disturbed shifts in the direction that partly undoes the disturbance, and reaches a new equilibrium.
- Adding a substance shifts the equilibrium away from it, and removing a substance shifts the equilibrium toward it.
- Compressing a gas mixture shifts it toward the side with fewer moles of gas, and expanding it shifts it toward the side with more. Diluting a solution shifts it toward the side with more dissolved particles.
- Raising the temperature shifts an equilibrium in the endothermic direction, and lowering it shifts it in the exothermic direction. Temperature is the only stress that changes $K$.
- A catalyst, an unreactive gas added at constant volume and more of a pure solid or liquid cause no shift. More solvent added to a solution is dilution, which can shift it.
- A shift shows up in what can be measured: a color deepens or fades, an insulated mixture warms or cools, and the pH ends lower when the mixture ends with a higher concentration of H⁺ than it started with, and higher when it ends with a lower one.

</KeyPoints>
