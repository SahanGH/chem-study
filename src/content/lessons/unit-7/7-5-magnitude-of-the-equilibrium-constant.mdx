Breathe out. Part of that breath is carbon dioxide. On paper, carbon dioxide can fall apart into carbon monoxide and oxygen. Does any of that carbon dioxide do it? And if some does, how much? The answer is packed into one number: the equilibrium constant for that reaction at 25 °C is about 10⁻⁹⁰. This lesson is about what a number like that tells you.

## What the size of K tells you

$K$ is a fraction with the products on top and the reactants on the bottom, each raised to its coefficient (topic 7.3). So its size tells you which side has the upper hand at equilibrium.

**A very small $K$.** The top of the fraction is tiny beside the bottom. At equilibrium the mixture is almost all reactants, and the reaction has barely gone at all. The reaction from the opening is one of these:

$$
\ce{2CO2(g) <=> 2CO(g) + O2(g)}
$$

At 25 °C, $K_p$ is about 10⁻⁹⁰. So how much of the carbon dioxide falls apart? Picture a liter of CO₂ at 1 atm and 25 °C, at equilibrium. It would hold, on average, about 3 × 10⁻⁸ molecules of CO. You would need tens of millions of liters of the gas to expect to find a single CO molecule.

**A very large $K$.** The top of the fraction is huge beside the bottom. At equilibrium almost everything is products, and the reaction has gone essentially to completion: nearly every particle that could react has reacted. Write the same reaction the other way round:

$$
\ce{2CO(g) + O2(g) <=> 2CO2(g)}
$$

Now $K_p$ is about 10⁹⁰ at 25 °C. At equilibrium, carbon monoxide and oxygen are, for every practical purpose, all carbon dioxide, with whichever reactant was in excess left over. Topic 7.6 shows why the two constants are exact reciprocals.

**A $K$ near 1.** Neither side wins. At equilibrium there are plenty of reactants and plenty of products, in amounts that are easy to compare. Exactly how much of each depends on the exponents in the $K$ expression and on the starting mixture.

Where does "very large" begin? As a rule of thumb, a $K$ above about 10³ means mostly products, a $K$ below about 10⁻³ means mostly reactants, and a $K$ between them means a mixture with noticeable amounts of both. Those two numbers are only a guide. There is no sharp line, and the exam does not ask for one. What the exam asks for is the link: a very large $K$ means essentially all products, and a very small $K$ means essentially all reactants.

However large or small $K$ is, it is never infinite and never zero. At equilibrium every substance in the equation is present (topic 7.1), even if only as a trace.

<KeyIdea>

$K$ is products over reactants. A very large $K$ means the equilibrium mixture is almost all products. A very small $K$ means it is almost all reactants. A $K$ near 1 means plenty of both.

</KeyIdea>

In water, a glucose molecule switches back and forth between two ring shapes, called α and β, that differ in which way one –OH group points: α-glucose(aq) ⇌ β-glucose(aq). At room temperature $K$ = 1.8. What does a glucose solution hold at equilibrium?

- Almost all of the glucose in the β form
- Exactly equal amounts of the two forms, because $K$ is close to 1
- A large share in each form, with more β than α
- Almost all of the glucose in the α form

<div slot="why">

$K$ is neither very large nor very small, so neither form wins outright. The expression is

$$
K = \frac{[\beta\text{-glucose}]}{[\alpha\text{-glucose}]} = 1.8
$$

So there are 1.8 molecules of β for each molecule of α. The fraction of the glucose in the β form is

$$
\frac{1.8}{1.8 + 1} = 0.64
$$

That is about 64 percent β and 36 percent α. The amounts would be exactly equal only if $K$ were exactly 1 (for a reaction like this one, where one particle becomes one particle).

</div>
</Check>

## A very large K: treat the reaction as complete

This is where points are lost on the exam. When $K$ is enormous, do not set the problem up as an equilibrium problem with an unknown. The reaction goes essentially all the way, so work it as you would any reaction that goes to completion. Find the **limiting reactant**, which is the reactant that runs out first. Use it up, and see what is left.

The equilibrium mixture is the products, plus whatever reactant was in excess, plus a trace of the limiting reactant too small to matter in the arithmetic.

The mirror image holds for a very small $K$. The reactants are left almost untouched, so their concentrations at equilibrium are almost exactly the starting ones. Topic 7.7 turns that into a shortcut.

<Worked title="A reaction that goes essentially all the way">

A test chamber for a car's exhaust catalyst is filled with carbon monoxide and oxygen:

$$
\ce{2CO(g) + O2(g) <=> 2CO2(g)}
$$

At the chamber's temperature, $K_c$ = 4 × 10²⁰. The chamber is hot, and $K$ changes with temperature, so this value is far smaller than it would be at 25 °C, and it is still enormous. The chamber starts with [CO] = 0.060 M and [O₂] = 0.040 M, and no CO₂. Find the concentrations at equilibrium.

1. **Read the size of $K$.** 4 × 10²⁰ is enormous, so the reaction goes essentially to completion. Treat it as complete.

2. **Find the limiting reactant.** The equation uses 2 CO for each O₂, so 0.060 M of CO needs half as much O₂.

   $$
   \frac{0.060\ \text{M}}{2} = 0.030\ \text{M}
   $$

   There is 0.040 M of O₂, more than enough. CO runs out first.

3. **Use it up.** A before-and-after table keeps the amounts straight, with every value in M:

   | | CO | O₂ | CO₂ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Before | 0.060 | 0.040 | 0 |
   | Change | −0.060 | −0.030 | +0.060 |
   | After | about 0 | 0.010 | 0.060 |

   So at equilibrium [CO₂] = 0.060 M and [O₂] = 0.010 M.

4. **How close to zero is the CO?** It cannot be exactly zero, because every substance is present at equilibrium. Put the two concentrations just found into the $K$ expression, and solve for the one that is left.

   $$
   K_c = \frac{[\ce{CO2}]^2}{[\ce{CO}]^2[\ce{O2}]}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [\ce{CO}]^2 &= \frac{[\ce{CO2}]^2}{K_c[\ce{O2}]} \\
   &= \frac{(0.060)^2}{(4 \times 10^{20})(0.010)} \\
   &= 9 \times 10^{-22}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   [\ce{CO}] = 3 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{M}
   $$

   The answer has one significant figure, as $K$ has.

5. **Does it make sense?** 3 × 10⁻¹¹ M is what is left of 0.060 M.

   $$
   \frac{0.060}{3 \times 10^{-11}} = 2 \times 10^{9}
   $$

   That is about one CO molecule left in every two billion. Calling the reaction complete changed no digit of the other two answers.

</Worked>

A spark sets off a mixture of methane and oxygen in a sealed steel vessel:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) <=>} \\
&\quad \ce{CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
\end{aligned}
$$

For this reaction $K_c$ is about 10¹⁴⁰ at 25 °C, and it is still enormous in the hot vessel. Before the spark, [CH₄] = 0.012 M and [O₂] = 0.040 M. What is [O₂] at equilibrium?

<div slot="why">

$K_c$ is enormous, so treat the reaction as complete. Each CH₄ uses two O₂, so 0.012 M of CH₄ needs

$$
2 \times 0.012\ \text{M} = 0.024\ \text{M}
$$

of O₂. There is 0.040 M, so methane is the limiting reactant. The oxygen left over is

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{O2}] &= 0.040 - 0.024 \\
&= 0.016\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mixture also holds 0.012 M of CO₂ and 0.024 M of H₂O, and a trace of methane far too small to measure.

If you got 0.028 M, you forgot that each CH₄ uses two O₂.

## How far, not how fast

$K$ describes the mixture at equilibrium. It says nothing about how long the mixture takes to get there. Sulfur dioxide and oxygen show it:

$$
\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}
$$

At 25 °C, $K_p$ is about 10²⁵. By that number, a mixture of SO₂ and oxygen should turn almost completely into SO₃. It does not. A dry mixture of the two gases, sealed in a bottle at room temperature, can sit for years with almost no SO₃ forming. The reaction has a high activation energy (topic 5.5), so at room temperature very few collisions have enough energy to react.

The factories that make sulfuric acid run this step hot and over a catalyst (topic 5.11), to make it go at a useful rate. The catalyst changes only how fast equilibrium is reached. The heat speeds the reaction up as well, but it also does something the catalyst does not: it makes this $K$ smaller. (Topic 7.9 explains why temperature changes $K$.) $K$ is still well above 1 at the plant's temperature, and the plant accepts a smaller $K$ for the sake of speed.

The size of $K$ told you only where the reaction is headed. So there are two separate questions, with two separate answers. **How far** is answered by $K$, in this unit. **How fast** is answered by the rate law and the activation energy, in Unit 5. A large $K$ does not mean a fast reaction, and a small $K$ does not mean a slow one.

Ethene and hydrogen are sealed in a glass bulb at 25 °C. They can react to make ethane:

$$
\ce{C2H4(g) + H2(g) <=> C2H6(g)}
$$

For this reaction, $K$ is greater than 10¹⁷ at 25 °C. A year later the bulb still holds ethene and hydrogen, and no ethane can be detected. Which explanation fits?

- The mixture is at equilibrium, and equilibrium lies on the side of ethene and hydrogen.
- The mixture is far from equilibrium, but the reaction is too slow to see.
- A $K$ that large means a fast reaction, so the value of $K$ must be wrong.
- Ethane forms quickly and breaks down again just as quickly.

<div slot="why">

With $K$ above 10¹⁷, the equilibrium mixture would be almost all ethane. So a bulb with no ethane in it is nowhere near equilibrium.

Nothing is wrong with $K$. It says how far the reaction can go, not how fast it goes. This reaction has a high activation energy: without a catalyst, hardly any collisions at 25 °C have enough energy. Topic 5.11 showed the same two gases reacting quickly on platinum. The platinum changes how fast the mixture gets to equilibrium. It does not change where equilibrium lies.

The last option describes a mixture at equilibrium, which would have to be nearly all ethane.

</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Five mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Reading a large $K$ as a fast reaction.** $K$ says how far, never how fast. The rate depends on the activation energy and the rate law.
- **Setting up a table with an unknown (the method of topic 7.7) when $K$ is enormous.** Treat the reaction as complete: find the limiting reactant and use it up.
- **Saying a reactant is completely gone, or that no product forms at all.** At equilibrium every substance in the equation is present. With a very large or a very small $K$, one of them is only a trace.
- **Treating 10³ and 10⁻³ as sharp lines.** They are a rough guide. The AP course gives no cutoff.
- **Reading a negative exponent as a negative number.** 10⁻⁹⁰ is a tiny positive number. $K$ is never negative.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- $K$ is a ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium, so its size tells you which side the equilibrium mixture favors.
- A very large $K$ means the reaction goes essentially to completion, and the equilibrium mixture is almost all products.
- A very small $K$ means the reaction barely goes, and the equilibrium mixture is almost all reactants.
- A $K$ near 1 means noticeable amounts of both reactants and products at equilibrium.
- When $K$ is very large, find the equilibrium amounts by treating the reaction as complete: find the limiting reactant, use it up, and see what is left.
- $K$ says how far a reaction goes and nothing about how fast. How fast is the business of the activation energy and the rate law.

</KeyPoints>
