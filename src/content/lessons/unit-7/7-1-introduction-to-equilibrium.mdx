Fill a water bottle halfway, screw the cap on tight and leave it on a shelf. A year later the water level has not moved. Pour the same water into an open glass and it is gone in a few weeks. Yet the water in the bottle never stopped evaporating. Something else is happening in there that exactly cancels it.

## Processes that run both ways

A **reversible process** is one that can go in either direction. Water evaporates, and water vapor condenses. Write the two together with a double arrow, as topic 5.9 did for a reversible step:

$$
\ce{H2O(l) <=> H2O(g)}
$$

Read from left to right, this is the **forward** process: liquid becoming vapor. Read from right to left, it is the **reverse** process: vapor becoming liquid. By convention, the substances on the left are called the reactants and those on the right the products, even though each side is being made from the other.

Reversible processes are everywhere:

- A liquid evaporates, and its vapor condenses. That is the water in the bottle.
- A gas is taken up by a liquid, and comes back out. The CO₂ in a sealed bottle of sparkling water does both:

  $$
  \ce{CO2(g) <=> CO2(aq)}
  $$

- A salt dissolves, and comes back out of solution as a solid, which is called **precipitating** (topic 4.7). Picture salt crystals lying at the bottom of water that can hold no more salt:

  $$
  \ce{NaCl(s) <=> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}
  $$

- Chemical reactions run both ways too: a bond that can form can also break. Two large families of reversible reactions come later in the course. In one, an acid passes a proton to another particle and gets it back (Unit 8). In the other, one particle passes electrons to another (Units 4 and 9).

This lesson follows one chemical example from start to finish. Brown nitrogen dioxide gas, NO₂, is sealed in a quartz tube and kept in a furnace at a constant temperature above 500 °C. That hot, it breaks down into two colorless gases, nitrogen monoxide and oxygen, and those two gases join to make it again:

$$
\ce{2NO2(g) <=> 2NO(g) + O2(g)}
$$

The tube starts with 0.0500 M NO₂ and nothing else. (The concentrations are made up for this lesson. The behavior is real.) You have met one reversible reaction already: the fast step $\ce{2NO <=> N2O2}$ in topic 5.9.

## What equilibrium looks like

Watch the sealed bottle with instruments instead of eyes. For a while after the cap goes on, the pressure of water vapor in the space above the liquid climbs. Then it stops. At 25 °C it settles at 23.8 torr and stays there. (Topic 3.2 called this the vapor pressure.) The water level, the pressure and the temperature all hold steady for as long as the bottle is left alone.

A system in this state is at **equilibrium**: nothing measurable changes any longer, even though reactants and products are both still there and the process can still run in both directions. Three things mark it:

1. Nothing you can observe is changing. No property you can measure drifts.
2. Reactants and products are both present. There is still liquid, and there is vapor.
3. The concentration or partial pressure of every substance is constant.

One condition makes this possible: nothing that takes part in the process can get in or out. The bottle's cap keeps the water vapor in. The open glass never reaches equilibrium, because its vapor drifts away into the room.

Now the tube. At first its brown color fades, as NO₂ turns into colorless NO and O₂. Then the fading stops. The tube stays brown, paler than at the start, for as long as the furnace holds its temperature. All three gases are in it: 0.0200 M NO₂, 0.0300 M NO and 0.0150 M O₂. The color is a sign you can see of the NO₂ concentration, and it has stopped changing.

<div slot="why">

The pressure has stopped changing, so there is no more net change: the mixture is at equilibrium.

It cannot be that all the NOCl is gone. Every 2 molecules of NOCl become 3 molecules of gas, so complete breakdown would raise the pressure to

$$
1.20\ \text{atm} \times \frac{3}{2} = 1.80\ \text{atm}
$$

It stopped at 1.52 atm, so NOCl is still there beside its products. (The rise of 0.32 atm is the Cl₂ formed, so 0.64 atm of NOCl has reacted and 0.56 atm is left.)

The amounts cannot all be equal either: the equation makes two NO for every Cl₂.

## Nothing has stopped

Shrink down to the size of a molecule and look inside the bottle. At the surface of the liquid, the fastest-moving molecules keep breaking free into the space above (topic 3.3). In the vapor, molecules keep striking the surface and sticking.

Go back to the moment the cap went on. Molecules leave the liquid at a rate set by the temperature and the size of the surface, and neither of those changes. But there is little vapor at first, so few molecules come back. So the vapor builds up. The more vapor there is, the more molecules return each second. The return rate climbs until it matches the leaving rate. From then on, for every molecule that leaves, another comes back, and the amounts stop changing.

  caption="Molecules leave the liquid at the same rate in both bottles. With four times as much vapor, four times as many return each second, and the two rates are equal. Only a few molecules are drawn."
/>

This is why chemists call it **dynamic equilibrium**: both processes are still running, as fast as each other. The same is true in the tube. NO₂ is still breaking down, and NO and O₂ are still joining to make NO₂, at the same rate. (Topic 5.9 said the same of its fast step.)

Be careful about what equal rates do not mean. The bottle holds far more water as liquid than as vapor. The tube holds 0.0200 M, 0.0300 M and 0.0150 M of its three gases: no two alike. What ends up matched is the two rates. How much of each substance is there when they match is a separate matter: it depends on the process, the temperature and how much was put in.

<KeyIdea>

Equilibrium is dynamic. The forward and reverse processes keep running at equal rates, so nothing you can measure changes. Equal rates, not equal amounts.

</KeyIdea>

If both had stopped, the shape could not have changed: equal rates are not zero rates. If the solution had not been saturated, the crystal would have lost mass. If ions had joined faster than they left, it would have gained mass.

## How equilibrium is reached

Follow the tube from the start, using what Unit 5 taught: a reaction goes faster when the particles that must meet are more concentrated (topics 5.2 and 5.5).

At the first moment the tube holds only NO₂. The forward reaction is as fast as it will ever be. The reverse reaction cannot run at all, because there is no NO or O₂ to react.

As NO₂ is used up, the forward reaction slows. As NO and O₂ build up, the reverse reaction gets going and speeds up. The gap between the two rates narrows, and the concentrations change more and more slowly. When the rates are equal, the concentrations stop changing.

<Figure
  src="/figures/7-1-concentration-time.svg"
  alt="Graph of concentration against time for a sealed tube that starts with only nitrogen dioxide at 0.0500 molar. The nitrogen dioxide curve falls and levels off at 0.0200 molar. The nitrogen monoxide curve rises from zero and levels off at 0.0300 molar. The oxygen curve rises from zero and levels off at 0.0150 molar. The nitrogen dioxide and nitrogen monoxide curves cross at 0.0250 molar, before either is flat. All three curves become flat at the same time, marked equilibrium reached."
  caption="The concentrations change quickly at first, then more slowly, then not at all. They level off at three different values, and none of them is zero."
/>

Notice four things. Each curve is steepest at the start and then flattens. All three go flat at the same moment. They flatten at different heights. And none of them ends at zero.

<Worked title="Reading a concentration–time graph">

Use the graph of the tube to answer three questions. When is the tube at equilibrium? What does it hold then? Did the reaction finish?

1. **Find where the curves go flat.** By the dashed line, no curve is rising or falling. Constant concentrations are the sign of equilibrium, so the tube is at equilibrium from that moment.

2. **Read the concentrations there.** NO₂ is at 0.0200 M, NO at 0.0300 M and O₂ at 0.0150 M.

3. **Check them against the equation.** NO₂ fell by

   $$
   0.0500 - 0.0200 = 0.0300\ \text{M}
   $$

   NO rose by 0.0300 M and O₂ by 0.0150 M. The changes are in the ratio 2 : 2 : 1. Those are the coefficients of the equation, as they must be:

   $$
   \ce{2NO2 <=> 2NO + O2}
   $$

4. **Ask whether the reaction finished.** If every NO₂ molecule had reacted, the tube would end with no NO₂, 0.0500 M NO and 0.0250 M O₂. It stopped well short of that. Only 0.0300 M of the 0.0500 M reacted:

   $$
   \frac{0.0300\ \text{M}}{0.0500\ \text{M}} = 0.600
   $$

   That is 60 percent.

5. **Say what is happening on the flat part.** NO₂ is still breaking down, and NO and O₂ are still recombining, at equal rates. That is why the remaining 0.0200 M of NO₂ never gets used up.

</Worked>

The rate constants decide where the mixture levels off. With the forward constant four times the reverse one (0.8 and 0.2), it ends with four times as much B as A, 0.80 M against 0.20 M, whichever side it starts from. The amounts end up equal, 0.50 M each, only when the two constants are equal. With either other pair the rates end equal and the amounts do not. Choose the equal constants and start from half of each, and nothing moves at all: that mixture is already at equilibrium.

## The same place from either side

Start the tube again, this time with no NO₂ at all. Put in 0.0500 M NO and 0.0250 M O₂, which contain exactly the atoms of 0.0500 M NO₂, and keep the tube in the same furnace at the same temperature.

Now only the reverse reaction can run at first. The colorless mixture turns brown as NO₂ forms. The browning slows, and then it stops, at the very same mixture as before: 0.0200 M NO₂, 0.0300 M NO and 0.0150 M O₂.

An equilibrium mixture does not remember which side it started from. With the same atoms in the same volume at the same temperature, the forward and reverse rates balance at one particular mixture, and the tube arrives there from either direction. (In the diagram above, choose "Only B" to see the same thing.)

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Thinking the reaction has stopped.** Both directions are still running, at equal rates. Only the net change is zero.
- **Thinking the amounts are equal.** Equal rates, not equal amounts. The tube levels off at 0.0200 M, 0.0300 M and 0.0150 M.
- **Looking for where the concentration curves cross.** In the tube's graph the NO₂ and NO curves cross at 0.0250 M while both are still changing. Equilibrium is where every curve goes flat.
- **Thinking the reactants are used up.** Reactants and products are both present at equilibrium. In the tube, 0.0200 M of NO₂ is still there.
- **Calling the open glass an equilibrium.** It dries up because its vapor escapes. Equilibrium needs everything that takes part to stay in, which for a liquid and its vapor means a sealed container.
- **Thinking equilibrium can only be reached from the reactants.** The tube reaches the same mixture starting from NO and O₂.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Many physical and chemical processes are reversible, which means they can go in either direction. A double arrow in the equation shows it.
- At equilibrium nothing observable changes, reactants and products are both present, and every concentration or partial pressure is constant.
- Equilibrium is dynamic: the forward and reverse processes are still running, at equal rates.
- Equal rates do not mean equal amounts, and constant does not mean equal.
- On a graph of concentration against time, equilibrium is where every curve goes flat. A graph of partial pressures reads the same way. On a graph of the forward and reverse rates against time, equilibrium is where the two curves meet and run on together as one flat line.
- The same equilibrium mixture is reached from either side, as long as the atoms, the volume and the temperature are the same.

</KeyPoints>
