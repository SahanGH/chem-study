Start a reaction, then just watch. Every few seconds, write down how much of one reactant is left. In topic 5.2 you needed several runs to find the order of a reaction. Here one run is enough, because the order shows up in the *shape* of the fall.

## Three orders, three shapes

Call the reactant you are following A. If the reaction has a second reactant, the experiment is set up with that reactant in large excess, so its concentration hardly changes and only the fall of A shows. The $k$ you then get from the plot has that constant concentration folded into it.

How $[\ce{A}]$ falls depends on the order in A. A zero-order reaction does not care how much A is left, so $[\ce{A}]$ drops by the same amount every second. A first-order or second-order reaction slows down as A runs out, so its curve bends and flattens. Even then, something changes by the same amount every second. You just have to know what to look at.

In the equations below, $[\ce{A}]_0$ is the concentration at the start and $[\ce{A}]_t$ is the concentration at time $t$.

| Order in A | What falls steadily | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $[\ce{A}]$ itself | $[\ce{A}]_t = [\ce{A}]_0 - kt$ |
| 1 | $\ln[\ce{A}]$ | $\ln[\ce{A}]_t = \ln[\ce{A}]_0 - kt$ |
| 2 | nothing, but $1/[\ce{A}]$ rises steadily | $\frac{1}{[\ce{A}]_t} = \frac{1}{[\ce{A}]_0} + kt$ |

All three equations are on the AP equation sheet. The sheet writes them with both concentrations on the left side, but they are the same equations.

Each one is the equation of a straight line. Put time on the horizontal axis and the quantity on the left on the vertical axis. The number multiplying $t$ is then the slope.

| Order in A | Straight-line plot | Slope |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $[\ce{A}]$ against $t$ | $-k$ |
| 1 | $\ln[\ce{A}]$ against $t$ | $-k$ |
| 2 | $1/[\ce{A}]$ against $t$ | $+k$ |

Choose an order and see which plot straightens out.

<KeyIdea>

Make all three plots from your data. The one that comes out straight tells you the order, and its slope gives you $k$.

</KeyIdea>

## Half-life

The **half-life**, written $t_{1/2}$, is the time it takes for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half of what it was.

A first-order reaction has a half-life with a special property: it is the same no matter how much is left. Going from 0.80 M to 0.40 M takes exactly as long as going from 0.10 M to 0.050 M. The half-life depends only on the rate constant:

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}
$$

The 0.693 is $\ln 2$, rounded.

Each half-life cuts what is left in half again. So after $n$ half-lives, the fraction left is $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n$. One half-life leaves 1/2. Two leave 1/4.

For zero-order and second-order reactions, the half-life changes as the reaction runs. That makes a constant half-life a sign of first order all by itself.

<Worked title="Order and k from one run">

The reaction $\ce{A -> products}$ is followed for 300 s.

| Time (s) | $[\ce{A}]$ (M) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.800 |
| 100 | 0.400 |
| 200 | 0.200 |
| 300 | 0.100 |

Find the order in A and the value of $k$.

1. **Look at how the concentration falls.** It halves every 100 s, whatever it starts from: 0.800 M to 0.400 M, then 0.400 M to 0.200 M, then 0.200 M to 0.100 M. That is a constant half-life of 100 s, so the reaction is first order.

2. **Confirm with the plot.** Take the natural log of each concentration. In order, $\ln[\ce{A}]$ is $-0.223$, $-0.916$, $-1.609$ and $-2.303$. It falls by 0.693 every 100 s. (Subtract the last pair and you get 0.694. That is only rounding.) Equal drops in equal times make a straight line, which is what first order predicts.

3. **Find $k$** from the half-life.

   $$
   k = \frac{0.693}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{100\ \text{s}} = 6.93 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}
   $$

The line in step 2 drops 0.693 for every 100 s, so its slope is $-6.93 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. That is $-k$. Both routes give the same rate constant.

</Worked>

<Check numeric="30.0" unit="min">

<div slot="why">

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{0.0231\ \text{min}^{-1}} = 30.0\ \text{min}
$$

</div>
</Check>

The three equations are not only for drawing plots. If you know $k$ and the starting concentration, you can work out the concentration at any later time.

<Worked title="A concentration at a later time">

A second-order reaction has $k = 0.040\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ and starts at $[\ce{A}]_0 = 0.50\ \text{M}$. Find $[\ce{A}]$ after 50 s.

1. **Pick the equation that matches the order.** The reaction is second order, so use the one with $1/[\ce{A}]$.

2. **Put in the numbers.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{1}{[\ce{A}]_t} &= \frac{1}{0.50\ \text{M}} + (0.040\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1})(50\ \text{s}) \\
   &= 2.0\ \text{M}^{-1} + 2.0\ \text{M}^{-1} = 4.0\ \text{M}^{-1}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Turn it over.** The equation gives you $1/[\ce{A}]$, not $[\ce{A}]$. Take the reciprocal: $[\ce{A}]_t = 0.25\ \text{M}$.

Check that the answer makes sense. The concentration started at 0.50 M and A is being used up, so 0.25 M is believable. An answer above 0.50 M would mean you forgot the last step.

</Worked>

<Check numeric="0.40" unit="M">
A zero-order reaction starts at $[\ce{A}]_0 = 0.60\ \text{M}$ with $k = 0.020\ \text{M/s}$. What is $[\ce{A}]$ after 10 s?

<div slot="why">

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\ce{A}] &= 0.60\ \text{M} - (0.020\ \text{M/s})(10\ \text{s}) \\
&= 0.40\ \text{M}
\end{aligned}
$$

</div>

## Radioactive decay

Every radioactive decay is a first-order process. So each radioactive isotope has one fixed half-life, whether the sample is large or small, and that is why half-life is the number quoted for it.

<Check answer={3}>
A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8.0 days. What fraction of the isotope is left after 24 days?

- 1/2
- 1/3
- 1/8
- 1/24

<div slot="why">24 days is three half-lives, and $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \tfrac{1}{8}$.</div>
</Check>

<Trap>

Four common mistakes on this topic:

- **Using $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$ for any order.** It holds for first order only.
- **Getting the sign of the slope wrong.** The $[\ce{A}]$ and $\ln[\ce{A}]$ plots slope down, with slope $-k$. The $1/[\ce{A}]$ plot slopes up, with slope $+k$.
- **Judging the order by eye from the curve of $[\ce{A}]$ against time.** First-order and second-order curves both bend and look alike. Make the $\ln[\ce{A}]$ and $1/[\ce{A}]$ plots and see which one is straight.
- **Using log base 10.** The first-order equation uses the natural log, ln.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The plot that is a straight line gives the order: $[\ce{A}]$ against time for zero order (slope $-k$), $\ln[\ce{A}]$ against time for first order (slope $-k$), $1/[\ce{A}]$ against time for second order (slope $+k$).
- The equations are $[\ce{A}]_t = [\ce{A}]_0 - kt$ for zero order, $\ln[\ce{A}]_t = \ln[\ce{A}]_0 - kt$ for first order and $\frac{1}{[\ce{A}]_t} = \frac{1}{[\ce{A}]_0} + kt$ for second order.
- The half-life of a first-order reaction is constant and equals $0.693/k$.
- For a first-order reaction, after $n$ half-lives the fraction of reactant left is $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n$.
- Radioactive decay is first order, so every radioactive isotope has one fixed half-life.

</KeyPoints>
