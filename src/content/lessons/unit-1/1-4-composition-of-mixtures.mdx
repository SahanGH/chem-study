Pure zinc oxide, ZnO, is 80.34 percent zinc by mass, in any sample. Its formula settles that. A tube of zinc-oxide sunscreen is another matter. The maker can stir in a little zinc oxide or a lot, so one brand is 5 percent zinc oxide and another is 25. No formula will tell you which. For a mixture, the composition is something you measure.

## One pile or more

Here is a test that works for any sample. Imagine sorting its particles into piles, identical ones together. If everything ends up in one pile, the sample is a **pure substance**. If you need two piles or more, it is a **mixture**, and one pile can be large or small beside another, because the proportions are not fixed.

Identical here means the same kind. Atoms of one element go in one pile whatever their isotope (topic 1.2). So do molecules built from the same atoms joined the same way.

What you sort is the unit the substance is built from:

- single atoms, for an element such as argon
- molecules, for a substance made of molecules
- formula units, for a substance that has no molecules

An element made of molecules, such as O₂, is also one pile.

A compound is still one pile, even though it contains more than one kind of **atom**, because its atoms come joined in one fixed pattern. That goes for a compound made of ions too. Potassium bromide holds two kinds of ion, but always one potassium ion, K⁺, for each bromide ion, Br⁻. So you sort it by formula units' worth, as in topic 1.3's figure, not by separate ions, and each formula unit's worth is like every other. The fixed ratio is the point: if it were not fixed, there would be no formula unit to sort by, and the two kinds would go in separate piles.

Read the three boxes in turn. The first holds single atoms, all alike. That is one pile: an element. The second holds molecules, all alike, each built of two kinds of atom. That is one pile too: a compound. The third holds two different kinds of molecule. That is two piles: a mixture, here of two compounds.

Sorting by kinds of atom would call the second box a mixture, and it is not.

## No formula for a mixture

Bronze is copper with tin mixed in. Take a bronze that is 80.0 percent copper and 20.0 percent tin by mass. In 100.0 g of it there are 80.0 g of copper and 20.0 g of tin. In moles, that is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{80.0\ \text{g}}{63.55\ \text{g/mol}} &= 1.259\ \text{mol Cu} \\
\frac{20.0\ \text{g}}{118.71\ \text{g/mol}} &= 0.1685\ \text{mol Sn}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide one by the other and you get 7.47 copper atoms for every tin atom. A bronze with 10.0 percent tin gives a different ratio, 16.8 to 1. So the ratio is whatever the maker chose, and nothing makes it come out in small whole numbers.

A compound's atoms come in a fixed whole-number ratio. A mixture's need not, and the ratio can change from one batch to the next. That is why bronze has no formula unit to sort by: its copper atoms and its tin atoms go in two piles.

The masses follow the atoms. A compound's composition by mass is fixed by its formula (topic 1.3). Bronze has no formula to do that job. Its makeup was set by whoever melted the copper and tin together, and the next batch can be different. So a mixture's mass percents cannot be calculated from a formula. You find them by analyzing a sample.

<KeyIdea>

A pure compound's composition comes from its formula. A mixture's comes from how much of each substance went into it, so you find it by analyzing a sample, not by calculating from a formula.

</KeyIdea>

## What one element can tell you

An **elemental analysis** is a measurement of the mass of each element in a weighed sample.

Suppose one of those elements is found in only one substance of the mixture. Then its mass leads straight to the mass of that substance:

1. grams of the element to moles of the element
2. moles of the element to moles of the substance, using the formula's subscript
3. moles of the substance to grams of the substance

Divide by the mass of the sample and you have the mixture's composition.

<Worked title="Zinc oxide in a sunscreen">

A mineral sunscreen is zinc oxide, ZnO, stirred into a lotion that contains no zinc. A chemist analyzes a 2.00 g sample of the sunscreen and finds 0.321 g of zinc in it. What percent of the sunscreen, by mass, is zinc oxide?

1. **Decide what you need.** The mass of zinc oxide in the sample, and the mass of the sample. All the zinc is in the zinc oxide, so the zinc will give the first. The second is 2.00 g.

2. **Grams of zinc to moles of zinc.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\frac{0.321\ \text{g}}{65.38\ \text{g/mol}} \\
   &= 4.910 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol Zn}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Moles of zinc to moles of zinc oxide.** Each formula unit of ZnO holds one zinc, so there are $4.910 \times 10^{-3}$ mol of ZnO.

4. **Moles of zinc oxide to grams.** First the molar mass of ZnO.

   $$
   65.38 + 16.00 = 81.38\ \text{g/mol}
   $$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &4.910 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \times 81.38\ \text{g/mol} \\
   &= 0.3996\ \text{g ZnO}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Percent of the sample.**

   $$
   \frac{0.3996\ \text{g}}{2.00\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 20.0\%
   $$

   The other 1.60 g is lotion.

6. **Check by another road.** Pure ZnO is 80.34 percent zinc. So the zinc oxide that holds 0.321 g of zinc has a mass of

   $$
   \frac{0.321\ \text{g}}{0.8034} = 0.3996\ \text{g}
   $$

   The same.

7. **The answer to avoid.** Divide 0.321 g by 2.00 g and you get about 16 percent. That is the percent of **zinc** in the sunscreen, not of zinc oxide. The oxygen in the zinc oxide has been left out.

</Worked>

## Testing purity

The same measurement tests whether a sample is pure. A pure compound must show the mass percent its formula gives. A match does not prove purity, because an impurity could happen to hold the same share of that element, but a mismatch rules it out.

A jar labeled zinc oxide should analyze at 80.34 percent zinc. If it comes out at 76 percent, the jar is not pure zinc oxide. Something that holds less zinc, or none, is mixed in.

And if the impurity holds none of that element, the element's mass tells you how much of the sample is the compound, just as in the worked example.

Rutile is an ore of titanium, mined for white paint pigment and for the metal. It is mostly titanium dioxide, TiO₂. A 5.00 g sample of a rutile ore is found to contain 2.70 g of titanium, and none of the ore's impurities contain titanium. What percent of the ore, by mass, is TiO₂? (Type the number only, without the % sign.)

<div slot="why">

All the titanium is in the TiO₂. Start with the moles of titanium.

$$
\frac{2.70\ \text{g}}{47.87\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.05640\ \text{mol}
$$

There is one Ti in each TiO₂, so that is also the moles of TiO₂. Next, its molar mass.

$$
47.87 + 2(16.00) = 79.87\ \text{g/mol}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0.05640\ \text{mol} \times 79.87\ \text{g/mol} \\
&= 4.505\ \text{g}\ \ce{TiO2}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{4.505\ \text{g}}{5.00\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 90.1\%
$$

The purity test agrees that the ore is not pure. The sample has 2.70 g of titanium in 5.00 g, which is 54.0 percent titanium. Pure TiO₂ would be 59.93 percent.

If your answer was marked wrong, it may be one of these common slips:

- **54.0** is the percent of titanium in the sample, not of TiO₂.
- **135** comes from using oxygen's share of TiO₂ in place of titanium's. No sample can be 135 percent of anything.
- **0.901** is the answer left as a fraction.
- **72.0** comes from forgetting the subscript 2 on oxygen.
- **9.9** is the percent that is not TiO₂.
- **94.1** comes from taking the shortfall in titanium, 59.93 − 54.0, off 100. That gap is a difference in percent titanium. It is not the percent of the sample that is impurity.

## When two substances share the element

Sometimes both substances in a mixture contain the element you measured. Then the mixture's mass percent of that element lies between the values for the two pure substances, and it lies nearer the value of whichever substance there is more of by mass.

It is a weighted average, like the average atomic mass of topic 1.2, with the two substances in place of the isotopes and their shares of the mass in place of the abundances. So one measured percent, set beside two values worked out from formulas, is enough to say roughly how much of each substance a two-part mixture holds.

Quicklime, CaO, is 71.47 percent calcium by mass. Left open to damp air, it takes up water and turns, bit by bit, into Ca(OH)₂, which is 54.09 percent calcium. A sample from an old bag, which holds nothing but these two compounds, is 67.1 percent calcium. What does that show?

- The bag is still pure CaO
- All of the CaO has turned into Ca(OH)₂
- The bag is a mixture, and more than half of it is Ca(OH)₂
- The bag is a mixture, and more than half of it is CaO

<div slot="why">

67.1 is neither 71.47 nor 54.09, so the sample is not either pure compound. It is a mixture of the two.

Its calcium percent lies between the two pure values, and it is nearer CaO's (4.4 away) than Ca(OH)₂'s (13.0 away). So most of the sample is still CaO. The midpoint, 62.78 percent, is what a half-and-half mixture by mass would give.

Working it through, the sample is about three quarters CaO by mass:

$$
\frac{67.1 - 54.09}{71.47 - 54.09} = 0.75
$$

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Calling a compound a mixture because it has two elements.** It is the number of kinds of unit (atom, molecule or formula unit) that counts, not the number of elements. Quartz is still one pile of SiO₂ formula units, a pure substance.
- **Reading a mixture's composition from a formula.** A formula fixes the composition of a compound only. A mixture's is measured.
- **Reporting the element's percent as the compound's.** The sunscreen is about 16 percent zinc but 20.0 percent zinc oxide.
- **Following an element that two substances share.** Iron cannot give the chalcopyrite when pyrite holds iron too. Follow copper.
- **Forgetting the sample's mass.** A percent needs the mass of the whole sample underneath.
- **Taking "pure" on trust.** A pure compound shows its formula's mass percent: 59.93 percent titanium for TiO₂, not 54.0.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A pure substance is one kind of atom, molecule or formula unit. A mixture is two or more kinds, in proportions that can vary.
- A compound's composition by mass is fixed by its formula. A mixture's has to be measured.
- When only one substance in a mixture contains a certain element, the mass of that element gives the mass of that substance, by way of moles and the formula.
- A sample is pure only if its measured mass percent of each element matches the value from the formula.
- In a mixture of just two substances that share an element, the mixture's mass percent of that element lies between their two values, nearer that of the substance there is more of by mass.

</KeyPoints>
