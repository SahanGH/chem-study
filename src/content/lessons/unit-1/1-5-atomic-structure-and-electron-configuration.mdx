Take a load of laundry out of the dryer and a sock clings to a shirt. Peel it away and it crackles, and in a dark room you can see the sparks. Tumbling has left the two fabrics with opposite charges, and opposite charges pull on each other.

That same pull holds every atom together: a positive nucleus, negative electrons. This lesson is about where an atom's electrons are, and how firmly that force holds each one.

## What an atom is made of

An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** at its center. The nucleus is made of **protons**, each with a charge of 1+, and **neutrons**, which have no charge. Around it are the **electrons**, each with a charge of 1−. Nearly all of the atom's mass is in the nucleus.

The number of protons is the **atomic number**, the whole number in each box of the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/). It decides which element the atom is: an atom with 14 protons is silicon.

A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so a silicon atom has 14 electrons.

An **ion** has a different number of electrons from protons, and its charge is protons minus electrons:

- 56 protons with 54 electrons is a barium ion, Ba²⁺.
- 53 protons with 54 electrons is an iodide ion, I⁻.

Neutrons change an atom's mass, and nothing in this lesson. (Topic 1.2 is about them.)

## Coulomb's law

Two charged particles push or pull on each other. Opposite charges attract, and like charges repel. **Coulomb's law** says how strongly.

$$
F_{\text{coulombic}} \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}
$$

Here $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the two charges, $r$ is the distance between them, and the sign $\propto$ means "is proportional to". Read two things from it:

- **More charge, more force.** Double either charge and the force doubles.
- **More distance, much less force.** The distance is squared, so doubling it cuts the force to a quarter, and tripling it cuts the force to a ninth.

The equation sheet prints the law in exactly this form. It has no constant, so it cannot give a force in newtons. It is for comparing and explaining.

In an atom, every electron is attracted to the nucleus and repelled by every other electron. How much energy it takes to pull an electron out of an atom depends on those forces, and that is where this lesson ends up.

## Shells and subshells

The electrons in an atom cannot have just any energy. They occupy **shells**, also called energy levels, numbered 1, 2, 3 and so on. An electron in a higher-numbered shell is, on average, farther from the nucleus.

Each shell is divided into **subshells**, also called sublevels. A subshell is named by its shell's number and a letter: s, p, d or f.

- Shell 1 has one subshell, 1s.
- Shell 2 has two, 2s and 2p.
- Shell 3 has three: 3s, 3p and 3d.
- Shell 4 has four: 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f.

Each kind of subshell holds a fixed maximum number of electrons. Here they are, with a column on orbitals that the next section explains.

| Subshell | Orbitals | Holds up to | First appears in |
|---|---|---|---|
| s | 1 | 2 electrons | shell 1 |
| p | 3 | 6 electrons | shell 2 |
| d | 5 | 10 electrons | shell 3 |
| f | 7 | 14 electrons | shell 4 |

So shell 1 can hold 2 electrons in all, shell 2 can hold 2 + 6 = 8, shell 3 can hold 2 + 6 + 10 = 18, and shell 4 can hold 2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32.

In an atom with more than one electron, electrons in different subshells of one shell do not have the same energy. Within a shell, s is lowest, then p, then d. The steps are large enough that the shells overlap: the 4s subshell fills before 3d. A picture of the whole order comes at the end of the next section.

## Orbitals, briefly

This picture of shells and subshells comes from quantum mechanics, the physics of very small things. In it, each subshell is made of **orbitals**: regions around the nucleus where an electron of that subshell is likely to be found.

An orbital holds at most two electrons. (This limit comes from the Pauli exclusion principle.) An s subshell is one orbital, a p subshell is three, a d subshell is five and an f subshell is seven, which is where the capacities 2, 6, 10 and 14 come from.

Electrons repel each other, so the electrons of one subshell spread out: each takes an empty orbital of that subshell before any two share one. (This is called Hund's rule.) A p subshell with three electrons has one in each of its three orbitals. Give it a fourth and that electron has to share an orbital with another. Topic 1.7 uses that fact.

Now that orbitals are defined, here are the subshells in the order in which they fill, lowest first, each drawn as its orbitals.

## Reading a configuration from the periodic table

An **electron configuration** lists the occupied subshells, each with its number of electrons as a superscript.

The **ground state** of an atom is its arrangement of lowest energy: the electrons fill the lowest subshell first, and each subshell fills to its limit before the next one starts. That build-up rule is the **Aufbau principle**. (Aufbau is German for "building up".)

The equation sheet does not give the order, and you do not have to memorize it, because the periodic table is laid out in it. Read the table like a page: left to right along each row, top row first.

Read one row, box by box. Row 4 has eighteen boxes:

- **The first two boxes**, potassium and calcium, fill 4s.
- **The next ten boxes**, scandium to zinc, fill 3d, not 4d. These boxes run one shell behind, because 4s fills before 3d.
- **The last six boxes**, gallium to krypton, fill 4p.

Now the rule for the whole table. Every row from row 2 on begins with two boxes that fill an s subshell, the one of the row's own shell, and ends with six boxes that fill that shell's p subshell. From row 4 on, the boxes between them fill the d subshell of the shell before. Those three stretches of the table are called the **s block**, the **d block** and the **p block**.

Three things to notice:

- Row 1 has two boxes, both 1s. Helium is set at the far right, above the other noble gases (the elements of the last column), although it belongs to the s block.
- The two rows set below the table belong to rows 6 and 7. They are the **f block**, where 4f and 5f fill, two shells behind their rows.
- Each of those lower rows has fifteen boxes, although an f subshell holds 14 electrons. Each lower row also carries the one d-block box of its own row (row 6 or row 7), which is why rows 6 and 7 show nine d-block boxes in the main table, not ten.

Now find silicon. It is element 14, in row 3, the second box of the p block. Reading up to it you pass 1s² (row 1), 2s² 2p⁶ (row 2), then 3s² and two boxes of the p block, 3p². Its ground-state configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². The superscripts add up to 14, the number of electrons.

The first ten electrons are arranged exactly as in neon, the noble gas that ends row 2, so the configuration is shortened to [Ne] 3s² 3p². A noble gas's symbol in brackets stands for that atom's whole configuration.

<Worked title="Titanium, read off the periodic table">

1. **Find it.** Titanium, Ti, is element 22: row 4, the second box of the d block. A neutral atom has 22 electrons.

2. **Read rows 1 to 3.** Row 1 gives 1s² (2 electrons so far). Row 2 gives 2s² 2p⁶ (10 so far). Row 3 gives 3s² 3p⁶ (18 so far).

3. **Read row 4 up to titanium.** First the s block: 4s² (20 so far). Then the d block, which is one shell behind its row, so it is 3d, not 4d. Titanium is two boxes in: 3d² (22).

4. **Write it out.** 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d².

5. **Check the count.** 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 2 = 22, the atomic number.

6. **Shorten it.** The first 18 electrons are argon's, the noble gas that ends row 3: [Ar] 4s² 3d².

</Worked>

You will also see the subshells listed in order of shell number, [Ar] 3d² 4s². It is the same configuration, and both ways of writing it are in use.

**Beyond the exam:** about twenty elements, chromium and copper among them, have ground states that depart a little from this pattern. Writing their configurations is not assessed on the AP exam, and this lesson does not use them.

## Core and valence electrons

The elements of the s block and the p block are called the **main-group elements**. For a main-group element, the electrons in the atom's outermost occupied shell are its **valence electrons**. They are the ones farthest from the nucleus, the ones another atom meets first, and the ones that take part in bonding (topic 2.1). The electrons in the shells inside are **core electrons**.

Take sulfur, element 16, two boxes to the right of silicon in row 3. It is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴, with six valence electrons, the 3s² 3p⁴ of shell 3, and ten core electrons in shells 1 and 2.

The 10 electrons of the two inner rings are marked as core electrons and the 6 electrons of the outer ring as valence electrons."
  caption="Sulfur's 16 electrons, shell by shell. The rings show which shell each electron is in. They are not paths that the electrons follow."

For sulfur the shorthand shows the split at a glance: the bracket is the core.

Farther down the table, look twice. From row 4 on, the configuration of a main-group element to the right of the d block also shows a full d subshell after the bracket (and, in rows 6 and 7, a full f subshell as well). Those electrons are in a lower shell than its valence electrons, so they are not valence electrons: count them with the core. Tellurium, element 52, two rows below sulfur, is [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p⁴. It has six valence electrons again, 5s² 5p⁴, and 46 core electrons.

For the d-block metals themselves the split is less tidy, because their outer s electrons and their d electrons can both be involved in bonding. This lesson counts valence electrons for main-group elements only.

## Ions

To write the configuration of an ion, start from the atom's configuration, then change the number of electrons. The charge says how: 2+ means two electrons fewer than the atom, and 1− means one more. So the superscripts of an ion's configuration add up to a different total from the atom's.

A negative ion is called an **anion**, and a positive ion a **cation**. Each has its own rule:

- **An anion's extra electrons** go where the next electron would go: into the subshell that is being filled.
- **A cation's lost electrons** come out of the highest-numbered shell first, because those electrons are farthest from the nucleus and held least tightly. Within that shell, p electrons leave before s electrons.

The second rule matters most for the d-block metals. The configuration of a row-4 d-block metal such as titanium or manganese ends in 4s² and some 3d electrons. The 4s electrons are the first to go, although 4s filled before 3d. Once 3d holds electrons, they lie closer to the nucleus than the 4s electrons, which are then the outermost and the least tightly held.

<Worked title="Two ions">

Potassium iodide, added to some table salt, supplies iodine as the iodide ion, I⁻. The pink of the mineral rhodochrosite comes from the manganese ion Mn²⁺. Write the ground-state configuration of each ion.

1. **Start from the atoms.** Iodine is element 53, in row 5, the fifth box of the p block: [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p⁵. Manganese is element 25, in row 4, the fifth box of the d block: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁵.

2. **Iodide gains one electron.** The charge 1− means 53 + 1 = 54 electrons. The extra electron goes into the subshell being filled, 5p: [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p⁶. That is xenon's configuration, so I⁻ is [Xe].

3. **Manganese loses two.** The charge 2+ means 25 − 2 = 23 electrons. They come out of the highest shell, shell 4: the two 4s electrons. That leaves [Ar] 3d⁵.

4. **Check the counts.** For I⁻: 36 + 2 + 10 + 6 = 54. For Mn²⁺: 18 + 5 = 23.

5. **The answer to avoid.** [Ar] 4s² 3d³ also has 23 electrons, but it takes them from 3d and leaves the outer shell untouched. The electrons farthest from the nucleus leave first.

</Worked>

Atoms and ions with the same electron configuration are **isoelectronic**. The telluride ion Te²⁻ (52 + 2 electrons), the iodide ion, a xenon atom and the cesium ion Cs⁺ (55 − 1) all have 54 electrons arranged as [Xe]. They are not alike in every way: their nuclei hold 52, 53, 54 and 55 protons, and topic 1.7 uses such a set to compare sizes.

## Ground state, excited state, or impossible

Not every configuration you are shown is a ground state. Sort one with three questions:

1. **Count the electrons.** Do they match the atom or ion named? If not, it is a configuration of a different atom or ion.
2. **Is any subshell over its limit?** An s subshell holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10. If one is overfilled, no atom can have that configuration: it is impossible.
3. **Is a subshell that fills earlier left with room while one that fills later holds electrons?** Earlier and later go by the filling order, not by the order in which the configuration happens to be written. If so, it is an **excited state**: the atom has taken in energy, and an electron sits in a higher subshell than it needs to.

If the answer to questions 2 and 3 is no, it is the ground state.

One caution. Question 3 works for neutral atoms, apart from the exceptions mentioned above, and for anions. For a cation, use the cation rule instead: work out the ground state from the atom, taking electrons from the highest shell first, and compare. A cation's configuration that has the right count and nothing overfilled, but is not that ground state, is an excited state.

Question 3 by itself would get some cations wrong. It would call Mn²⁺ and Ga³⁺ excited, because each has an empty 4s subshell although 3d holds electrons. And it would call [Ar] 4s² a ground state for Ti²⁺, since no earlier subshell in it is left with room, although the cation rule gives [Ar] 3d².

Take three configurations with 11 electrons, sodium's number:

- 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ is sodium's ground state.
- 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3p¹ overfills nothing, but 3s is empty while 3p is occupied. It is an excited state of sodium.
- 1s³ 2s² 2p⁶ puts three electrons in a subshell that holds two. It is impossible.

An excited atom can give out light as its electron drops back and the atom returns to its ground state. (Topic 3.12 takes that up.) An excited atom is still the same element, with the same number of electrons.

## Ionization energy and Coulomb's law

**Ionization energy** is the energy needed to remove an electron from an atom or ion in the gas phase. Here it is for silicon. Read the arrow as "becomes". The (g) marks a gas, and e⁻ stands for the electron.

$$
\ce{Si(g) -> Si+(g) + e-}
$$

This takes 787 kJ for every mole of silicon atoms. It is silicon's **first ionization energy**: the energy to remove the most loosely held electron from the neutral atom.

The equation sheet has no entry for ionization energy. What it has is Coulomb's law, and that is what explains it. Three things decide how strongly an electron is held:

- **Nuclear charge:** the number of protons. More protons, more attraction.
- **Distance:** the shell. An electron in a higher shell is farther from the nucleus, and the force falls with the square of the distance.
- **Shielding:** the electrons that lie between an electron and the nucleus repel it, and so cancel part of the nucleus's pull. Core electrons shield a valence electron well: each cancels about one proton. Electrons in the same shell shield one another poorly, because they are not in between.

What an electron feels once shielding is taken off is the **effective nuclear charge**. A rough estimate is the number of protons minus the number of electrons in the shells inside. Silicon, two boxes to the left of sulfur, has the same ten core electrons, so for its valence electrons that is 14 − 10, about +4.

A stronger attraction means more energy to remove the electron: a larger ionization energy.

<KeyIdea>

To explain how tightly an electron is held, name the levers: the nuclear charge, the distance (which shell) and the shielding by the electrons inside. A larger effective nuclear charge or a smaller distance means a stronger attraction, and a larger ionization energy.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Three electrons in one silicon atom">

In a silicon atom, which takes the most energy to remove, and which the least: a 1s electron, a 2p electron or a 3p electron? Justify the order with Coulomb's law.

1. **Place each electron.** Silicon is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². The 1s electron is in shell 1, the 2p electron in shell 2 and the 3p electron in shell 3, the valence shell.

2. **Compare the distances.** Shell 1 is closest to the nucleus and shell 3 is farthest. The force falls with the square of the distance.

3. **Compare the effective nuclear charges.** The nucleus holds 14 protons. A 1s electron has no shell inside it, so it feels nearly all of +14. A 2p electron has the two 1s electrons inside: about 14 − 2 = +12. A 3p electron has all ten core electrons inside: about 14 − 10 = +4.

4. **Make the claim.** Both levers point the same way, so the order is 1s (most energy), then 2p, then 3p (least).

5. **Write the answer.** "A 1s electron takes the most energy to remove and a 3p electron the least. The 1s electrons are closest to the nucleus, with no electrons inside them to shield its 14 protons. The 3p electrons are in the third shell, farthest from the nucleus, and the 10 core electrons shield them, so they feel an effective nuclear charge of only about +4. By Coulomb's law, a smaller charge at a greater distance gives a weaker attraction, so less energy is needed to remove the electron. The 2p electrons lie between the two on both counts."

6. **Compare with measurement.** The measured energies are 178 MJ/mol for a 1s electron, 10.3 MJ/mol for a 2p electron and 0.79 MJ/mol for a 3p electron (1 MJ is 1000 kJ). A 1s electron takes more than 200 times as much energy to remove as a 3p electron, and a 2p electron about 13 times as much. The 0.79 MJ/mol is silicon's first ionization energy again, 787 kJ/mol, rounded. Topic 1.6 shows how such energies are measured.

</Worked>

Even inside one shell the subshells differ. Silicon's 3s electrons take 1.46 MJ/mol to remove, against 0.79 MJ/mol for its 3p electrons. An s electron spends more of its time close to the nucleus, inside the shielding of the other electrons, so it feels more of the nuclear charge. That is why each shell fills its s subshell before its p.

For the same inner subshell in two different atoms, the lever is the nuclear charge. A 1s electron in silicon and a 1s electron in sodium are both in the first shell, with nothing inside to shield them. But silicon's nucleus has 14 protons to sodium's 11, and its 1s electrons take 178 MJ/mol to remove, against 104 MJ/mol. Comparisons like these, carried along whole rows and columns of the table, are the periodic trends of topic 1.7.

<Trap>

Seven mistakes to watch for on this topic:

- **Writing 4d for the d block of row 4.** The d block runs one shell behind its row: row 4 fills 3d.
- **Taking a d-block ion's electrons from 3d first.** They leave the highest shell first: Mn²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵, not [Ar] 4s² 3d³.
- **Leaving an ion's electron count unchanged.** A charge means electrons gained or lost: I⁻ has 54 electrons, not 53.
- **Taking an overfilled subshell for an excited state.** An excited state of sodium, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3p¹, overfills nothing. 1s³ 2s² 2p⁶ is not excited: it is impossible.
- **Counting a full inner d subshell as valence.** Tellurium's 4d¹⁰ is in shell 4, a lower shell than its valence shell, shell 5.
- **Explaining with "it wants a full shell".** An explanation has to name what pulls on the electron, and how strongly: the nuclear charge, the distance, the shielding.
- **Looking for a number from Coulomb's law.** The sheet gives a proportionality with no constant. Use it to compare.

<KeyPoints>

- An atom is a nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons around it. The number of protons fixes the element, and an ion's charge is its protons minus its electrons.
- Electrons occupy shells divided into subshells. An s, p, d or f subshell holds up to 2, 6, 10 or 14 electrons.
- A ground-state configuration fills subshells in the order the periodic table shows, row by row, with the d block one shell behind its row. A noble gas's symbol in brackets stands for the inner electrons.
- For an ion, write the atom's configuration first. Then add electrons to the subshell being filled, or remove them from the highest shell, so a row-4 d-block metal loses its 4s electrons before its 3d.
- An atom's configuration that leaves an earlier subshell with room while a later one holds electrons is an excited state. Any configuration with an overfilled subshell is impossible.
- Ionization energy is the energy to remove an electron. By Coulomb's law it is larger when the effective nuclear charge is larger and the electron is closer to the nucleus. Core electrons shield well, and electrons of the same shell shield poorly.
