Cassiterite is the mineral that nearly all mined tin comes from. A pure crystal dug in Bolivia and a pure crystal dug in Cornwall, England, have this in common: each is 78.77 percent tin by mass. Not roughly the same. The same, wherever the pure mineral is found. A miner can know how much tin a sack of pure cassiterite will give before the sack is opened. This lesson is about where such a number comes from, and how to run the reasoning backward, from the numbers to the formula.

## Molecules and formula units

Pure substances are built in two ways, and a formula means something a little different for each.

Some are made of **molecules**: separate groups of atoms joined together. Hydrogen sulfide, the gas that bubbles from sulfur springs, is one. Every hydrogen sulfide molecule is one sulfur atom and two hydrogen atoms, and the formula H₂S describes one molecule.

Others have no molecules at all. Potassium bromide is an array of potassium ions and bromide ions. (An ion is an atom that carries an electric charge; topic 1.8 says which charges to expect.) There is one ion of each kind for every one of the other, and the array continues in every direction to the edge of the crystal. The drawing below shows a small patch of it: the small dark circles are the potassium ions, and the larger pale circles are the bromide ions.

You cannot pick out one "KBr" particle. The formula gives the ratio, and the smallest group of atoms or ions in that ratio is called a **formula unit**.

Cassiterite, SnO₂, has no molecules either: it is one tin for every two oxygens throughout the crystal. Quartz, SiO₂, which is built of atoms and not ions, also has no molecules. It is one silicon atom for every two oxygen atoms, bonded together all the way through.

Either way, the formula fixes how many atoms of each element there are **relative to one another**. That is all the rest of this lesson needs.

## The same proportions in every sample

A compound's formula does not depend on how big the sample is or where it came from. Every bit of pure cassiterite has one tin atom for every two oxygen atoms, so every bit has the same share of its mass as tin.

That is the **law of definite proportions**: in any pure sample of a compound, the elements are present in the same ratio by mass.

For cassiterite the ratio of tin to oxygen by mass is 3.71 to 1. (The next section shows where that number comes from.) Here are two crystals of different sizes:

| Crystal | Tin (g) | Oxygen (g) | Tin : oxygen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10.00 g | 7.877 | 2.123 | 3.71 : 1 |
| 2.500 g | 1.969 | 0.531 | 3.71 : 1 |

A different ratio would mean a different compound, or a sample that is not pure (topic 1.4).

<KeyIdea>

Every molecule or formula unit of a compound has the same atoms in the same ratio. So in every pure sample, each element makes up the same share of the mass, and a measured composition points back to one ratio of atoms: a formula.

</KeyIdea>

## Mass percent from a formula

The usual way to state a composition is the **mass percent** of each element: the grams of that element in 100 g of the compound.

From a formula, work with one mole of the compound and its molar mass, which topic 1.1 showed how to find.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{mass percent of an element} \\
&= \frac{\text{mass of the element in 1 mol}}{\text{molar mass of the compound}} \\
&\quad \times 100\%
\end{aligned}
$$

The top of the fraction is the element's atomic mass times its subscript. The equation sheet has nothing on percent composition, so this is one to know.

From measurements, the idea is the same: the mass of the element found, divided by the mass of the sample, times 100.

<Worked title="Tin in cassiterite">

Find the mass percent of tin and of oxygen in cassiterite, SnO₂.

1. **Molar mass.** One Sn and two O.

   $$
   118.71 + 2(16.00) = 150.71\ \text{g/mol}
   $$

2. **Tin's share.** One mole of SnO₂ holds one mole of tin, 118.71 g.

   $$
   \frac{118.71}{150.71} \times 100\% = 78.77\%
   $$

3. **Oxygen's share.** One mole of SnO₂ holds two moles of oxygen atoms, 32.00 g.

   $$
   \frac{32.00}{150.71} \times 100\% = 21.23\%
   $$

   So one mole holds 118.71 g of tin for every 32.00 g of oxygen.

   $$
   \frac{118.71}{32.00} = 3.71
   $$

   That is the 3.71 to 1 of the two crystals in the table above.

4. **Check.** The percents of all the elements in a compound add up to 100.

   $$
   78.77\% + 21.23\% = 100.00\%
   $$

5. **Use it.** Find the tin in a 25.0 kg sack of pure cassiterite. As a fraction, 78.77 percent is 0.7877.

   $$
   25.0\ \text{kg} \times 0.7877 = 19.7\ \text{kg}
   $$

   The sack holds 19.7 kg of tin. Notice that tin is one atom in three but nearly four fifths of the mass: a tin atom is far heavier than an oxygen atom. A mass percent is not a percent of the atoms.

</Worked>

## The empirical formula

Now the other direction. An analysis of an unknown compound gives masses or percents, and you want a formula.

What composition can give you is the **empirical formula**: the formula with the lowest whole-number ratio of atoms. For a substance without molecules, such as SnO₂ or KBr, the formula you already write is usually the empirical formula: 1 to 2 and 1 to 1 cannot be reduced.

For a substance made of molecules there is a second formula, the **molecular formula**, which counts the atoms in one molecule. The two can differ. Hydrazine has molecules of two nitrogen atoms and four hydrogen atoms. Its molecular formula is N₂H₄. Its empirical formula, with the ratio cut down to lowest terms, is NH₂. For hydrogen sulfide the two are the same, H₂S, because 2 to 1 cannot be reduced.

The molecular formula is always the empirical formula multiplied through by a whole number: 2 for hydrazine, 1 for hydrogen sulfide. Composition alone cannot tell you that whole number, because N₂H₄ and NH₂ have the same mass percents. The molar mass can.

## From composition to a formula

Four steps take you from a composition to an empirical formula.

1. **Start from masses.** If you are given percents, imagine 100 g of the compound. Then a percent is a number of grams, with nothing to work out.
2. **Turn each mass into moles**, dividing it by the element's atomic mass, as in topic 1.1. A formula counts atoms. Grams do not count atoms, and moles do.
3. **Scale the amounts so that the smallest is 1.** To do it, divide each number of moles by the smallest of them. A formula is a ratio, and a ratio is easiest to read against 1.
4. **Make the ratios whole.** Atoms come whole, so a formula needs whole numbers. A ratio within a few hundredths of a whole number can be rounded. A ratio with a clear fraction left over cannot: clear the fraction by multiplying all the ratios by the whole number that matches it.

For step 4, read the leftover decimal as a fraction. A leftover near .5 is a half, so double every ratio. Near .33 or .67 it is thirds, so triple them. Near .25 or .75 it is quarters, so multiply by four. Near .2, .4, .6 or .8 it is fifths, so multiply by five. Near .17 or .83 it is sixths, so multiply by six. Round only when a ratio is within a few hundredths of a whole number.

Then check. After multiplying, every ratio should be within a few hundredths of a whole number. If one is not, the multiplier was wrong. Go back and try 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 in order. The first one that makes every ratio whole is the one to use. If none does, recheck the moles before trying a larger number.

If you also know the molar mass, you can go on to the molecular formula. Add up the mass of the empirical formula, just as you would add up a molar mass, and divide the molar mass by it. The answer is the whole number to multiply the empirical formula by. If it comes out as 1, the molecular formula is the empirical formula.

<Worked title="The formula of hexamine">

Hexamine is the white solid in the fuel tablets that hikers light to heat a meal. It contains only carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen: 51.40 percent C, 8.63 percent H and 39.97 percent N by mass. A separate measurement gives its molar mass, 140.2 g/mol. Find its empirical formula and its molecular formula.

1. **Start from masses.** In 100 g there are 51.40 g of C, 8.63 g of H and 39.97 g of N.

2. **Turn each mass into moles.** Divide each mass by the element's atomic mass.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{C:}\quad \frac{51.40}{12.01} &= 4.280\ \text{mol} \\
   \text{H:}\quad \frac{8.63}{1.008} &= 8.562\ \text{mol} \\
   \text{N:}\quad \frac{39.97}{14.01} &= 2.853\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

3. **Scale the amounts so that the smallest is 1.** Divide each by the smallest, 2.853.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{C:}\quad 4.280 \div 2.853 &= 1.50 \\
   \text{H:}\quad 8.562 \div 2.853 &= 3.00 \\
   \text{N:}\quad 2.853 \div 2.853 &= 1.00
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **Make the ratios whole.** 3.00 is whole. 1.50 is nowhere near a whole number, and the leftover .50 is a half, so double everything: C 3, H 6, N 2. The empirical formula is C₃H₆N₂.

5. **Find the multiplier.** Add up the mass of one C₃H₆N₂, as you would a molar mass.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &3(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 2(14.01) \\
   &= 36.03 + 6.048 + 28.02 \\
   &= 70.10\ \text{g/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Divide the molar mass by it.

   $$
   \frac{140.2}{70.10} = 2.00
   $$

6. **Write the molecular formula.** Twice C₃H₆N₂ is C₆H₁₂N₄.

7. **See what rounding would have done.** Round 1.50 down to 1 and the formula becomes CH₃N, which adds up to 29.04 g/mol.

   $$
   \frac{140.2}{29.04} = 4.83
   $$

   Round it up to 2 and you get C₂H₃N, which adds up to 41.05 g/mol.

   $$
   \frac{140.2}{41.05} = 3.42
   $$

   Neither is a whole number. A multiplier that will not come out whole is a sign that a ratio was rounded when it should have been multiplied.

</Worked>

The nitrogen is 5.00 − 3.00 = 2.00 g. Change both masses to moles.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Si:}\quad \frac{3.00}{28.09} &= 0.1068\ \text{mol} \\
\text{N:}\quad \frac{2.00}{14.01} &= 0.1428\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by the smaller. Silicon becomes 1, and nitrogen becomes

$$
\frac{0.1428}{0.1068} = 1.337
$$

The leftover .337 is close to a third, so triple both: Si 3, N 4.01. The formula is Si₃N₄.

Rounding 1.337 down to 1 gives SiN, which throws away a quarter of the nitrogen. Doubling gives 2 to 2.67, still not whole, so Si₂N₃ is not it either.

Si₃N₂ comes from reading the masses themselves, 3.00 to 2.00, as the ratio of atoms. They are not that ratio. A silicon atom is about twice as heavy as a nitrogen atom, which is why the comparison is made in moles.

## What composition can and cannot tell you

A table of mass percents looks as if it should tell you everything about a compound. Before you use one to answer a question, ask whether it can answer that question at all.

<Check
  answer={1}
  misses={{
    2: "Different compounds can have the same percent composition. Matching percents cannot show that two liquids are the same.",
    3: "Percent composition gives the ratio of atoms. A molar mass needs a separate measurement.",
    4: "Percent composition gives the ratio of atoms, not how many of them are in one molecule.",
  }}
>
A chemist measures the composition of two pure, colorless liquids from unlabeled bottles. Both come out the same: 85.63 percent carbon and 14.37 percent hydrogen by mass. Which question can be answered from these results alone?

- What is the empirical formula of each liquid?
- Are the two liquids the same compound?
- What is the molar mass of each liquid?
- How many carbon atoms are in one molecule of each liquid?

<div slot="why">

In 100 g of either liquid there are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{C:}\quad \frac{85.63}{12.01} &= 7.130\ \text{mol} \\
\text{H:}\quad \frac{14.37}{1.008} &= 14.26\ \text{mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by the smaller.

$$
\frac{14.26}{7.130} = 2.00
$$

That is 2.00 hydrogen atoms for every carbon atom, so both liquids have the empirical formula CH₂. About the formula, the results can say no more than that.

Many different compounds share the empirical formula CH₂. Cyclopentane, C₅H₁₀ (70.13 g/mol), and cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂ (84.16 g/mol), are both colorless liquids that are 85.63 percent carbon. So the same composition does not show that the two liquids are the same compound; it only fails to show that they are different.

The molar mass, and with it the number of atoms in a molecule, needs another measurement.

</div>
</Check>

So keep three things straight:

- Composition by mass gives the empirical formula.
- With a molar mass as well, it gives the molecular formula.
- Two samples with **different** compositions cannot be the same pure compound. Two samples with the **same** composition may or may not be.

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Rounding a ratio that should be multiplied.** 1.50 is not 1 or 2. Round only within a few hundredths of a whole number. Otherwise multiply every ratio by a small whole number, usually 2, 3 or 4.
- **Taking the masses as the formula.** 3.00 g of silicon with 2.00 g of nitrogen is not three atoms to two. A silicon atom is heavier than a nitrogen atom, so turn each mass into moles before you compare.
- **Forgetting a subscript in a mass percent.** Li₂CO₃ holds two lithium atoms: 18.78 percent, not 9.39.
- **Reading a mass percent as a percent of the atoms.** Tin is one atom in three in SnO₂ but 78.77 percent of the mass.
- **Stopping at the empirical formula when a molar mass is given.** Divide the molar mass by the empirical formula's mass to find the multiplier.
- **Assuming the molecular formula must be bigger.** The multiplier can be 1.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Some pure substances are made of molecules. Others are atoms or ions in a fixed ratio, which a formula unit describes.
- The law of definite proportions: in any pure sample of a compound, the elements are present in the same ratio by mass.
- The mass percent of an element is its mass in one mole of the compound, divided by the compound's molar mass, times 100.
- The empirical formula is the lowest whole-number ratio of atoms. To find it, start from masses (given percents, imagine a 100 g sample), convert to moles, divide by the smallest, and multiply if a ratio is not whole.
- The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Divide the molar mass by the empirical formula's mass to find the multiple, which can be 1.
- Composition alone gives the empirical formula. The molecular formula needs a molar mass as well.

</KeyPoints>
