The silicon that computer chips are built on is not quite pure. Like everything around you, it is made of atoms, tiny particles that come in about a hundred kinds, and each kind is an element. Silicon (Si) is one element. As the silicon crystal is grown, the maker adds a trace of another, phosphorus for example: often about one phosphorus atom for every ten million silicon atoms. No tweezers place atoms like that, and nobody counts them out. The added element is measured out by mass. Chemists count atoms the same way, by weighing, and for the same reason: there are too many to count, and each is too small to pick out.

## From mass to number of particles

A chemical formula is a statement about numbers of atoms. Take propane, C₃H₈. The symbols C and H stand for the elements carbon and hydrogen, and the small numbers after them, called subscripts, count the atoms. A symbol with no subscript counts as one atom. Propane is made of molecules, groups of atoms joined together, and each propane molecule is three carbon atoms and eight hydrogen atoms. So to make a substance, or to find out what one is made of, chemists need to know how many particles a sample holds.

But a balance, the laboratory's precise scale, reads mass, not number, and the numbers are beyond counting: the disk of silicon in a worked example below holds more than 10²⁴ atoms. A balance can still answer the question, if you know what one particle weighs. Then the mass of the sample tells you how many particles are in it. The rest of the lesson builds the two tools that make this work: a counting unit sized for atoms, and the mass of that many particles.

## The mole

A **mole** is a number of particles: $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ of them. That number is **Avogadro's number**, $N_A$, and the equation sheet you are given on the exam prints it. (The sheet writes its unit as mol⁻¹, which means "per mole".)

The symbol for an amount in moles is $n$, and the unit is written mol. Always say which particle you are counting: a mole of silicon atoms, a mole of propane molecules.

To go from moles to a number of particles, multiply by Avogadro's number:

$$
\text{number of particles} = n \times N_A
$$

The equation sheet does not print this one. You have to know it. For 2.00 mol of silicon atoms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&2.00\ \text{mol} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms/mol} \\
&= 1.20 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms}
\end{aligned}
$$

The answer has three digits, like the 2.00. A result is written with only as many digits, called significant figures, as the measurements behind it justify.

Going the other way, from particles to moles, you divide.

Why so large a number? Because atoms are so small. A mole of silicon atoms is a lump of 28.09 g, small enough to hold in one hand. The next section says where the 28.09 comes from.

## Atomic mass units and molar mass

A gram is far too big a unit for one atom. So the mass of an atom is given in a much smaller unit, the **atomic mass unit**, amu. A hydrogen atom has a mass of about 1 amu.

Look at the [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/), the chart that lists all the elements. The decimal number in an element's box is its **atomic mass**, the average mass of one atom of that element in amu: 28.09 amu for silicon. It is an average because the atoms of most elements do not all have the same mass. Topic 1.2 explains.

The mole was sized so that the same number does a second job. One silicon atom: 28.09 amu, on average. One mole of silicon atoms: 28.09 g. The mass of one mole of a substance is its **molar mass**, $M$, in grams per mole: 28.09 g/mol for silicon.

Here is why it works. One gram is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ amu. (So 1 amu is $1.661 \times 10^{-24}$ g, a number you never need to remember.) Take a mole of silicon atoms and add up their masses:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&28.09\ \text{amu} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \\
&= 1.692 \times 10^{25}\ \text{amu}
\end{aligned}
$$

That many amu is 28.09 g. Going from one atom to a mole multiplies the mass by Avogadro's number, which is exactly the step from amu to grams.

<KeyIdea>

The decimal number in an element's box on the periodic table does two jobs. It is the average mass of one atom in amu, and it is the mass of one mole of atoms in grams.

</KeyIdea>

## The molar mass of a compound

A compound is a substance made of two or more elements joined in a fixed ratio. Its formula says how many atoms of each element are in one particle of it. So its molar mass is the sum of the atomic masses, each multiplied by its subscript.

Methanol, CH₃OH, is the alcohol in many windshield washer fluids. Its formula has one C, four H (three in CH₃ and one in OH) and one O:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&12.01 + 4(1.008) + 16.00 \\
&= 32.04\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The four hydrogens add 4.032. The sum, 32.042, is kept to the two decimal places that 12.01 and 16.00 allow.

The two jobs of the number carry over from atoms to molecules. One methanol molecule has an average mass of 32.04 amu, and one mole of methanol molecules has a mass of 32.04 g.

**Parentheses.** A subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside. Aluminum hydroxide, Al(OH)₃, an antacid, has one Al, three O and three H:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&26.98 + 3(16.00) + 3(1.008) \\
&= 78.00\ \text{g/mol}
\end{aligned}
$$

The three terms are 26.98, 48.00 and 3.024. Their sum, 78.004, is kept to two decimal places.

**Elements that come as molecules.** Under ordinary conditions seven elements are made of two-atom molecules: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂. A mole of oxygen gas is a mole of O₂ molecules, so its molar mass is twice oxygen's atomic mass:

$$
2(16.00) = 32.00\ \text{g/mol}
$$

It is not 16.00 g/mol.

One word about the name of the particle. Methanol and oxygen are made of molecules. A compound such as Al(OH)₃ is not. Its formula gives the simplest group of atoms in the right ratio, called a **formula unit**, and a mole of it is a mole of formula units. Topic 1.3 says more.

## Between grams and moles

The equation sheet gives the relation between a mass and an amount in moles:

$$
n = \frac{m}{M}
$$

Here $n$ is the amount in moles, $m$ is the mass in grams and $M$ is the molar mass in g/mol. (On the sheet the molar mass is a bold M. A plain M there means something else, molarity, which belongs to topic 3.7.) Rearranged, $m = nM$.

The safest way to use it is to write the molar mass as a fraction with its units, a conversion factor, and let the units decide whether it goes on top or underneath.

**Grams to moles.** How many moles is 80.1 g of methanol? Put the grams underneath, so that they cancel:

$$
80.1\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{32.04\ \text{g}} = 2.50\ \text{mol}
$$

The grams cancel and moles are left.

**Moles to grams.** What is the mass of 0.150 mol of Al(OH)₃? This time put the moles underneath:

$$
0.150\ \text{mol} \times \frac{78.00\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 11.7\ \text{g}
$$

The moles cancel and grams are left.

This way of working, carrying the units and canceling them, is called **dimensional analysis**. If the units of your answer are wrong, the setup is wrong.

## Moles in the middle

Now join the two tools. Mass and moles are tied by the molar mass. Moles and particles are tied by Avogadro's number. Both of the numbers the exam hands you, the molar mass (from the periodic table) and Avogadro's number (from the equation sheet), connect to moles. So the route from grams to particles has two steps, with moles in the middle.

<KeyIdea>

Grams to particles takes two steps. Divide by the molar mass to get moles, then multiply by Avogadro's number. The other way round, divide by Avogadro's number, then multiply by the molar mass.

</KeyIdea>

<Worked title="Atoms in a silicon wafer">

Computer chips are built on thin disks of silicon called wafers. A wafer 300 mm across has a mass of about 128 g. How many silicon atoms is that? (The trace of another element in it, about one atom in ten million, does not change an answer given to three figures.)

1. **Plan the route.** Grams to moles to atoms: two steps, through moles.

2. **Grams to moles.** Silicon's molar mass is 28.09 g/mol.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &128\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{28.09\ \text{g}} \\
   &= 4.557\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   That is 4.56 mol to three significant figures. Carry the extra digit and round at the end.

3. **Moles to atoms.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &4.557\ \text{mol} \\
   &\quad \times \frac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms}}{1\ \text{mol}} \\
   &= 2.74 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

4. **As one line.** The two steps can be chained.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &128\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{28.09\ \text{g}} \\
   &\quad \times \frac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms}}{1\ \text{mol}} \\
   &= 2.74 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Grams cancel, then moles cancel, and atoms are left.

5. **What the shortcut costs.** Multiply the 128 g straight by Avogadro's number and you get $7.71 \times 10^{25}$, about 28 times too many. Its unit is g·atoms/mol, which is not a count of anything. The 28 is silicon's molar mass, the step that was skipped.

</Worked>

## Atoms inside a compound

One more step is often needed. A formula's subscripts count atoms per molecule or per formula unit: each propane molecule, C₃H₈, holds 3 carbon atoms and 8 hydrogen atoms. What is true of one molecule is true of a mole of them: 1 mol of C₃H₈ holds 3 mol of C atoms and 8 mol of H atoms.

So to count the atoms of one element, find the number of molecules (or formula units) first, then multiply by that element's subscript.

<Worked title="Hydrogen atoms in a propane cylinder">

A small propane cylinder, of the kind sold for portable grills, holds 465 g of propane, C₃H₈, when full. How many hydrogen atoms are in the propane?

1. **Plan the route.** Grams of propane to moles of propane to molecules of propane to atoms of hydrogen. The first two steps are the usual ones. The last uses the subscript.

2. **Molar mass.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &3(12.01) + 8(1.008) \\
   &= 44.09\ \text{g/mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The two terms are 36.03 and 8.064. Their sum, 44.094, is kept to two decimal places.

3. **Grams to moles.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &465\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{44.09\ \text{g}} \\
   &= 10.547\ \text{mol}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   That is 10.5 mol to three significant figures. Carry the extra digits.

4. **Moles to molecules.**

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &10.547\ \text{mol} \\
   &\quad \times \frac{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} \\
   &= 6.351 \times 10^{24}\ \text{molecules}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

5. **Molecules to hydrogen atoms.** Each molecule has 8, and 8 is an exact count, so it does not limit the significant figures.

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &6.351 \times 10^{24}\ \text{molecules} \\
   &\quad \times \frac{8\ \text{H atoms}}{1\ \text{molecule}} \\
   &= 5.08 \times 10^{25}\ \text{H atoms}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

6. **Look back.** The answer has to be larger than the number of molecules, because every molecule holds several hydrogen atoms. Stopping at step 4 would have given $6.35 \times 10^{24}$, eight times too few. The carbon atoms in the cylinder are counted the same way, three to a molecule:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &3 \times 6.351 \times 10^{24} \\
   &= 1.91 \times 10^{25}\ \text{C atoms}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

</Worked>

<Trap>

Six mistakes come up again and again on this topic:

- **Taking grams to particles in one step.** The molar mass and Avogadro's number both connect to moles, so it takes two steps. The 128 g of silicon times Avogadro's number came out 28 times too large.
- **Dividing where you should multiply.** Write the units and let them cancel. Moles to particles multiplies by Avogadro's number. Grams to moles divides by the molar mass.
- **Forgetting the subscript.** The 465 g of propane is $6.35 \times 10^{24}$ molecules but $5.08 \times 10^{25}$ hydrogen atoms.
- **Using an atom's molar mass for a molecule.** Oxygen gas is O₂, 32.00 g/mol. Iodine is I₂, 253.80 g/mol.
- **Ignoring parentheses.** Cu(OH)₂ has two O and two H, not one of each.
- **Not saying which particle.** "A mole of oxygen" could mean atoms or O₂ molecules. Say which.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- A mole is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles. That number is Avogadro's number.
- The decimal number in an element's box on the periodic table is the average mass of one atom in amu, and also the mass of one mole of atoms in grams, its molar mass.
- The molar mass of a compound is the sum of the atomic masses in its formula, each times its subscript.
- Moles and grams are tied by $n = m/M$. Moles and particles are tied by Avogadro's number.
- With a molar mass and Avogadro's number, getting from grams to particles takes two steps, through moles, with the units written and canceled.
- To count the atoms of one element in a compound, find the molecules or formula units, then multiply by the subscript.

</KeyPoints>
