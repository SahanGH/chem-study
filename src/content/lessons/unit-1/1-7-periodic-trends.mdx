A lithium-ion battery runs on one habit of lithium atoms: each gives up a single electron easily, and takes it back when the battery is charged. Lithium is costly, so battery makers have begun to sell cells built around sodium instead.

Why sodium, out of more than a hundred elements? Because it sits directly below lithium in the periodic table, and elements in one column behave alike. This lesson is about why they do, and about what changes as you move along a row or down a column.

## A table built on repeats

Line the elements up by atomic number and their properties do not drift steadily in one direction. They come round again. Lithium, element 3, is a soft metal that reacts with water. So is sodium, element 11. So is potassium, element 19.

The [periodic table](/tools/periodic-table/) stacks such repeats in columns, which is what "periodic" means.

A row of the table is called a **period**, and periods are numbered from the top: lithium is in period 2. A column is called a **group**, and groups are numbered 1 to 18 from the left: lithium, sodium and potassium are in group 1. The d block (topic 1.5) takes up groups 3 to 12. Periods 2 and 3 have no d-block elements, so there group 13 comes right after group 2.

The cause of the repeats is in the configurations of topic 1.5:

- Lithium is 1s² 2s¹.
- Sodium is [Ne] 3s¹.
- Potassium is [Ar] 4s¹.

Each has one s electron outside a core with a noble gas's configuration.

A period begins when electrons start a new shell. It ends at a noble gas, where that shell's s and p subshells are full. (The first shell has no p subshell, so period 1 ends when 1s alone is full.) Then the next element starts another shell, and the pattern of outer electrons starts over.

Same outer configuration, similar properties: that is the idea the whole table is built on.

This lesson follows four properties across the table: first ionization energy, radius (of atoms and of ions), electron affinity and electronegativity.

## Three questions about any electron

By Coulomb's law (topic 1.5), the attraction between a nucleus and an electron grows with the charge and falls off quickly with distance. So for a valence electron, ask three things:

1. How many protons are in the nucleus? That is the **nuclear charge**.
2. Which shell is the electron in? That sets its **distance** from the nucleus.
3. How many electrons lie between it and the nucleus? That is the **shielding**.

Core electrons shield well. Electrons in the same shell shield one another poorly. (One exception matters later in this lesson: a full d subshell, which the rough count below puts with the core, shields less well than the rest of the core.)

Shielding and nuclear charge combine into the **effective nuclear charge**, the charge an electron feels once shielding is taken off. A rough estimate is the number of protons minus the number of electrons in the shells inside: about +6 for sulfur's valence electrons (16 − 10), and about +1 for potassium's one valence electron (19 − 18).

Two moves lie behind the trends in this lesson:

- **Across a period,** each element has one more proton than the last. In periods 2 and 3 every added electron goes into the same shell, where it shields the others poorly. So the effective nuclear charge climbs, from about +1 for sodium to about +7 for chlorine, while the shell stays the same. The valence electrons are pulled harder.
- **Down a group,** the effective nuclear charge, by the same rough count, stays about the same, because the added protons are offset by added core electrons. But the valence electrons are in a shell farther from the nucleus. They are pulled less hard.

<KeyIdea>

Nearly every periodic trend comes from asking of the valence electrons what topic 1.5 asked of any electron: how much nuclear charge, how far away, how much shielding. Across a period the effective nuclear charge grows in the same shell. Down a group the shell gets farther out.

</KeyIdea>

## First ionization energy

The first ionization energy (topic 1.5) is the energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron from an atom in the gas phase, given in kJ for a mole of atoms. The harder the valence electrons are pulled, the more energy it takes. So, from the two moves above:

- **Across a period it generally rises.** In period 2 it goes from 520 kJ/mol for lithium to 2081 kJ/mol for neon, four times as much.
- **Down a group it generally falls.** Down group 18: helium 2372, neon 2081, argon 1521, krypton 1351, xenon 1170 kJ/mol. Even a noble gas gives up an electron more easily when that electron starts farther out.

Germanium and tin sit one below the other in group 14. Gallium and indium sit directly to their left, in group 13. Which of the four has the highest first ionization energy, and why?

Tin does have the most protons, but 46 of its 50 are offset by the electrons in the shells inside.

A nucleus's pull is not shared out among the electrons: what matters is the charge each valence electron feels and how far away it is.

And an electron that is far out and well shielded is easier to remove, not harder.

## Two breaks in the climb

Look at the figure again. In each of these two periods the line drops twice, and in the same two places:

- **Period 2:** beryllium 900 to boron 801, and nitrogen 1402 to oxygen 1314 kJ/mol.
- **Period 3:** magnesium 738 to aluminum 578, and phosphorus 1012 to sulfur 1000 kJ/mol.

(That last drop is small, but the steps on either side of it, silicon to phosphorus and sulfur to chlorine, are rises of more than 200 kJ/mol.)

In each of these steps the extra proton should have made the electron harder to remove. Two things about subshells explain why it did not.

**From group 2 to group 13, the electron removed is the first p electron.** In periods 2 and 3 this drop is large. Beryllium is 1s² 2s² and boron is 1s² 2s² 2p¹.

Within a shell, a p electron is held less tightly than an s electron (topic 1.5): an s electron spends more of its time close to the nucleus, and the s electrons shield a p electron from part of the nuclear charge. Boron's extra proton does not make up the difference. Its 2p electron is held less tightly than beryllium's 2s electrons, and takes less energy to remove. The same happens one period down, from magnesium, [Ne] 3s², to aluminum, [Ne] 3s² 3p¹.

**From group 15 to group 16, the electron removed is the first paired p electron.** This drop is large in period 2 (88 kJ/mol) and already slight in period 3 (12 kJ/mol). A p subshell has three orbitals (topic 1.5). Nitrogen, 1s² 2s² 2p³, has one electron in each. Oxygen, 1s² 2s² 2p⁴, has a fourth 2p electron, and it has to share an orbital with one that is already there.

Two electrons in one orbital are close together, and like charges push each other apart. So the fourth electron is held less tightly than it would be with an orbital to itself, and it comes out for less energy than the extra proton would lead you to expect. (These two drops, nitrogen to oxygen and phosphorus to sulfur, are the two slips of the outermost peak that topic 1.6 left for this lesson.)

Why does fluorine not drop again? From oxygen on, every electron removed in the row is a paired one. The penalty is there once and does not grow, and each added proton raises the value again: fluorine 1681, neon 2081 kJ/mol.

The breaks do not reach the whole table, and each one fades for its own reason.

The first break fades after the d block. In period 4 the step from calcium to gallium is a drop of 11 kJ/mol (590 to 579). In period 5 the step from strontium to indium is a rise (549 to 558). Ten d-block metals lie between the two elements of each pair. They add ten protons, and the ten d electrons that come with them shield those protons only in part.

The second break fades because the orbitals get larger down the table. In a larger p orbital the two paired electrons are farther apart, so they repel each other less. The drop is 88 kJ/mol in period 2, 12 in period 3 and 3 in period 4 (arsenic 944 to selenium 941).

Two popular answers explain nothing here: "beryllium has a full subshell, and full subshells are stable" and "nitrogen has a half-filled subshell, and half-filled subshells are stable". Both describe the pattern in other words. Neither names a force: nothing in them is an attraction or a repulsion, a charge or a distance.

<Worked title="Explaining a step that is missing">

Selenium comes right after arsenic in period 4. It has one more proton, and its outer electrons are in the same shell, so by nuclear charge alone its first ionization energy should be clearly higher. The step before, germanium to arsenic, is a rise of 182 kJ/mol, and the step after, selenium to bromine, is a rise of 199 kJ/mol.

Yet the measured values are 944 kJ/mol for arsenic and 941 kJ/mol for selenium. Explain why the step is missing, as you would on the exam.

1. **State the claim.** The electron removed from a selenium atom is held no more tightly than the electron removed from an arsenic atom, although selenium's nucleus has one more proton.

2. **Find the evidence in the configurations.** Arsenic is [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p³. Selenium is [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁴. Each atom loses a 4p electron. A p subshell has three orbitals, so arsenic's three 4p electrons have an orbital each. Selenium's fourth 4p electron has to share one.

3. **Give the reason.** The two electrons in that shared orbital are close together and push each other apart. The push works against the pull of the extra proton. So selenium's paired electron comes out for no more energy than an electron from arsenic.

4. **Check it against other evidence.** The same step, from group 15 to group 16, is a drop in the two periods above: nitrogen 1402 to oxygen 1314, and phosphorus 1012 to sulfur 1000 kJ/mol.

5. **Leave out what explains nothing.** "Arsenic has a half-filled 4p subshell, so it is stable" restates the data. A real answer names the paired electron and the push that makes it easier to remove.

</Worked>

In period 5, the first ionization energies of tin, antimony, tellurium and iodine are 709, 831, 869 and 1008 kJ/mol. Each step adds one proton. The step to tellurium is a rise of 38 kJ/mol, and the steps on either side of it are rises of 122 and 139 kJ/mol. Which statement fits these numbers?

- Tellurium's 5p electrons are shielded by the ten 4d electrons more than antimony's are, which cancels most of the extra proton's pull.

Antimony is [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p³, with one electron in each of its three 5p orbitals. Tellurium is [Kr] 5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p⁴: its fourth 5p electron has to pair up, and two electrons in one orbital repel each other. That makes the paired electron easier to remove than the extra proton alone would allow.

Here it rises, but by 38 kJ/mol where its neighbors rise by 122 and 139. So the repulsion takes back part of what the proton adds, and not all of it. It is weaker here than in period 2 because the two paired electrons are farther apart, in a larger orbital.

The 4d electrons cannot be the cause. Antimony and tellurium have the same full 4d subshell and the same 46 electrons in the shells inside the fifth, so the shielding is the same in both, and tellurium has one more proton (52 against 51).

Distance cannot be the cause either. All four atoms have their valence electrons in the fifth shell, and in that same shell the steps on either side are 122 and 139 kJ/mol. Distance cannot single out one step of the three.

## Atomic radius

An atom has no hard edge, so its size has to be defined by a measurement. The values in this lesson are covalent radii. A **covalent radius** is the share of the distance between two bonded nuclei that belongs to one atom, in picometers (1 pm = 10⁻¹² m).

The noble gases form few bonds or none, so a radius of this kind is not well defined for them, and this lesson leaves them out.

The two moves again:

- **Across a period the radius generally falls.** More effective nuclear charge acting on the same shell pulls that shell in. Across period 2: lithium 128, beryllium 96, boron 84, carbon 76, nitrogen 71, oxygen 66, fluorine 57 pm. Fluorine, with the most electrons of the seven, is the smallest.
- **Down a group the radius generally grows.** Each period adds a shell, and the valence electrons are farther out. Down group 2: beryllium 96, magnesium 141, calcium 176, strontium 195 pm.

The measured values are not perfectly smooth. From germanium to bromine the listed radii are 120, 119, 120 and 120 pm, the same within what the measurement can tell.

Radius and ionization energy run opposite ways for one reason: an atom whose valence electrons are pulled hard is small, and its electrons are hard to remove.

## When an atom becomes an ion

An ion is not the size of its atom.

- **A cation is smaller than its atom.** A rubidium atom, [Kr] 5s¹, has a radius of 220 pm. The ion Rb⁺ has lost that 5s electron, and with it the whole fifth shell: its outermost electrons are now in the fourth shell. Its radius is 152 pm.
- **An anion is larger than its atom.** A bromine atom has a radius of 120 pm, and the ion Br⁻ a radius of 196 pm. No shell was added: the extra electron went into the 4p subshell that bromine was already filling. What changed is the balance. The same 35 protons now hold 36 electrons where they held 35, so each outer electron is held a little less tightly against the push of the others, and the whole outer shell sits farther out.

A rubidium atom is about 1.8 times as wide as a bromine atom, but as ions the order is reversed.

One caution. An atom's radius is measured in bonds and an ion's in a crystal. (The values here are for an ion with six neighbors, as in topic 2.3.) The two kinds of radius are measured differently, so the figure shows the direction of each change better than its exact size.

Now compare ions with one another. Rb⁺ has 36 electrons, arranged like krypton's. So do the ions of the next three elements, Sr²⁺, Y³⁺ and Zr⁴⁺. (Yttrium and zirconium, the first two d-block metals of the row, have given up all the electrons outside the krypton core.) Ions with the same electron configuration are isoelectronic (topic 1.5).

| Ion | Protons | Electrons | Radius (pm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rb⁺ | 37 | 36 | 152 |
| Sr²⁺ | 38 | 36 | 118 |
| Y³⁺ | 39 | 36 | 90 |
| Zr⁴⁺ | 40 | 36 | 72 |

The electrons are the same in all four: the same number, in the same shells, shielding one another in the same way. Only the nucleus differs. More protons pull the same 36 electrons in more tightly, so the ion with the most protons is the smallest.

So the two ions have the same 10 electrons in the same shells (13 − 3 and 8 + 2), arranged like neon's, and their electrons shield in the same way. The difference is the nucleus: aluminum's 13 protons pull that set of electrons in harder than oxygen's 8 can. The sizes of the atoms no longer decide anything.

An aluminum atom does have more electrons than an oxygen atom, but a count of electrons names no force.

## Electron affinity

**Electron affinity** is the energy change when an atom in the gas phase gains one electron and becomes a 1− ion. For most elements energy is released.

Some tables write a release of energy as a negative number and others as a positive one, so read the heading. The table here gives the energy released.

| Period | Group 1 | Group 16 | Group 17 | Group 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | K 48 | Se 195 | Br 325 | Kr none |
| 5 | Rb 47 | Te 190 | I 295 | Xe none |

Energy released, in kJ/mol, when an atom gains one electron. Where the table says "none", the atom forms no stable 1− ion.

- **Across a period, more energy is generally released.** The added electron goes into the valence shell, where it feels nearly the same effective nuclear charge as the electrons already there, and that charge grows toward the right. A bromine atom releases about seven times as much energy as a potassium atom. The largest releases of all are found in group 17.
- **A noble gas forms no stable anion.** Its valence s and p subshells are full, so an added electron would have to start a new shell, far from the nucleus and shielded by every electron the atom has. It would feel almost no attraction. A few other elements form no stable anion either, nitrogen, beryllium and magnesium among them: the added electron would have to start a p subshell in beryllium and magnesium, and to pair up in an orbital in nitrogen.
- **Down a group the change is usually much smaller than the change across a period** (325 to 295 for bromine and iodine; 48 to 47 for potassium and rubidium), and it is not always in the same direction. Do not lean on it.

## Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the electrons it shares with the other atom. It is not an energy and has no unit. The usual scale, Pauling's, runs up to 4.0 for fluorine, the highest of any element.

The noble gases are left off the scale used here, because they form few bonds or none.

The two moves a third time:

- **Across a period it generally rises.** The shared electrons sit in the valence shell and feel the effective nuclear charge, which grows across the period. Across the main-group elements of period 4: potassium 0.8, calcium 1.0, gallium 1.8, germanium 2.0, arsenic 2.2, selenium 2.6, bromine 3.0.
- **Down a group it generally falls,** because the shared electrons are farther from the nucleus. Down group 16: oxygen 3.4, sulfur 2.6, selenium 2.6, tellurium 2.1. In this group the largest drop is from the second period to the third.

Keep the two "electron" properties apart. Electron affinity is a measured energy for a lone atom gaining an electron. Electronegativity is a scale for an atom sharing electrons in a bond.

Topic 2.1 uses the difference in electronegativity between two atoms to tell how evenly they share electrons.

<Check answer={1}>
Nitrogen's electronegativity is 3.0 and sulfur's is 2.6. By the rough count, sulfur's valence electrons feel the larger effective nuclear charge: about +6 (16 − 10), against about +5 for nitrogen's (7 − 2). Yet nitrogen attracts shared electrons more strongly. Which statement explains this?

- Nitrogen's valence electrons are in the second shell, closer to the nucleus, and the shorter distance outweighs sulfur's larger charge.
- Electronegativity falls down the table, so an element in period 3 attracts shared electrons less strongly than any element in period 2.
- A sulfur atom has 16 electrons to nitrogen's 7, and the extra electrons repel shared electrons more strongly than the extra protons attract them.
- Nitrogen is three electrons short of a full octet and sulfur only two, so nitrogen pulls harder on the electrons it needs.

<div slot="why">

Here the two levers pull opposite ways, so they have to be weighed.

Charge favors sulfur: about +6 against about +5.

Distance favors nitrogen. Its valence shell is the second shell and sulfur's is the third. The measured radii show how much closer that is: 71 pm for a nitrogen atom, 105 pm for a sulfur atom.

By Coulomb's law the force grows in step with the charge but falls with the square of the distance. Sulfur's charge is one-fifth larger. Its distance is about one and a half times as large, which by itself cuts the force to less than half. These rough numbers agree with what is found here: distance is the stronger lever, and nitrogen attracts shared electrons more strongly. A weighing this rough settles a clear case, not a close one.

The second statement stretches a trend past where it holds. "Falls down a group" compares the elements of one group, and nitrogen and sulfur are in different groups. Boron is in period 2 as well, and its 2.0 is below sulfur's 2.6.

The third counts a repulsion that the rough charge already includes. Sulfur's ten core electrons are set against ten of its protons, and what is left, about +6, is still more than nitrogen's +5.

The last names nothing that acts on an electron. And by its reasoning fluorine, one electron short of eight, would attract shared electrons less strongly than nitrogen, yet fluorine's 4.0 is the highest value of all.

</div>

## Estimating a value you were not given

The trends are regular enough to fill a gap in a table, and the exam asks for that.

Suppose a table gave no first ionization energy for selenium. Sulfur, directly above it, has 1000 kJ/mol. Tellurium, directly below, has 869 kJ/mol. First ionization energy generally falls down a group, so selenium should lie between them. Halfway is about 935 kJ/mol. The measured value, from the worked example, is 941 kJ/mol.

Row neighbors can be used the same way. Neither direction is safe everywhere, so use both when you can, and know where each goes wrong:

- **A row estimate can be far off at the two breaks, and on either side of the d block.** Halfway between germanium (762) and selenium (941) is about 850 kJ/mol, well short of arsenic's 944, because arsenic sits just before the step that goes missing.
- **A column estimate can be far off when one of the two neighbors is in period 2.** In much of the table the step from period 2 to period 3 is the largest in the column, so the halfway point lands too near the period 2 value. Chlorine's first ionization energy is 1251 kJ/mol. Halfway between fluorine (1681) and bromine (1140) is about 1410 kJ/mol, far too high. Halfway between its row neighbors, sulfur (1000) and argon (1521), is about 1260 kJ/mol.

- About 170 pm

Use both trends.

Radius generally grows down a group, so potassium should lie between sodium and rubidium, from 166 to 220 pm.

Of the four, only "about 200 pm" fits both. The measured value is 203 pm.

## The whole pattern

First ionization energy and electronegativity generally grow toward the upper right of the table, and atomic radius generally grows toward the lower left. They are three views of how hard the valence electrons are pulled.

The energy released when an atom gains an electron generally grows toward the right, as far as group 17.

<Trap>

Eight mistakes to watch for on this topic:

- **Explaining a trend by what an atom is supposed to want.** An atom wants nothing. An explanation has to name what acts on the electrons: which nuclear charge, on which electrons, at what distance.
- **"A half-filled subshell is stable."** That restates the pattern in the data and names nothing that acts on the electron. The drop from group 15 to group 16 in periods 2 and 3 comes from two electrons pushing each other apart in one orbital.
- **Expecting the two breaks everywhere.** The first is a large drop in periods 2 and 3. The second is large in period 2 and 12 kJ/mol in period 3. In period 4 both are small drops (11 and 3 kJ/mol), and in period 5 both steps are small rises.
- **"More protons must mean a tighter hold."** Not down a group: the added protons are offset by added core electrons, and the valence shell is farther out.
- **Giving an anion an extra shell.** Br⁻ keeps bromine's shells. It is larger because the same nucleus holds one more electron.
- **Sizing an ion by its atom.** An aluminum atom is larger than an oxygen atom, but Al³⁺ is smaller than O²⁻. For ions with the same number of electrons, count the protons.
- **Mixing up electron affinity and electronegativity.** One is an energy for a lone atom gaining an electron. The other is a scale for an atom sharing electrons in a bond.
- **Treating a trend as a law for every pair.** Some differences are too small to trust: selenium, 120 pm, is listed 1 pm larger than arsenic, 119 pm. Trust large differences, not small ones. And some pairs run against a trend for a reason. Gallium and germanium come just after the d block, where ten added protons are shielded only in part by the ten d electrons. So gallium's first ionization energy, 579 kJ/mol, is not below aluminum's, 578, and electronegativity rises going down from aluminum, 1.6, to gallium, 1.8, and from silicon, 1.9, to germanium, 2.0.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- The periodic table puts elements with the same kind of outer electron configuration in one column, which is why their properties recur. (Helium, 1s², sits with the other noble gases.)
- Nearly every trend is explained with Coulomb's law and three things about the valence electrons: the nuclear charge, the distance and the shielding. Across a period the effective nuclear charge grows in the same shell. Down a group the valence shell lies farther out.
- First ionization energy generally rises across a period and falls down a group. The rise breaks from group 2 to group 13, where the first p electron is partly shielded by the s electrons, and from group 15 to group 16, where the first paired p electron is repelled by its partner. The first break is a large drop in periods 2 and 3. The second is large in period 2 and 12 kJ/mol in period 3. In period 4 both are small drops (11 and 3 kJ/mol), and in period 5 both steps are small rises.
- Atomic radius generally falls across a period and generally grows down a group. A cation is smaller than its atom and an anion larger. Among ions with the same number of electrons, the one with the most protons is the smallest.
- Electron affinity is the energy change when an atom gains an electron. The largest releases are in group 17, and the noble gases form no stable anion. Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons. It generally rises across a period and falls down a group, and fluorine's is the highest.
- A missing value can be estimated from an element's neighbors in its column and in its row. Use both: a column estimate can be far off when one neighbor is in period 2, and a row estimate at the two breaks or beside the d block.

</KeyPoints>
