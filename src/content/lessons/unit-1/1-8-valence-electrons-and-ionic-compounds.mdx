Some glow-in-the-dark paints and stickers go on shining for hours after the light is switched off. In the kind that glows longest, the afterglow comes from a compound of strontium, strontium aluminate.

Take that compound, or any other strontium compound off a chemist's shelf, and the strontium in it is there as the ion Sr²⁺. Not Sr⁺, and not Sr³⁺. Something makes two the number for strontium, and the same thing makes it one for potassium and three for aluminum. You can see what it is by pulling electrons off an atom one at a time and measuring what each one costs.

## Removing electrons one after another

Topic 1.7 used the first ionization energy, the energy to remove one electron from an atom. You do not have to stop at one. The **second ionization energy** is the energy to remove the next electron, from the 1+ ion. The third removes one more, from the 2+ ion, and so on. The whole list is the atom's **successive ionization energies**.

Each one is larger than the one before. The same nucleus now holds fewer electrons, they repel one another less, and those that remain are held more tightly.

Here are the first four for strontium:

- first: 549 kJ/mol
- second: 1064 kJ/mol
- third: 4138 kJ/mol
- fourth: 5430 kJ/mol

Read the chart by the size of each increase. The second costs 515 kJ/mol more than the first, which makes it about twice the first. Then the pattern breaks. The third costs 3074 kJ/mol more than the second, which makes it nearly four times the second. After that the rise is smaller again, 1292 kJ/mol for the fourth.

Look at it another way. The first two electrons together cost 1613 kJ/mol, less than half of what the third costs alone.

## What the first big jump counts

Strontium is [Kr] 5s² (topic 1.5). Its first two ionization energies remove the two 5s electrons, the valence electrons. They are in the fifth shell, far from the nucleus, and the 36 core electrons shield them. By the rough count of topic 1.5, the protons minus the electrons in the shells inside, they feel an effective nuclear charge of about +2 (38 − 36).

The third electron has to come out of the core, from the 4p subshell. That is one shell closer to the nucleus, with only 28 electrons inside it, so it feels about +10 (38 − 28). A much larger charge at a shorter distance is, by Coulomb's law, a much stronger attraction. That is the jump.

The same reading works for other main-group elements: the first big jump in a list of successive ionization energies comes right after the last valence electron has gone.

Judge a jump by the size of the rise in kJ/mol, not by how many times larger one value is than the one before. A smaller step-up can appear inside the valence electrons, where removal moves from the p electrons to the s electrons. In group 13 that step-up follows the very first electron and is already sizable. (A group is a column of the periodic table, and the groups are numbered 1 to 18 from the left.)

A longer list shows further jumps each time a deeper shell or subshell is reached, some of them larger still. The first big jump is the one that counts the valence electrons.

The jump is plainest in periods 2 and 3, the second and third rows of the table, and for the s-block metals. For gallium, indium and the elements to their right, the first core electrons come out of a full d subshell, and the jump can be as little as two to three times the rises before it. There the group number is the safer guide to the number of valence electrons, as the next section shows.

<KeyIdea>

For a main-group element, successive ionization energies rise step by step until the valence electrons are gone. The next electron must come from the core, closer to the nucleus and far less shielded, and it costs much more. Count the electrons removed before that first big jump: that is the number of valence electrons.

</KeyIdea>

The data are nitrogen's, 1s² 2s² 2p³: five electrons in the second shell, which feel about +5 (7 − 2), and then a 1s electron right beside a nucleus of seven protons, with nothing inside it to shield it.

The third increase, 2897, is somewhat larger than its neighbors, but nothing like the rise at the end of the list, which is fifteen times as large. It is not the jump: it is where removal moves from the 2p electrons to the 2s electrons.

One warning. The jump counts the valence electrons, but it does not say that the atom will lose them. Pulling five electrons off a nitrogen atom costs more than 25,000 kJ/mol in all, and nitrogen forms no N⁵⁺ ion. The next section shows what a nonmetal does.

## From valence electrons to a charge

The jump also tells you the charge a main-group element usually has in an **ionic compound**, a compound made of cations and anions. There are two cases. (Apart from hydrogen, the elements at the left and in the middle of the periodic table are metals, and those at the upper right are nonmetals.)

- **Metals at the left of the table** (group 1 apart from hydrogen, group 2, and group 13 apart from boron) have one, two or three valence electrons, far from the nucleus and well shielded. Each of those can be removed at a moderate cost. One more would have to come out of the core, at a much higher cost. Nor does the atom usually stop short: strontium's second electron comes off at a moderate cost, and the stronger attraction of a 2+ ion for the anions around it more than pays for it (topic 2.2). So in an ionic compound the atom has usually lost exactly its valence electrons, and its charge is their number: 1+, 2+ or 3+.

- **Nonmetals at the right** (nitrogen and phosphorus in group 15, and the nonmetals of groups 16 and 17) have five, six or seven valence electrons, which feel a large effective nuclear charge. They are hard to remove: bromine's first ionization energy, 1140 kJ/mol, is about twice strontium's 549. And an extra electron that enters the same shell is attracted by that same large charge (the electron affinity of topic 1.7). A lone atom would not take a second or third electron (a nitrogen atom does not hold even a first). In a compound, the attraction between the ions pays for them (topic 2.3).

  The valence shell's s and p subshells hold eight electrons in all. In an ionic compound the atom has taken on electrons until they are full: three, two or one more, for a charge of 3−, 2− or 1−. There the count stops, because the next electron would have to start a new shell, far from the nucleus and shielded by all the others.

  The same holds for a metalloid such as tellurium, which this lesson uses as Te²⁻. (A metalloid is an element on the border between the metals and the nonmetals.)

| Group | Valence electrons | Usual charge | Ion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (not hydrogen) | 1 | 1+ | K⁺ |
| 2 | 2 | 2+ | Sr²⁺ |
| 13 (not boron) | 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| 15 (N and P) | 5 | 3− | P³⁻ |
| 16 | 6 | 2− | O²⁻ |
| 17 | 7 | 1− | Br⁻ |

For these groups the number of valence electrons is the last digit of the group number. The charge of a nonmetal of groups 15 to 17, or of a metalloid such as tellurium, is the group number minus 18: for group 16 that is 16 − 18, a charge of 2−.

Each ion in the table has ended up with the electron count of a noble gas: K⁺ and P³⁻ with argon's 18, Sr²⁺ and Br⁻ with krypton's 36, Al³⁺ and O²⁻ with neon's 10. That is the outcome, not the reason. The reason is the jump: a nucleus holds its core electrons far too strongly to lose them in a reaction, and it has almost no hold on an electron that would have to begin a new shell.

The rule is for these main-group columns. Most metals of the d block, and a few heavy metals such as thallium, tin and lead, have more than one common charge, which position alone does not give, and this lesson leaves them out.

<Worked title="From ionization energies to a formula">

The electron sources of some particle accelerators are coated with a compound of tellurium and element X, a very soft metal from the s block of the periodic table. Light striking the coating knocks electrons out of it. The first three ionization energies of X are 376, 2234 and 3203 kJ/mol. What charge does X have in the compound? Justify the answer, and give the compound's formula.

1. **Find the first big jump.** Look at the increases: the second is 1858 kJ/mol above the first, and the third is 969 kJ/mol above the second. An s-block metal has one valence electron or two (topic 1.5). With two, the first increase would be the small one and the second the big one, as for strontium (515, then 3074). Here it is the other way round: the big jump comes between the first and the second.

2. **Count the valence electrons.** One electron comes off before the jump, so X has one valence electron. X is in group 1.

3. **State the charge, with the evidence.** In its compounds X is X⁺. Removing one electron costs 376 kJ/mol. Removing a second would cost 2234 kJ/mol: 1858 kJ/mol more, and nearly six times as much.

4. **Give the Coulomb's-law reason.** The first electron is the valence electron: alone in the outer shell, shielded by every other electron in the atom, feeling an effective nuclear charge of about +1. The second would come from the core, a shell closer to the nucleus and far less shielded, so the nucleus attracts it much more strongly.

5. **Find the other ion.** Tellurium is in group 16, with six valence electrons. It takes on two more: Te²⁻, with 52 + 2 = 54 electrons.

6. **Balance the charges.** Each X⁺ carries 1+ and each Te²⁻ carries 2−. Two X⁺ ions for every Te²⁻ ion add up to zero. The formula is X₂Te.

7. **Name the element.** The data are cesium's, the group 1 metal of period 6, the sixth row. Cesium is [Xe] 6s¹: its 6s electron feels about +1 (55 − 54), and the 5p electron that would go next feels about +9 (55 − 46) from one shell closer. The compound is Cs₂Te, cesium telluride.

</Worked>

<div slot="why">

The rise is 2632 kJ/mol for potassium, against 857, 515 and 462 kJ/mol for beryllium, strontium and barium.

The multiples point to the same element. For potassium the second is about 7.3 times the first. For beryllium, strontium and barium it is about twice the first (2.0, 1.9 and 1.9 times).

The reason is where the second electron comes from. Beryllium, strontium and barium are in group 2: each has two valence electrons, so its second electron is still a valence electron.

Potassium, [Ar] 4s¹, is in group 1 and has only one. Its second electron has to come from the core, from the 3p subshell, a shell closer to the nucleus and much less shielded.

For potassium the first big jump comes after the first electron. For the other three it has not come yet.

Potassium has the smallest first ionization energy of the four and the largest second.

## Balancing the charges

An ionic compound has no charge overall, so the positive and negative charges in it add up to zero. Its formula gives the smallest whole-number ratio of ions that does that (the formula unit of topic 1.3), with the metal written first and no charges shown. There are three cases:

- **Equal charges, one of each.** Strontium and oxygen: Sr²⁺ and O²⁻. The formula is SrO.
- **Unequal charges, where one is a multiple of the other.** Lithium and nitrogen: Li⁺ and N³⁻. Three 1+ charges balance one 3− charge. The formula is Li₃N.
- **Unequal charges, where neither is a multiple of the other.** Strontium and phosphorus: Sr²⁺ and P³⁻. The smallest total that both 2 and 3 divide is 6. Reaching 6+ takes three Sr²⁺ ions, and reaching 6− takes two P³⁻ ions. The formula is Sr₃P₂.

Always finish by adding the charges up. If the total is not zero, the formula is wrong.

<Check answer={2}>
Using the usual charge of each element, what is the formula of a compound of indium and oxygen?

## One column, one kind of formula

The charge comes from the number of valence electrons, and the main-group elements of one column have the same number (helium, with two, is the one exception). So the elements of one column form compounds with the same pattern of formula.

Take group 16. Oxygen, sulfur, selenium and tellurium each have six valence electrons and each form a 2− ion. With lithium they give Li₂O, Li₂S, Li₂Se and Li₂Te. With strontium they give SrO, SrS, SrSe and SrTe.

It works for a column of metals too: magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium form MgO, CaO, SrO and BaO. Chemists call such compounds analogous.

Elements in a column tend to do this. It is a strong pattern, not a law without exceptions.

The compound of strontium and phosphorus is Sr₃P₂. Which pair of elements would you expect to form a compound whose formula has the same pattern, three of the first to two of the second?

- Barium and phosphorus
- Sodium and phosphorus

- Aluminum and fluorine

<div slot="why">

A formula's pattern comes from the two charges, and the charges come from the two groups.

Barium is in strontium's column, group 2, so it forms a 2+ ion as strontium does. Phosphorus, in group 15, is 3− as before. The charges are the ones in Sr₃P₂, and so is the pattern: Ba₃P₂.

Matching one of the two is not enough. Sodium and phosphorus keep the phosphorus, but sodium is in group 1 and is 1+, which gives Na₃P. Magnesium is in strontium's column and is 2+, but sulfur is in group 16 and is 2−, which gives MgS.

Aluminum is 3+ and fluorine 1−, which gives AlF₃.

## Which elements form ionic compounds

Whether two elements bond, and how, comes down to how strongly each nucleus attracts valence electrons: its own, and the other atom's.

A strontium atom holds its two valence electrons weakly: its first ionization energy is 549 kJ/mol and its electronegativity 1.0. An oxygen atom holds its own valence electrons tightly, with a first ionization energy of 1314 kJ/mol, more than twice strontium's, and attracts other electrons strongly, with an electronegativity of 3.4.

Bring the two elements together and strontium's valence electrons end up on oxygen. The Sr²⁺ and O²⁻ ions that result attract each other, and that attraction holds the solid together (topic 2.3).

So a metal from the left of the table and a nonmetal from the right generally form an ionic compound. Two nonmetals both hold their electrons tightly, so generally neither loses any: they share (topic 2.1, which uses electronegativity to sort out the kinds of bond).

<Trap>

Six mistakes to watch for:

- **Counting to the jump one electron too far.** A jump between the second and third ionization energies means two valence electrons, not three. Count the electrons removed before the jump.
- **Looking for the largest number in place of the first big jump.** Every ionization energy is larger than the one before. Look at how much each rises over the one before it, and find the first rise that is several times the size of the rises before it. A rise that is only somewhat larger than its neighbors is not the jump: inside the valence electrons it is usually the move from p electrons to s electrons. For an s-block metal the jump can be the very first rise, with none before it to compare. Then see which of the first two rises is the big one. The jump is plainest in periods 2 and 3 and for the s-block metals. For gallium, indium and the elements to their right it is much less sharp, so go by the group number there.
- **Explaining a charge with "it wants a noble-gas configuration".** That is the outcome. The reason is that core electrons are held far too strongly to lose, and an electron that would start a new shell is hardly attracted at all.
- **Putting the right numbers on the wrong ions.** In₃O₂ in place of In₂O₃: add up the charges to check.
- **Leaving a formula unreduced.** Sr₂O₂ balances, but the formula is the smallest ratio, SrO.
- **Using the column rule on a transition metal.** The transition metals are in the d block, and the rule gives the charge for main-group columns only.

</Trap>

<KeyPoints>

- Each successive ionization energy is larger than the one before. For a main-group element, the first big jump comes when the valence electrons are gone and the next electron must come from the core.
- The number of electrons removed before the first big jump in an element's successive ionization energies is its number of valence electrons. For groups 1, 2 and 13 to 17 that is the last digit of the group number.
- In ionic compounds the metals of groups 1, 2 and 13 have usually lost their valence electrons, giving charges of 1+, 2+ and 3+. The nonmetals of groups 15, 16 and 17 have gained enough to fill the valence shell's s and p subshells, giving 3−, 2− and 1−.
- The formula of an ionic compound is the smallest whole-number ratio of ions whose charges add up to zero.
- The main-group elements of one column have the same number of valence electrons, so they tend to form compounds with the same pattern of formula.
- A metal that holds its valence electrons weakly and a nonmetal that attracts electrons strongly generally form an ionic compound.

</KeyPoints>
